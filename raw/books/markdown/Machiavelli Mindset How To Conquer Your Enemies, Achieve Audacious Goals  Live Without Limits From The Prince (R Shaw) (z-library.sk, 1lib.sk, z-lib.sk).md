## **Chapter 1 - Seeing the World through Machiavelli’s Eyes** 

## **Who is Machiavelli?** 

Niccolò Machiavelli was a politician, philosopher, and a diplomat from Renaissance Italy. He was also an author and a humanist who penned songs, poems and, of course, his greatest work, the Prince. Many regard him as the father of modern political science. 

Niccolò Machiavelli was born at the same time that Lorenzo “Il Magnifico” de Medici rose into power (1469). While the people of Florence liked to believe that no one man ruled them, the powerful Medici family have been pulling the strings behind the curtains for years and years. Lorenzo de Medici merely had to carry on the work that his ancestors had set into motion. 

The kind of Italy that Machiavelli grew up in was filled with culture and sophistication, a world where art, science and literature flourished. Italy was then the center of intellectual accomplishment and this drew in scholars and artists from various parts of Europe. At that time, everyone wanted to be in, live in and bask in Italy. 

This era was also a time of great religious change. The Catholic Church was growing more and more corrupt and depraved and the behavior of the notorious Pope Alexander VI did not help at all. The people were growing more and more hostile towards Catholic authority. During that period, the Protestant movement in the Holy Roman Empire was growing stronger. There was also a great deal of change developing in politics. 

The feudal territories were gradually being brought together under one central leadership. This instigated the formation of the present-day European nations. At this period, the contemporary concept of the state was already at its embryonic stage. During that time when nations were being combined, war was considered as the most treasured tool of a ruler. 

In 1492, Il Magnifico died and the power was handed over to his inept successor, Piero. The end of Piero de Medici marked the beginning of the Republic of Florence. The trouble all began when Ludovico Sfroza, Duke of Milan, allowed French forces into Italy. In exchange for the Venetian territory, he offered to support the claims of the French over Naples (which was a separate kingdom then). Power was ceased from the Medici family in 1494 when the French, led by King Charles the Eighth stormed in with their troops. 

Florence was then led by a republican government. It didn’t take a year, though, before good old Charlie was driven out by the Italian coalition. The great irony was that Sfroza himself joined in the league. Even so, it did not escape Machiavelli’s keen observation that the French king was able to conquer Italy with almost no effort at all. For several years, Machiavelli served as a senior official in the Florentine Republic where he handled foreign relations and military undertakings. 

Many years later, King Louis of France worked with Pope Alexander VI and Cesare Borgia, to conquer the Romagna region. You need to understand that popes at that time were not kindly old men with bald heads who appeared in crowds and kissed babies. Sure, they were extremely religious. That said, they had money. They had power, and more often than not, they abused that power. They could mobilize armies and hoist their illegitimate sons into the seat of command, pretty much like what Pope Alexander did with his son, Cesare Borgia. 

According to Machiavelli, you could learn a thing or two from Pope Alexander. He said that the latter’s ruthlessness and dishonesty is something that could place a leader at a great advantage. If you’ve read the Prince, then you’ll know that it’s basically a book about WWCD (What Would Cesare Do). Machiavelli saw Cesare as someone who had true potential for greatness. 

According to him, when you consider Cesare’s personal attributes and his position in life, it makes you think that he was sent by God Himself to redeem the country. After all, power was handed to him in a silver platter. Machiavelli had such high hopes for this guy. 

It was in 1499 when the French king took Milan from Sfroza. However, Louis’ clutches on Naples was not strong enough. He appointed his cousin, Frederick of Aragon as a dummy ruler at first. Meanwhile, he had a secret agreement on the side with the Spanish King, Ferdinand. The two agreed to divide the kingdom of Naples amongst themselves. Ironically, Ferdinand went back on this arrangement and instead, pushed the French out of Naples. 

## **Okay, so what exactly did Cesare Borgia do to merit this kind of admiration from Machiavelli?** 

- For one thing, he slaughtered his rival families, the Orsinis and the Colonnas. That went well with Machiavelli’s primary teaching: 

## _**Get rid of the people who have the power to compete with you.**_ 

- Another thing that Cesare did was to convert the previous supporters of these families so they end up supporting him. This went well with another one of Machiavelli’s ideas: 

## _**Take advantage of the powerless.**_ 

(In the succeeding chapters, we’ll go into further detail about Machiavelli’s unconventional ideas on leadership.) 

- Cesare also eliminated the mercenaries. As an alternative, he gathered his own armed forces. Again, this agrees with another one of Machiavelli’s teachings: 

## . _**Avoid using supplementary forces and private armies**_ 

   - Cesare safeguarded his new state, Romagna, by obtaining it through cruel means. Afterwards, he bestowed independence upon it.  He may have been merciless beyond words, but one thing that he did correctly was to pin it all on a fall guy, then he murdered the fall guy. This is extremely advantageous for him because after he did that, he did not only had the people’s deepest gratitude; he also had their fear. Once again, this fits in perfectly with two of Machiavelli’s strongest ideas: 

- _**1) Utilize violent means to acquire a position of power. After that, seem kind.**_ 

- _**2) Do not place yourself in a position that will inspire hate. Instead, place yourself in a position that will evoke fear.**_ 

All of this, Cesare was able to do over a span of five years only! The young man also formulated a plan, which he intended to follow after his father’s death. He plotted to slaughter the local rulers. He intended to gain the support of the Roman nobility. Moreover, he planned on achieving control over the individuals who had the power to select the new pope. 

To put things plainly, the real reason behind Machiavelli’s guy crush on the young Borgia was because he was a decisive fellow who knew how to be merciless when needed. More than that, he was self-sufficient. Sure, he was born with power practically thrust upon him but unlike others, he did not rely on his father’s help. He was not afraid to get his hands dirty (or bloody). 

However, when Cesare Borgia’s father died, he undoubtedly lost a huge chunk of his influence. Moreover, he was also suffering from poor health. All this contributed to his downfall – much to Machiavelli’s disappointment, of course. The lesson here is that it’s never enough to be born into a position of power. You need to find a way to keep that power and to make that power your own. 

_Example: If you became the boss of a company because your father was the previous boss, you have to recognize that your father’s successes were his own. You need to make a name for yourself as well. Make sure that your subordinates respect you not because of what your predecessor had done but because of what you’ve done. Furthermore, just because you were “born into power”, that doesn’t mean that it’s yours forever. Some hotshot somewhere is going to challenge your authority and criticize how you run things. You have to be prepared for that._ 

There was a void of power after the decline of the Borgia family. Many wished to fill it. In 1503, Julius the Second (born Giuliano della Rovere) achieved papacy. In 1508, he established the league of Cambrai consisting of Spain, the Holy Roman Empire, and France to control the rebelling Venetians. After the defeat of the Venetian militia, Julius came to be threatened by France’s power over Italy, so he then set his mind to getting rid of them. 

Machiavelli observed that the King of France has had the warrior pope at his mercy for more than once yet he never used this fact to his advantage. According to Machiavelli, this was a poor move on the part of the French king, and he was right. In 1512, France was driven out and completely lost whatever power it had over Italy. The Medici family, with the help of Pope Julius, brought in an army to conquer the city. The great lesson here that Machiavelli was trying to impart is this: _Never allow people to be in a position where they could screw you over because eventually, they will._ History has shown time and time again that no one could ever resist that kind of temptation. 

_Example: When you’re delegating tasks to your subordinates, don’t do it so that one of your underlings will end up possessing so much power in his hands. As a rule, whatever authority you bestow upon others should never be enough for them to undermine your power._ 

With the Medici family back on top of the ladder, Machiavelli was not only out of a job but he was also captured and tortured. According to historians, the real reason why the Medicis had him captured was that they wanted to discredit Machiavelli. That’s because his previous writings ( _Istorie fiorentine_ ) threatened to show the public the true nature of the foundation of the Medicis’ power. When Machiavelli was released, he quit politics and instead, begun writing his popular book, the Prince, in 1513. 

## **Alright, so how was all that history relevant?** 

It was this succession of events that inspired the Prince. Machiavelli wrote his philosophies based on what he had observed firsthand. Not only was he a man ahead of his time, but he was at a position that made him privy to the entire history of the Florentine families. As such, he knew and understood the very source of their power base. During his time, Italy was being passed into the hands of one leader to another. 

At a short span of time, a lot of great men rose to power only to be kicked out of the throne before the seat was even warm. Because Machiavelli was not on the seat of power himself, he was able to see things that the conquerors themselves were not able to.  He studied where leaders triumphed and where they failed. Through this, he came up with the ideal characteristics of a great conqueror - one who will not only rise to the top, but _stay_ on top. He believed that unification of the Italian peninsula was impossible unless a leader had all these ideal qualities including cunning, duplicity, and ruthlessness. 

Initially, Machiavelli was rooting for the Medici family. He actually believed that they could unite Italy and rule it with an ir0n fist. Why not? Their family was used to having power in their hands, the children were all groomed for ruling, and they possessed the necessary wealth and resources. However, to Machiavelli’s dismay, when he begged the Medici heir Lorenzo di Piero to unify Italy, he didn’t listen. All he ever wanted was for Italy to be united and for a rightful leader to sit on the throne. 

That said, he believed that for a potential Prince to get to the throne and stay there, he should know how to play the game and _not_ be played by the game. He should be merciless, deceptive and not be bound by ethics. Even so, he should possess the _illusion_ of being a magnanimous leader and should strive to aim for both the love _and_ the fear of his constituents. In other words, it’s all about achieving the perfect balance. (If you’re familiar with George R.R. Martin’s fictional world in the Game of Thrones, you’ll see Machiavelli as a cross between Lord Varys and Lord Baelish.) 

In the end, Machiavelli wrote his book, Il Principe, believing that a new Prince should and could rule the Italian peninsula in place of the Medicis. More importantly, he wrote about _how_ a new Prince could _rise_ into power and _maintain_ and _safeguard_ that power. 

It wasn’t until 1532, five years after his death, that the manuscript was finally published. In the same year as his death, Rome fell into the hands of mercenary soldiers, just as Niccolò Machiavelli had predicted. 

## **Chapter 2 - Getting Over the Guilt** 

## **How does the world today see Machiavellianism?** 

Today’s perception of the word Machiavellianism is tainted. The term Machiavellian has been used to describe unscrupulous politicians who display immoral conduct (including treachery and the sacrifice of the innocents) as though they are not just normal but also _necessary_ in politics. The Prince obtained its unsavory reputation when readers claimed that it spreads evil suggestions to wannabe tyrants. 

In Psychology, Machiavellianism is a personality type, which describes a kind of person who is naturally manipulative, calculating and deceitful. We’ve all experienced lying to get out of a sticky situation. You may have called in sick at work so you could watch a ball game. As a kid, you may have lied to your parents to avoid punishment. The point is that we all have it in us to trick other people to our advantage. At times, you may feel a sense of guilt over a lie. This guilt, however, rarely occurs in Machiavellians. In other words, Machs are people who think it is normal to lie, trick, and even step on other people to reach their goals. 

Machiavellians are adept at playing mind games. In fact, Machs excel in careers and social conditions where there are indefinite rules and boundaries. It is Machiavellians who were able to see opportunity in chaos. They thrive in competitive environments. They possess minimal emotional detachment to other people and things so they are able to manage their impulses. This way, they are more patient than most and when an opportunity arises, they are able to see it and seize it. 

On the outside, Machs may be friendly and charming. To get what they want, they can covertly prey on other people’s guilt or sympathy or any other human emotion that they could sink their claws into. When these subtle tactics fail to help them get what they desire, they use coercion. Moreover, they can practically get away with anything by formulating a credible basis for denial for when they’re caught. That said, when the shit hits the fan, they’re not afraid to carry out threats. 

You see this sort of thing done by successful and powerful people every time. Even if it turns your stomach, you can’t deny that this kind of mindset is effective in politics and in the corporate world. 

## **So does this mean that Machiavellianism promotes amoral behavior?** 

Not really. First, to be truly Machiavellian, you need to stop seeing things in black and white. There’s more to life than simple honesty vs. dishonesty, morality vs. immorality. As previously mentioned, Machiavellianism is all about getting that perfect balance. It’s not about being dishonest all the time. It’s not about turning into a psycho who can’t tell the difference between honesty and dishonesty. You actually need to distinguish between the two so you can use _both_ honesty and dishonesty in a way that will become most profitable for you. 

According to the Machiavellian’s viewpoint, turning others into stepping stones to reach the top is not really an immoral act _. It’s simply the way things are._ If other people allow themselves to be stepped on, then chances are, they deserve it. 

Imagine a staircase. At the top of the staircase is your goal. There you are climbing it steadily. Then, on one of the steps, you see a person lying asleep. Do you stop? Do you sacrifice your goal because someone decided to snooze on one of the steps?  If you don’t step on him, it’s either you go down or you stagnate in the same place that you’re standing in. Either way, that’s the end for you. You will no longer be able to advance and you’ve got nothing but yourself to blame. 

On the other hand, if you step on that person, sure he may yell at you and call you an inconsiderate SOB. But was that person not being inconsiderate as well by simply lying there? That person deserved to be stepped on. In the end, it’s all a matter of people getting what they deserve. 

Okay, so what if in your heart of hearts, you believe that a person does not deserve to be stepped on yet you know that stepping on him will help you advance? 

_**According to Machiavelli, if you don’t grasp an opportunity when you see one, then you’re an even greater fool.**_ 

## **So, is it possible to obtain a Machiavellian Mindset even when you’re originally a morally upright individual?** 

Yes. While some were born with the Machiavellian mindset, others are molded into Machiavellianism by the adversities of life. If you were not born with Machiavellian tendencies, that’s perfectly fine. This means that you can become a _self-aware Machiavellian._ This way, you can be consciously tactical. Any self-serving form of manipulation that you perform need not seem crass or obvious. 

When socially acquired, Machiavellianism is not a form of negative behavior or a personality disorder. It’s human shrewdness at work, plain and simple. This isn’t you being evil. This is you utilizing your higher faculties in the way that they are meant to be used. This is you seizing opportunities in the way that they’re meant to be seized.  This is you living your life in a way that it’s meant to be lived. 

## _**This is not you being dishonest or unfair. This is you being honest with yourself. This is you being fair to yourself.**_ 

## **Chapter 3 - Moving Past Conventional Thinking** 

## **How do you eliminate conventional thinking and replace it with Machiavellian thinking?** 

Let’s not deny that it’s hard to rewire the pattern of thinking that has been rooted into our brains ever since we were little. Once you set aside all thoughts of others and concentrate chiefly on yourself, everything will become a whole lot simpler. 

## _**The Prince has no need to justify his actions.**_ 

According to Machiavelli, justification is only for the weak. If you need to explain yourself to others, then no one will ever truly respect you. When you justify yourself, you become transparent. You give away your motive. You show others where you stand. This ultimately makes you vulnerable. 

_Example: When a company make budget cuts in a certain department, the “explanations” are usually always general and even nebulous. They don’t let the employees in on the actual details. Why? It’s because once you even make an effort to go over the nitty-gritty stuff, the employees inevitably feel that you are, in fact, robbing them of what’s rightfully theirs. On the other hand, if you take stuff away with no explanation whatsoever, you are emphasizing the fact that that thing is yours to take and thus, you are taking it._ 

Simply put, when it is within your power and your right to do something, you don’t need to tell others why you’ve felt the need to do it. Just get on with it. People will yammer as they always do, but in the end, no one will have the guts to complain about it because after all, you didn’t really take away anything from them. 

You’ll be surprised at how many “powerful” men budge once they’ve been challenged or interrogated. A few questions and they reveal their thought process to the enemy. That’s because the society has wrongfully molded us 

to instinctively adhere to logical authoritarianism. As a Machiavellian, you need to forget all that. No one has authority over you except you. Furthermore, you have to remember that words are weapons. Anything that you say can be used by your enemy against you. The more you speak to defend yourself, the more you’ll have reason to defend yourself. 

Another reason why you should learn to filter what comes out of your mouth is that people instinctively distrust that which does not seem effortless. For instance, the more a person proclaims his innocence, the guiltier he appears. Truth is organic and self-evident. To others, if you’re innocent (even when you’re truly not), then you don’t need to prove yourself. 

Here’s another thing to think about: Mystery is essential in being a great leader and nothing ruins mystery more than honesty. When you assist people in understanding who you are, they don’t respect you. So if you want to achieve popularity, you don’t go about it by telling people all about yourself. You make them _want_ to know you and then you make them work for it. Remember that people only place value in the fruits of their efforts, not in what you feed them. It’s for this same reason why people value sexual partners better when they’re harder to get. 

Real world application: If you want true power, don’t reach out to others in an attempt to be likeable. People may think that you’re nice, sure, but subconsciously, they’ll also see you as a pathetic individual who needs their approval. It should be the other way around. People should feel like they need to gain your approval. 

Besides, if you’re the boss, the last thing you want is for your subordinates to see you as one of them. You are the Prince. You are irreplaceable. You are inimitable, thus, you are peerless. People should always believe that you’re above them so that they’ll continuously work to reach up to your level instead of you having to stoop down to their level. 

## _**Gone are the days when words were women’s weapons.**_ 

In Il Principe, all Machiavelli talks about are male leaders. That’s because during his time, female political leaders are a rare thing. That said, women who are the original Machiavellians. Evolution has shaped them that way. 

The early men had muscles to protect themselves while the women only had the men to protect them. And thus, they have mastered the art of manipulating men and exploiting masculine strength for their own gain. 

A foolish man allows his actions to be limited by morality. An intellectual man’s actions will be limited by rationalism. A woman’s actions will be restrained by neither. A rational man may attempt to improve his social standing by displaying his intelligence but unfortunately, the masses will find this repulsive. A typical woman, on the other hand, knows that the social game is won by magniloquence, sharp wit, and sex appeal. 

The typical woman is skilled in twisting the truth to get what she wants. A woman can be as contrary as she likes. In that, she is truly admirable. It is usually natural for a woman to lie barefacedly with utter conviction. That’s because she has the intrinsic ability to make herself believe that she has done the right thing. When a man does this, it makes him seem like a psychopath. 

Most men lack this ability to delude themselves, therefore, to fully embrace the Machiavellian mindset, men need to learn how to challenge rational tendencies beforehand. If you’re a man, you need to learn how to unplug that mechanical part of your brain, your “rational brain”, and conveniently switch to your “social brain” when necessary. Today, this art is referred to as utilitarian ambiversion. 

The true Machiavellian should learn when to change his style of communication so as to match the disposition of his company. Be a chameleon. Change your skin depending on which audience you are facing at the moment. If one group of people thinks that you’re generous but another group thinks you’re ungenerous, who cares. The important thing here is that you need that one group to believe that you’re kind and you need that other group to think that you’re unkind. 

As a general rule, as long as you don’t attack popular opinion, you’ll be fine. 

_Example: When attending social events, stay away from hot topics such as politics, abortion, or religion._ 

What really goes on inside your head, on the other hand, shall remain sacred and no one can touch that. 

_**Who you are within is your own concern but when it comes to other people, it’s what you appear to be that counts.**_ 

## **Chapter 4 - Machiavelli’s Thoughts on Generosity** 

## **How Handing Out Princely Gifts Can Work Against You** 

According to Machiavelli’s teachings, it is good to be seen by your peers and subordinates as someone who’s generous. That said, if you become overly generous to the point that you earn a reputation for it, then your kindness will eventually come ‘round and guess what you get for it? You _._ get screwed from behind Lend someone a hand and that person is likely to rip your whole arm off. People are just naturally abusive that way. 

Furthermore, if you want to keep up the image of being a generous person, then that means mean having to continuously show displays of extravagance, thus, your resources will eventually be used up. When do you think these people will be when all your resources are gone? Some will disappear at the first sign of trouble while others will happily kick you while you’re down. 

## _**Remember: To be poor is to be despised.**_ 

It may sound harsh, but if you have nothing to offer people, you’re nothing to them. Various religions teach us that true generosity, when employed virtuously, should be unrecognizable. In other words, if you’re going to donate a million bucks to charity, then it should be sent in as an anonymous donation for it to be considered as true generosity. 

Machiavelli says screw all that! You need to be generous especially (and sometimes _only_ ) when people are looking because otherwise, it places you at a disadvantage. After all, you’re using up your resources and still, you will not be able to avoid the infamy of being labeled as a parsimonious person. Bottom line: If you can’t use your reputation of generosity to your advantage, then it would be better to be a selfish bastard and not to give at all. 

Another thing that to keep in mind is that once you’ve gained a rep for being “the philanthropist”, you’ll always feel obliged to be open-handed. In his book, Machiavelli provides the example of the generous prince who 

ended up having to burden his people with heavy taxes so he’ll be able to procure the financial means to keep up his image of generosity. In the end, his subjects hated him. Yes, he was able to reward a few lords in his court but to do this, he had to injure the masses (which are greater in number) and thus, risk a revolt and place himself in danger. 

What Machiavelli is saying is that it’s totally fine to carry the status of a giver as long as your reputation for being charitable will not harm you. _How is “harm” defined exactly?_ 

## _**The mere idea that you’re giving away your own resources for free is already a way of hurting yourself.**_ 

The thing about generosity is that it feeds upon itself. The more you practice it, the more you lose the ability to practice it. 

Note that in here, resources don’t have to be in the form of money. It can be time that you’re giving away for free. It could be free knowledge or labor. Either way, when you give away something for nothing, you lose. Plain and simple. 

Here’re a truly Machiavellian idea: Establish the _appearance_ of being generous not by giving away your own resources, but by giving away other people’s resources. Just take a look at game show hosts and government officials. Those prizes and infrastructures did not come from their own pockets. Those came from other people’s wallets and people’s taxes. But do the viewers and the voters care? Nope. As far as they’re concerned, he who hands them the present is the soul of generosity himself. 

According to Machiavelli, when you find yourself in a dilemma between a generous image vs. a miserly image, it would be better to let people think that you’re the latter. A prince who is not known to be generous, but is selfsufficient enough to fight his own wars without milking the masses will, in the end, be seen as generous. 

Let’s apply this to the modern world setting. People won’t mind if you don’t give them anything as long as you don’t ask them for anything. On the other hand, if you’ve been generous in the past and suddenly lose your resources, you’ll quickly lose other people’s love when you run out of things to give. 

Furthermore, if you make the mistake of thinking that you can collect a little payback from the things that you’ve given others in the past, you’ll only end up being disappointed and loathed by others. It doesn’t matter whether you’re begging for money or information or help. People hate beggars. That’s just the way of the world. And as Machiavelli said: _You should never place yourself at a position where you will attract the hatred of people._ 

## **Chapter 5 - Compassion vs. Cruelty** 

## **Is it better to be loved or to be feared by others?** 

Machiavelli teaches that a Prince should strive to be loved by others. For this, others should see you as a merciful leader. That said, there is such thing as dangerous compassion. Cesare Borgia was deemed by others as a ruthless leader. Nevertheless, it was his ruthlessness which brought order, peace, and unity to Romagna. In the end, he was seen as someone who was more compassionate than the Florentines who, in their excessive misuse of mercy, allowed murders and plundering to occur and to endanger the entire community. 

In comparison, Borgia may have executed several persons but the fact is that in the end, the only ones injured is that relatively small percentage of individuals. The majority remain safe and happy.

## **Chapter 6 - What Machiavelli Says About Honesty**

## **Chapter 7 - How to Avoid Attracting Hatred and Other Lessons from the Prince**
