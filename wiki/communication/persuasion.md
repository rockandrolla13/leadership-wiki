# Persuading the Unpersuadable

## Use Case

You need to change the mind of a counterpart who is *not* open to a straightforward, well-evidenced case. Reach for this playbook when you have already argued the merits and the counterpart has not moved — and especially when they are senior to you, hold formal authority over the decision, or display one of the four classic resistance profiles: **overconfidence**, **stubbornness**, **narcissism**, or **disagreeableness**.

The defining situation: you are *right* (or believe you are), the cost of being unheard is real, and pushing harder on the same argument is making things worse, not better.

## Diagnosis

You are in a persuading-the-unpersuadable situation when:

- The other person rejects new evidence rather than updating on it.
- They reframe disagreement as personal disloyalty or incompetence.
- You have caught yourself avoiding the conversation because "they'll never change their mind."
- The substantive case has been made — clearly, more than once, with data — and there is still no movement.
- Others in the organization are watching to see whether you will keep pushing, fold, or quit.

If you are dealing with someone who is *thoughtful and open but disagrees on the merits*, you do not need this playbook — you need [[communication/productive-disagreement]]. Use this playbook only when the resistance is structural (one of the four profiles), not analytic.

## First Principles

**1. Personality is situational, not fixed (Grant).**
Every leader has an *if…then* profile. The dominant manager becomes submissive with their superior; the competitive colleague becomes cooperative with the important client. Even the most rigid person flexes in specific conditions. Your job is to *find* those conditions and engineer the conversation into them.

> "If you want to reason with people who seem unreasonable, pay attention to instances when they — or others like them — change their minds." — Adam Grant

**2. The counterpart must arrive at the new view themselves.**
External pressure on a rigid person produces *more* rigidity (the "rubber-band" effect that Grant cites from the Penn-State grading study). The persuasion move is not to push harder — it is to construct the situation in which they discover the new view.

**3. Match the move to the profile.**
Different rigidities have different unlocks. The wrong move on the right profile makes it worse:
- Pressing a *know-it-all* with more evidence triggers defensiveness.
- Hammering a *stubborn* person with conclusions produces snap-back.
- Praising a *narcissist*'s decision-making to soften criticism of it confirms their fragility.
- Backing down from a *disagreeable* person signals you are not worth engaging.

**4. Persuasion is multi-week, not single-meeting.**
Tony Fadell on the iPhone: people *"had to work as a group, not simply in one meeting but possibly over weeks, to get him to change his mind."* Build prototypes; refine pitches; repeat with new framings. The persuasion campaign is the unit of work; the meeting is just the touchpoint.

**5. The leader who *needs* persuading is failing at their own job.**
This is implicit but worth naming. *"When leaders lack the wisdom to question their convictions, followers need the courage to persuade them to change their minds."* Persuading the unpersuadable is a coping strategy, not a steady state — if you find yourself doing it constantly with the same person, the deeper problem is the system that put them in charge of decisions they cannot rethink.

## Step-by-Step Approach — The If…Then Map

### Profile 1 — The Know-It-All (Overconfidence)

**Move: Ask them to explain how it works.**

The Yale "how does a toilet flush?" experiment: students rated their knowledge highly until asked to write out the mechanism. The articulation pressure exposes the gaps the conscious mind has glossed over.

**Operationalize:**
1. Identify the specific causal claim they are confident about.
2. In a low-stakes setting (1:1, side conversation), ask them to walk through the mechanism step-by-step.
3. Listen for the moment the explanation stalls or oversimplifies.
4. *Do not pounce.* The recognition of the gap should arrive from their side, not be inserted by you.
5. Offer the alternative once the gap is acknowledged.

**Wendell Weeks at Corning** is the canonical example: Jobs insisted he understood glass. Weeks let him try to explain the chemistry of strong glass. The conversation stalled. Weeks then said *"let me teach you some science, and then we can have a great conversation"* — and Jobs accepted.

### Profile 2 — The Stubborn (Snap-Back)

**Move: Hand them the chisel.**

Stubborn people see consistency and certainty as virtues. Forceful arguments produce snap-back; light arguments are dismissed. The exception: their own ideas. When *they* are the originator, the same content becomes acceptable.

**Operationalize:**
1. Re-frame your conclusion as a question. Replace *"we should X"* with *"what if we tried X?"* or *"could we maybe X?"*
2. Plant the seed early. Mention the idea casually; do not press for a decision.
3. Let them imagine the possibility, modify it, claim it.
4. When they bring "their" idea to you weeks later — even if it is identical to yours — accept it without correction.
5. Hollywood screenwriter analogy: *toss the idea over; let them build on it and throw it back.* Treat the pitch as catch, not as a speech.

**Mike Bell at Apple** on streaming video: Jobs initially rejected the box concept. Bell planted the seed (*"What if we built a box that would let you play content?"*), let Jobs grow attached, and eventually heard Jobs *advocating Bell's own pitch* to other people. *"I knew I'd succeeded when he was arguing my point and proposing the project I'd pitched him."*

### Profile 3 — The Narcissist (Fragile Self-Esteem)

**Move: Praise a strength *unrelated* to the area where you want change.**

Narcissists have high but *unstable* self-esteem. Criticism reads as ego threat. The instinct to soften with a feedback sandwich does not work — they ignore the middle. The right move: affirm an *unrelated* strength, so they have a secure identity from which to acknowledge an imperfection elsewhere.

**Operationalize:**
1. Inventory their secure-identity strengths. (For Jobs: design taste, vision, intelligence.) Pick one *not* connected to the issue you need to raise.
2. Lead with sincere praise of that strength.
3. Then raise the substantive concern in the *unrelated* area.
4. Do *not* sandwich. Beginnings and endings are remembered; middles are not. Affirm first, deliver second.
5. The strength being praised must be *real*. Manufactured flattery is read as manipulation and produces the opposite of the desired effect.

**The Apple developer-conference critic** kicked off by saying *"Mr. Jobs, you're a bright and influential man,"* then delivered a harsh substantive criticism. Jobs replied with a (rare) public admission of fallibility: *"I readily admit there are many things in life that I don't have the faintest idea what I'm talking about. So I apologize for that…we'll find the mistakes; we'll fix them."*

### Profile 4 — The Disagreeable (Energized by Conflict)

**Move: Stand your ground; do not back down.**

Disagreeable people are energized by conflict and disrespect capitulation. Backing down signals you were not worth engaging — and forecloses your influence permanently. Standing up earns their respect, even if it costs the immediate argument.

**Operationalize:**
1. Rehearse the conversation where you do not yield.
2. Bring concrete evidence and concrete proposals — disagreeable people respect specificity.
3. When they push back hard, push back harder. *Not personally* — substantively.
4. Acknowledge their counter-points where they are correct; concede *only* what is genuinely concessible.
5. Repeat across multiple conversations. Disagreeable people who respect you will eventually move; ones who do not will eventually push you out. Either is better than the indeterminate middle.
6. If you are a junior person facing a disagreeable boss, build allies and prepare a *campaign*, not a single meeting. Tony Fadell on iPhone: the engineers chipped away over months with prototypes and refined pitches.

The 1980s Mac team's annual award for "the temerity to challenge Steve Jobs" — and Jobs's subsequent promotion of those winners to run divisions — is the institutional version of this move.

## Suggested Language

**For the know-it-all (asking them to articulate):**
- *"Help me understand how this would actually work. Walk me through the mechanism — what happens at each step?"*
- *"I want to make sure I'm following. If we did X, then Y would have to happen — how does Y actually play out?"*

**For the stubborn (handing them the chisel):**
- *"What if we…?"*
- *"Could we try…?"*
- *"I've been wondering about [seed]. I don't have a strong view yet — I'd value your thinking."*
- *"You know the constraints better than I do. If we wanted to get to [outcome], how would you go about it?"*

**For the narcissist (praising an unrelated strength):**
- *"You have the sharpest read on [unrelated strength] of anyone I know. That's exactly why I want to talk to you about [substantive concern]."*
- *"I've watched how you've handled [unrelated success]. I want your thinking on something different — I have a concern about [substantive issue]."*

**For the disagreeable (standing your ground):**
- *"I hear your objection. I still disagree, and here's why."*
- *"That's a fair point on [specific]. On [other specific], I think the data still goes the other way."*
- *"I'm not going to drop this. If you want to convince me, I'm listening. If you want me to drop it, I need a better reason than the one you've given me."*

**For the multi-week campaign:**
- *"Can we put this on the next 1:1? I want to come back to it with more data."*
- *"I've been thinking about your concern. Here's the alternative I've been working on…"*
- *"I built a prototype. Can I show you what we found?"*

## What To Avoid

- **Pressing the wrong move.** The four profiles are *not* interchangeable. More evidence on a know-it-all, more force on a stubborn, more flattery near the issue on a narcissist, more capitulation on a disagreeable — all of these *make it worse*.
- **The feedback sandwich on a narcissist.** They ignore the criticism in the middle. Grant: *"The feedback sandwich doesn't taste as good as it looks."*
- **Pre-arguing the conclusion.** Do not walk in with the slide deck. Walk in with the question. (Especially for stubborn counterparts.)
- **Insulting their intelligence.** Even when the case is obvious to you, do not show it. The know-it-all needs to discover the gap themselves.
- **Backing down once on a disagreeable boss.** They will treat you as low-value forever. Better to escalate one conversation than to capitulate three.
- **Treating one conversation as the persuasion attempt.** It is one touchpoint in a multi-week campaign. Plan the next touchpoint before you leave the current one.
- **Conflating profiles.** Many difficult counterparts are *both* narcissistic and disagreeable, or *both* stubborn and overconfident. Sequence the moves: in a mixed profile, start with the dominant trait.
- **Believing the persuasion is one-sided.** Sometimes you are wrong. Hold the possibility open. The if…then map is a *tool*; the truth may still be on the other side.

## Escalation Path

If multiple campaigns have failed:

1. **Re-diagnose the profile.** You may have mismatched the move. Get a second read from a trusted peer who has watched the dynamic.
2. **Build the coalition.** Disagreeable and narcissistic counterparts are sometimes immune to a single voice but movable by a coalition. *"All three of us are saying the same thing"* is different from *"I am saying it."*
3. **Make the decision visible to a third party with authority.** When the substantive case is overwhelming and the rigid counterpart is blocking it, surface the decision to someone with the authority to break the tie. Use carefully — this is a one-time political move.
4. **Decide whether to stay in the relationship.** If a leader cannot be persuaded on a decision that matters, and you cannot route around them, the question becomes whether to continue in the role. This is the [[principles/courage]] question — and the implicit alternative the playbook does not solve.

## Follow-Up Actions

- Within 48 hours of the conversation, write down: what was the move? what did the counterpart say? what is the next touchpoint? what is the new evidence I will bring?
- If the counterpart claimed *your* idea as their own (the stubborn-profile win condition), do not correct them. Let it stand; reinforce it publicly.
- Track which moves work on which counterparts. Over time, your if…then map becomes specific to the people you work with, not generic to the four types.
