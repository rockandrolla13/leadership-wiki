# Difficult Conversations

## Use Case

You need to have a conversation that is uncomfortable for at least one party — performance feedback, a role change, a disagreement with a peer, a "no" to a senior stakeholder, a values-based confrontation, or any moment where the truthful thing is also the costly thing. Reach for this playbook when the alternative is silence and the silence will compound.

## Diagnosis

You are in a difficult-conversation situation when any of the following are true:

- You are rehearsing the conversation in your head and finding ways to avoid it.
- You are softening the message to the point that the listener cannot identify what is wrong.
- You have already had "the conversation" once and nothing changed — which usually means the conversation did not actually happen.
- You are using channels (email, Slack) that allow you to deliver feedback without seeing the listener's reaction.
- You hear yourself thinking, "in a month we're doing formal feedback so I'll just give it then."

Lopp's diagnosis is sharp: "the majority of people-related disasters I've created have originated with my choice to not say the hard thing."

## First Principles

- **The empathy that makes you good at this is also what makes you avoid it.** You project yourself into the listener's seat, feel the discomfort, and conclude the conversation should not happen. The conclusion is wrong. Empathy should make you better at delivering the message, not better at not delivering it.
- **Hard feedback is distilled truth.** Lopp: "In the days and weeks full of vapid 'How ya doings?' 'Great jobs,' and high fives, hard feedback represents a rarely seen report on the state of your ability."
- **The medium matters.** Face-to-face conversations allow you to "see with your eyeballs how they're hearing it." Email, Slack, and async tools systematically remove the bidirectional signal you need.
- **Repetition is the work.** Substantive behavioral change takes months and multiple conversations. One hard conversation does not constitute "having said the hard thing."
- **Your goal is to make feedback in all directions no big deal.** That is the long-run game; this conversation is one step in it.

## Step-by-Step Approach

1. **Write the message before you say it.** Lopp: "Write it down before you say it. Ask a trusted someone to listen to your explanation and see if it resonates." If you cannot write a sentence that names the gap clearly, you are not ready to deliver it verbally.
2. **Schedule a dedicated 1:1 slot or use a recurring one.** Do not stack it on the end of a status meeting. Do not deliver it in passing. The frame is "we are doing one thing right now."
3. **Lead with the specific behavior, not the character.** "In yesterday's review you interrupted [name] three times before they finished" is workable. "You're not a team player" is not.
4. **Connect the behavior to the impact.** Why does it matter? Who is affected? What is the risk if it continues?
5. **Ask whether your assessment lands.** "Is it clear what I'm describing? Do you agree with my assessment?" If they disagree, treat that as the start of a conversation, not the end of one.
6. **Agree to a specific, measurable next action.** "We'll try X in next week's review and check in afterward." Vague commitments to "do better" do not survive contact with the next week.
7. **Plan the next two conversations.** A single hard conversation rarely changes behavior. Schedule the follow-up before you leave the room.
8. **If after multiple verbal attempts the message still has not landed, write it down and deliver it in printed form** — Lopp's escalation move at the two-to-three-month mark.

## Suggested Language

- **Opening:** "I want to talk about something that's been on my mind. It's not urgent, but I've been sitting on it too long. Can we spend the next 20 minutes on it?"
- **Naming the behavior:** "In [specific situation] you did [specific behavior]. Here's the impact I saw: [specific consequence]."
- **Checking understanding:** "Is it clear what I'm describing, and how we might address it? Did that make sense? Do you agree with my assessment?"
- **When they push back:** "What wasn't clear? What did you hear me say? What data do I not have? How do our facts differ?"
- **When you need time:** "Let's agree to table this discussion for today. Give ourselves a week to let the conversation percolate and pick it up next week in our 1:1. We're not on a timetable."
- **Closing with a commitment:** "Here's what I'm taking away: [restate]. Here's what you're going to try: [restate]. Let's check in next Thursday."
- **Receiving pushback as the senior person:** "I want to make sure I'm hearing this — you're saying that I [restate]?"

## What To Avoid

- **Sandwiching the message.** "You're great, but [criticism], anyway you're great" is the format that ensures the listener remembers neither piece.
- **Delivering it digitally.** Lopp is explicit: "Email, Slack, and any other non-face-to-face mediums are avoiding the important educational work of bidirectional communication."
- **Compressing months into one conversation.** If this is the first time they are hearing it, you are months early to any consequence step.
- **Speaking on behalf of others.** "Other people think…" weakens the message and gives the listener a fight to pick with absent parties. Own the feedback.
- **The Grim Reaper demeanor.** If you sound like you've already decided to fire them, they will hear "I should be looking for a new job" instead of "I need to change this."
- **Going in without writing the sentence down first.** You will improvise something softer than what the situation requires.

## Escalation Path

1. **First conversation, verbal, in a regular 1:1.** Diagnose; agree on a next action.
2. **Second conversation, verbal, within 2–4 weeks.** Did the action happen? What did we learn?
3. **Third conversation, verbal, within another 2–4 weeks.** Pattern check: is the behavior changing, partially changing, or unchanged?
4. **Written feedback, delivered in a printed-out form in a 1:1.** Lopp's intermediate step before any formal process. The written word forces clarity and reduces interpretive drift.
5. **Formal performance step.** See [[management/performance-management]] and [[management/underperformance]]. By the time you reach this step, nothing in it should be a surprise.

If the conversation involves a peer or senior person rather than a direct report, the path is similar but the escalation is to your manager or the affected stakeholder, not HR.

## Follow-Up Actions

- Schedule the next conversation before you leave the current one.
- Write a short note to yourself within an hour while the conversation is fresh: what did you say, what did they say, what did you commit to, what did they commit to.
- Watch for the behavior over the next two weeks. Note instances (positive or negative) to bring to the next check-in.
- If the conversation went badly, debrief with a peer or mentor — not to vent but to figure out what to do differently.
