# Listening and Conversation

## Use Case

You need to lead, not by broadcasting decisions, but by holding a conversation that uncovers information, builds trust, and produces shared commitment. Reach for this playbook for: strategy meetings where you want better questions on the table; 1:1s where you suspect your report has more to say than they are saying; cross-functional alignment sessions where everyone is talking past each other; cross-cultural negotiations where yes does not mean yes; or any moment when "What is being unsaid here?" matters more than "What is being said?"

## Diagnosis

You are in a conversation problem (not a content problem) when:

- The meeting felt productive but no decision actually got made — and now you don't know what was agreed.
- Your report keeps nodding and saying "yes" to things they then don't do — you may not have heard what they actually meant.
- You finish a 1:1 and realise you spent 90% of the time talking.
- The dynamic feels off — someone went quiet, someone got loud — but no one named it.
- You realise after the fact that no one asked the obvious question (e.g., "Wait, why are we doing this?").
- You are about to deliver feedback or a decision and are *not* curious about how it will land — that is itself a signal.

## First Principles

- **Leadership is a conversation, not a broadcast.** Groysberg & Slind: "Smart leaders today engage with employees in a way that resembles an ordinary person-to-person conversation more than it does a series of commands from on high."
- **Four properties make conversation productive at scale** (Groysberg's four-I model): *Intimacy* (trust, listening, getting close), *Interactivity* (two-way, not monologue), *Inclusion* (employees as content producers, not just receivers), *Intentionality* (shared agenda, not aimless).
- **People don't ask enough questions** (Brooks & John). Most miss-conversations are missed-question moments. The skill is undertrained because most professions don't teach it.
- **Follow-up questions are disproportionately powerful.** They signal listening, respect, and curiosity. They produce more information than full-switch ("change of topic") questions or mirror questions ("I'm fine, you?").
- **There are five question domains and most leaders cover two of them.** Chevallier/Dalsace/Barsoux: *Investigative* (what's known?), *Speculative* (what if?), *Productive* (now what?), *Interpretive* (so what?), *Subjective* (what's unsaid?). The questions you don't ask are usually the ones that bite you later.
- **In a triggered state, you cannot listen.** Heen & Stone: identity / relationship / truth triggers shut down the part of the brain that can integrate criticism. Recognising the trigger is the first move.
- **Across cultures, the literal content of words is the smallest signal.** Meyer's five cross-cultural rules apply: disagreement style, emotional expression, trust type, yes/no semantics, written recap risk.
- **Self-disclosure is a tool, not a virtue.** Rosh & Offermann: too much disclosure (open book), too little (inscrutable), wrong timing (bumbler), or wrongly calibrated (oblivious) — all destroy the conversation.

## Step-by-Step Approach

### Before the conversation

1. **Decide which of the five question domains you are weakest in for this topic** (use the Chevallier self-assessment if helpful). If you usually go investigative-then-productive, force yourself to plan one speculative ("what if?") and one subjective ("what's unsaid?") question.
2. **Decide your role: listener, advisor, decision-maker, or sounding board.** Different roles need different question mixes. Don't drift between them mid-conversation.
3. **Decide what you will and won't disclose** (Rosh & Offermann's five-step check). Is the personal story relevant to the task? Will it create the impression you intend?

### During the conversation

4. **Open with a context-setting question that requires more than a yes/no.** "How are things going with X?" not "Is X going well?"
5. **Favour follow-up questions over new questions.** Brooks & John: people interacting with someone who asks follow-ups feel "respected and heard." Three follow-ups beats three new topics.
6. **Use "In my world… / In your world…?" framing** (Toegel & Barsoux, borrowed from Schein) when surfacing differences. "In my world, missing a deadline means the deadline was wrong. In your world…?"
7. **Practice the 90-second silence rule.** After you ask a hard question, count to ninety in your head before filling the silence. Most leaders close the silence at 5–10 seconds and lose the answer.
8. **Listen for the gap between what is said and what is meant.** In high-context cultures, the gap is the message. In low-context cultures, it is often where dissent hides.
9. **Notice when a trigger has fired.** Yours or theirs. Heen & Stone's three: *truth* ("you're wrong about the content"), *relationship* ("you of all people"), *identity* ("this means I'm not who I think I am"). Naming the trigger out loud, gently, often defuses it.
10. **In multilateral meetings, watch the airtime distribution.** If two people are doing 80% of the talking, you have lost the conversation. Su's recommendation: invite the quiet ones explicitly by name. Toegel & Barsoux's "five-minute silent writing before discussion" gives quieter members entry points.

### When negotiating across cultures

11. **Map the four cultural dimensions** (Meyer): How is disagreement expressed (upgraders/downgraders)? Is emotional expression valued or considered unprofessional? Does trust come from track record (cognitive) or relationship (affective)? Are yes/no answers literal or layered?
12. **Avoid yes/no questions when you suspect a high-context culture is in play.** Ask "How long would it take you to do X?" not "Will you do X?"
13. **Be careful about written recap.** In Northern Europe and the US, recap is professionalism. In parts of Africa, Asia, and Latin America, written recap after a verbal agreement signals you don't trust your counterpart's word.

### After the conversation

14. **Recap what you heard back to the speaker.** Brooks & John's research: this is the simplest and most ignored trust-building move.
15. **Write down the commitments.** Within 24 hours, write what you said, what they said, what was agreed.
16. **Notice what you didn't ask.** Was there a domain (speculative, subjective) that never came up? Plan it for next time.

## Suggested Language

### Opening

- **At the start of a 1:1:** "Before we get into the status stuff — what's on your mind?"
- **At the start of a strategy meeting:** "Before we propose anything: what are we actually trying to decide today? What would make this meeting worth the hour?"
- **Opening a hard conversation:** "I want to talk about something I've been thinking about. I don't have the answer yet, so I'm hoping we can think about it together."

### Five Question Domains (Chevallier/Dalsace/Barsoux)

- **Investigative:** "What's the cause of this problem? What evidence supports what we're saying? What are we treating as a fact that is actually an assumption?"
- **Speculative:** "What if we did the opposite? What if we couldn't do this at all — what else could we do? What if [external constraint] changes?"
- **Productive:** "Do we have the resources to do this? What is the next concrete step? What do we need to be true before we move?"
- **Interpretive:** "So what does this mean? How does it fit our goal? What did we just learn from that information?"
- **Subjective:** "What is no one saying out loud? Who in this conversation is uncomfortable, and why? Are we genuinely aligned, or are we taking the polite no for a yes?"

### Follow-up questions

- "Tell me more about that."
- "What makes you say that?"
- "What does that look like when it goes well?"
- "Walk me through the worst-case version of that."
- "What would have to change for that to be different?"

### Inviting dissent (countering the petting-zoo failure mode)

- "Who's the person in the room most likely to disagree with this? What would they say?"
- "Let's spend the next five minutes only listing reasons this might fail."
- "What am I not asking you that I should?"

### Cross-cultural / high-context

- **Instead of "Will you do this by Friday?":** "How long would it take you to do this, if you started now?"
- **Instead of "Do you agree?":** "What would have to change in this plan for you to be more comfortable with it?"
- **When someone says "yes" but you read hesitation:** "I want to make sure I'm not putting you in a difficult spot. Is there anything that's making this harder than I realise?"

### When you've triggered someone

- "I noticed something shifted. Can we pause for a minute? What's going on for you?"
- "I'm not asking you to agree with me. I'm asking whether you understand what I'm saying. Those are different."

### Self-disclosure (Rosh & Offermann)

- **Genuine and task-relevant:** "I've been through something similar. Here's what I learned — your situation is different but maybe this is useful…"
- **What to avoid:** opening cold meetings with "let me tell you about my struggle"; performing vulnerability without trust having been earned.

## What To Avoid

- **Question-as-statement.** "Don't you think we should…?" is not a question. It is a closed declaration in question's clothing. Drop it.
- **The interrogation cadence.** Three questions in a row with no acknowledgement of the answer feels like cross-examination, not a conversation.
- **Confusing listening with waiting to speak.** If you are constructing your reply during their answer, you are not listening.
- **Treating silence as failure.** Quiet people are often doing the most thinking. The Japanese-bridge advice from Sarah Stevens (HBR Comm Vol 2): "After asking a question, wait patiently and quietly for an answer."
- **Pluralistic ignorance.** Everyone in the room is privately worried, but no one is saying so because no one else is. The leader's job is to break the silence first.
- **Front-loading questions in a sales/discovery call.** The data on top salespeople: 11-14 questions, scattered across the call, not all at the start.
- **Documenting verbal agreements in cultures where that signals distrust.** Test the norm first.
- **Self-disclosure as bonding shortcut.** Rosh & Offermann's open-book archetype: gives away weakness too fast and burns credibility.

## Escalation Path

If repeated conversations are not landing:

1. **Change the channel.** Move from Slack to email; from email to a call; from a call to face-to-face. Lopp's wiki page argues face-to-face for hard things; Groysberg notes that quality video can substitute for some kinds of intimacy.
2. **Bring a third party who is trusted by both sides** — a "cultural bridge" (Meyer) when the issue is cross-cultural; a senior peer when the issue is interpersonal; HR when the issue is structural.
3. **Move from listening to declarative communication.** Conversation is the default; there are moments (a clear values violation, a safety issue, a deadline crisis) where the right move is to state the position and stop discussing.
4. **Run a five-conversation preempt (Toegel & Barsoux)** with the team if the friction is structural — look, act, speak, think, feel. See [[communication/conflict-language]].

## Follow-Up Actions

- Within 24 hours: written summary of what was said and agreed.
- Within a week: check on the action that came out of the conversation.
- After 90 days: ask the other person, "How do you feel that conversation went? Anything I should do differently next time?"
- Quarterly: audit your own question mix. Take the Chevallier self-assessment. Note which question types you avoid.

## Diagnostic Questions

- In my last meeting, what fraction of the time was I asking versus asserting?
- Which of the five question domains (investigative, speculative, productive, interpretive, subjective) am I weakest in? Which do I never use?
- When was the last time I asked a follow-up question that produced new information?
- Am I able to sit through 60 seconds of silence after asking a hard question?
- If I had to name what wasn't said in the last hard conversation I had, what would it be?
- In the relationships where I keep getting misunderstood, am I assuming literal meaning where the culture is high-context?

## Useful Scripts

- **When you want to slow a conversation that's drifting toward premature consensus:** "Let me push back on this for a minute — not because I disagree, but because no one has, and I want to make sure we've tested it."
- **When you suspect quiet dissent:** "Before we move on — is anyone holding back something they want to say?"
- **When you're not sure you've understood:** "Let me try to say back what I think you mean, and you tell me where I'm wrong."
- **To invite a junior person:** "I'd like to hear from you specifically on this. What's your read?"
- **When someone has shared something hard:** "Thank you for telling me. I want to sit with that for a minute before I respond. Is that OK?"
- **To probe an under-discussed risk (premortem):** "Imagine it's six months from now and this didn't work. Tell me the story of why."

## Open Questions

- The four-I model assumes leaders want bidirectional conversation. In a crisis or values violation, the right mode is sometimes declarative. How do you signal the mode-shift cleanly?
- Question-asking is undertrained, but training it badly produces "leading questions" — the questions of an interrogator, not a curious leader. How is this taught?
- In hybrid / remote settings, the subjective question ("what's unsaid?") is harder because body language is impoverished. What are the remote-first substitutes?
- The cross-cultural rules are well-supported, but they essentialise national cultures. On a globally distributed team where everyone has navigated multiple cultures, the rules apply at the individual level. How is this calibrated?
