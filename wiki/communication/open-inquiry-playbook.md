# Playbook: Open Inquiry When You're the Most Experienced Person in the Room

## Use Case

You are experienced, often right, and perceived as closed to external opinions. You want to genuinely hear your team's thinking — not perform listening, but actually receive information that is better than what you have. This playbook gives exact language for 7 recurring situations.

## Diagnosis

Two things are true simultaneously and the instinct is to treat them as a contradiction:

1. You are more experienced. Your judgment is often better.
2. Your team perceives you as closed to their input. That perception causes real harm regardless of whether it is "fair."

The instinct is to resolve this tension by proving one side wrong. That instinct is the problem.

When people believe their input will be discounted, they do three things:
- Stop volunteering concerns early, when they're cheap to address
- Tell you what you want to hear instead of what is true
- Stop thinking independently — and your judgment becomes the only judgment in the room

> *"The leader's job is not to have all the answers — it is to create the conditions under which the best answers can surface."* — Edmondson, *The Fearless Organization*, 2019

## First Principles

**Being right and being effective are different things.** Even if your judgment is correct 80% of the time, shutting down input costs you the 20% of cases where they see something you don't.

**The order of speaking changes the quality of input.** When the senior person speaks first, everyone else anchors to that position. Go last.

> *([[communication/persuasion]]) Grant's core finding: when the experienced person speaks first, contributions are filtered through agreement or polite disagreement rather than independent thinking.*

**The question before the position.** Make this a rule: you do not share your position until you have heard and restated theirs.

**Restate before you respond.** Before sharing your view, say back what you heard — accurately and in the strongest version of their argument. If they confirm you've got it right, you've earned the right to respond.

## Step-by-Step Approach: 7 Situations

---

### Situation 1: You walk into a meeting with a strong view

**The instinct:** Open with your position to save time.

**The problem:** Everyone now filters their contribution through agreement or polite disagreement. You get edited input, not real input.

**What to do:** State the question, not your answer.

**Scripts:**
> "I want to hear what you're all seeing before I say anything. What's your read on this?"

> "I have a view, but I'm going to hold it. [Name], what would you do here and why?"

> "Before we get into solutions — what's the actual problem we're solving? I want to make sure we're all looking at the same thing."

**If the room is silent:** Wait. Count to ten silently. Resist the urge to fill the silence with your own answer. Silence is not agreement — it is the gap where the real answer lives.

**What to watch for:** Does the first person to speak look at you for a reaction before continuing? That is the signal that they are self-censoring in real time.

---

### Situation 2: Someone says something you immediately disagree with

**The instinct:** Correct it. Explain why it's wrong. Move on.

**The problem:** You have just taught the room that disagreeing with you has a cost. Every person who watched that exchange will calibrate their next contribution accordingly.

**What to do:** Get curious before getting corrective.

**Scripts:**
> "Walk me through your reasoning. I want to understand how you got there."

> "What are you seeing that leads you to that?"

> "What would have to be true for that to be the right call?"

> "What's the strongest version of that argument? Make the case."

**After they explain** — if you still disagree:
> "I see it differently — here's where I land and why. But I want to know: what am I not seeing in your model?"

**What to watch for:** If they have a real answer to "what am I not seeing?" — you just learned something. If they don't, you can hold your position with more confidence, and they know you genuinely considered it.

---

### Situation 3: Someone's idea is genuinely weak

**The instinct:** Dismiss it, or fix it for them.

**The problem:** If you fix every weak idea, the team never develops the judgment to produce strong ones. You become the only source of quality in the room.

> *([[management/coaching-junior-people]]) Lopp's "let the result be a B" principle — the team must develop their own judgment through doing, not receiving your corrected version.*

**What to do:** Help them find the weakness themselves.

**Scripts:**
> "What's the biggest risk with this approach?"

> "If this goes wrong, where does it go wrong?"

> "What would a skeptic say about this? What's their best argument?"

> "What assumption is this most dependent on? How confident are you in that assumption?"

**If they find the problem:** Let them fix it.
> "Good catch. What would change your approach given that?"

**If they don't find it:** Name it as a question first.
> "I want to test one thing. What happens if [the key assumption] turns out to be wrong?"

**What to watch for:** Are you doing most of the talking? If yes, you're solving, not coaching.

---

### Situation 4: One-on-one — drawing out someone who never pushes back

**The instinct:** Take their agreement at face value.

**The problem:** Agreement from someone who never disagrees is not information. It is compliance.

**What to do:** Make disagreement explicitly safe, in the moment.

**Scripts:**
> "I want your honest read, not the polished version. What's wrong with how I'm thinking about this?"

> "If you had to find the flaw in my argument, what would it be?"

> "What are you not saying that you probably should be?"

> "Tell me something I don't want to hear about this situation."

**After they give you something critical — do not immediately defend.** This is the most important moment.

Say:
> "That's useful. Tell me more about that."

Or simply:
> "Okay. Keep going."

**What to watch for:** Do they hedge before giving honest feedback? ("Well, I'm not sure this is right, but...") That hedge is the cost of psychological safety in your relationship. The more they hedge, the more work there is to do.

---

### Situation 5: You've already stated your position — and someone disagrees

**The instinct:** Defend your position. Explain why you're right.

**The problem:** Are you speaking to update your thinking, or to win? If it's the latter, you're performing the conversation, not having it.

> *([[communication/listening-and-conversation]]) The WAIT diagnostic — Why Am I Talking? — is the check.*

**What to do:** Treat their disagreement as new information to investigate, not a position to defeat.

**Scripts:**
> "Say more. I want to understand your objection fully before I respond."

> "What would change my mind? If you could show me one thing that would shift my view, what would it be?"

> "Let me see if I can restate your position fairly." [Restate it.] "Is that right?"

> "If I'm wrong, where's the error?"

**After hearing them:**

If updating your view:
> "That changes how I see it. Here's where I land now..."

If holding your view:
> "I've heard your argument and I've genuinely considered it. I still come out in the same place, and here's why. But I want to flag: I might be wrong about [specific assumption]. We should watch for that."

**What to watch for:** Are you capable of updating your position in front of the team? If you never do, even when you should, they will eventually stop offering alternatives.

---

### Situation 6: You want to invite broader input in a group setting

**The instinct:** Ask "Does anyone have thoughts?" and wait.

**The problem:** Open invitations in a room where the senior person has a known view get answered by one person, or no one.

**What to do:** Ask directed, specific questions to specific people.

**Scripts:**
> "[Name], you've been quiet — what's your read?"

> "[Name], you're closest to the customer on this. What would they say?"

> "[Name], you disagreed with this approach six months ago. Has your view changed?"

> "I want to hear from the people who haven't spoken yet. [Name], [Name] — what are you seeing?"

**Framing when you sense hesitation:**
> "I'm asking because I genuinely don't know the answer. I'm not testing you."

> "There's no wrong answer here — I want your actual instinct."

**What to watch for:** Who never speaks unless directly called on? That person has probably learned that the meeting is not a safe place to speak.

---

### Situation 7: Someone gives you feedback that you're closed — in the moment

**The instinct:** Defend yourself. Explain that you are actually open.

**The problem:** That response confirms exactly what they believe.

> *([[communication/feedback-scripts]]) Heen and Stone's "truth trigger" — the impulse to evaluate whether feedback is accurate before receiving it. Truth-triggering collapses the conversation into a debate about the feedback, not the behaviour it describes.*

**What to do:** Receive it. Investigate it. Do not defend.

**Scripts:**
> "I appreciate you saying that. Can you give me a specific example? I want to understand what it looks like from your side."

> "I'm not going to defend myself on this right now — I want to understand it first. When did you last notice this happening?"

> "What would look different to you if I were genuinely open to your view?"

**After they answer:** Summarise what you heard.
> "So what I'm taking from this is [summary]. Is that right?"

Then commit to one specific change:
> "Here's what I'm going to try: [specific behavior]. Tell me in two weeks whether it's made a difference."

**What not to say:**
- "I think you're misreading my intent."
- "I actually do consider everyone's opinion."
- "I just have more context than the team on this one."

Each of these confirms the feedback, even as it denies it.

## Suggested Language

**The one follow-up question that works in every situation:**
> "Tell me more about that."

It works universally. It costs nothing. It almost always surfaces something you needed to know.

> *([[communication/listening-and-conversation]]) "The follow-up question is the most undertrained conversational move in senior leadership. Most leaders front-load questions and never come back."* — Groysberg & Slind, HBR Communication Vol. 2

**The restate discipline:**
> "Let me make sure I understand your position. You're saying [restate]. Is that right? What's the strongest version of that argument?"

**Making disagreement safe:**
> "I want your honest read, not the polished version."
> "I'm asking because I genuinely don't know the answer."
> "Tell me something I don't want to hear."

**When you update your view — say so explicitly:**
> "That changes how I see it."

This single sentence, said in front of the team, does more for psychological safety than any structural intervention.

## What To Avoid

- **Do not ask leading questions.** "Don't you think we should...?" is an opinion wearing a question mark.
- **Do not ask for input and immediately rebut it.** This teaches the room that contributing is futile.
- **Do not fill silence.** Silence is where honest answers form.
- **Do not conflate compliance with agreement.** A team that has stopped pushing back has learned that pushing back isn't worth it.
- **Do not make general improvement promises.** Commit to one specific behaviour change, name it, and invite accountability.
- **Do not defend against feedback by proving the feedback wrong.** The defence IS the evidence.

## Escalation Path

If the above practices don't shift the dynamic within 4–6 weeks:

1. Have a direct one-on-one conversation with the person who gave you the feedback: "Has anything changed? What am I still doing?"
2. Ask one trusted team member to give you a real-time signal in meetings — a word or gesture when you are closing down rather than opening up
3. Consider whether the issue is structural (the team lacks the expertise to give you input worth having) vs. relational (they have stopped trying)

If it is structural: invest in developing the team's capabilities rather than adjusting your listening posture alone.

If it is relational: the listening posture changes have to come first, consistently, before the team will test whether it is safe to contribute again.

## Follow-Up Actions

- After your next significant team discussion, ask one person privately: "Did you hold anything back in that conversation?"
- Pick one situation from this playbook that you will face in the next 48 hours. Write down the exact script you will use before you walk in. Debrief after: did you restate their position before responding?
- Track for two weeks: how many times did you hear something in a team meeting that changed your view?

## Diagnostic Questions

1. In my last three team meetings, how many times did I hear something that changed my view?
2. Who on my team last openly disagreed with me in a group setting? When?
3. When I ask "what do you think?" — do I already know what I think they should say?
4. In the last month, has anyone told me something I didn't want to hear? Did I reward or punish that?
5. What has my team stopped bringing to me that they used to bring?
6. Can I name one decision in the last month where someone else's input was the deciding factor?
