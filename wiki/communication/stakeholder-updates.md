# Stakeholder Updates

## Use Case

You need to communicate the state of your team, project, or initiative to people outside the day-to-day work — your skip-level, the board, a partner team's leader, a customer, an investor, a regulator. The audience does not have your context, has limited attention, and is making decisions based on what you tell them. This playbook is for getting the update right the first time so you do not spend the next quarter undoing the impression.

## Diagnosis

You need a deliberate stakeholder update — not just status — when:

- The stakeholder is going to make a resourcing, prioritization, or political decision based on your update.
- You are presenting bad news for the first time and the framing will shape the response.
- The stakeholder has asked the same question multiple times — which means previous updates have not answered it.
- You notice you are spending more time writing the update than doing the work; that is a sign the update is doing decision-making work, not status-tracking.
- You are between two layers of organization — a manager-of-managers in HBR's "Bricklayer to Architect" shift — where the work is too far from leadership for them to see it without you.

## First Principles

- **Stakeholders allocate attention.** HBR Vol. 2 ("How CEOs Manage Time"): senior leaders spend 3% of their time on investors and find that surprising. Your update has to compete with everything else fighting for that 3%.
- **Lead with the headline.** The first sentence is the update. Everything else is context for people who want it.
- **Frame the WHY before the WHAT.** Sinek's Golden Circle scales to stakeholder updates: lead with why this matters, then how you're doing it, then what is happening this week.
- **Bad news travels well only when it travels early.** A stakeholder who learns about a slip from someone else has lost trust in you; the same slip surfaced first is a signal of competence.
- **Stakeholders do not want a transcript — they want a decision they can verify or change.** Your update should make their decision easier, not transfer your indecision to them.
- **"Step up, do the vision thing."** HBR Vol. 2's case of Anne, the senior manager whose detail-heavy updates failed to land with the board chairman, is the prototypical failure of stakeholder communication: technically correct, narratively absent.

## Step-by-Step Approach

1. **Know who you are updating, and why _they_ care.** What decision are they making? What are they exposed to? What did they care about last time?
2. **Write the one-sentence headline first.** If the stakeholder reads only the first line, what should they walk away with?
3. **Structure as: Headline → Why It Matters → Status → Asks → Risks.** In that order. Stakeholders should not have to read to find the asks.
4. **Color-code or status-tag honestly.** Green/yellow/red drift toward green over time when there is no consequence for honesty. Use the colors that a peer would use.
5. **Surface bad news in your own framing first.** If you bury the slip on page 3, the stakeholder will find it and decide you were hiding it.
6. **Name the ask explicitly.** "We need X by Y date" or "no asks this week." Implicit asks do not get fulfilled.
7. **Anticipate the question.** What is the first question this update will produce? Answer it in the update.
8. **Send before the meeting, not in it.** Async-first updates protect the meeting for the conversation that the document cannot have.

## Suggested Language

### Headlines

- **Project on track:** "On track to ship X by [date]. One open risk on [Y]; mitigation in progress."
- **Project slipping:** "[Project] slipping by [N weeks]. Root cause: [one line]. Recovery plan: [one line]. Ask: [one line]."
- **Mixed news:** "[Headline win] this period. [Headline concern] this period. Asks below."
- **Pure update:** "Status: [color]. No asks this period. Top risk: [one line]."

### Framing Bad News

- **Owning it:** "We missed [target]. Here's how we got there, what we know now, and what we're going to do next."
- **Distinguishing root cause from symptom:** "The visible problem is [X]. The underlying cause is [Y]. The fix has to address [Y], which means [implication]."
- **Asking for what you need:** "To recover, we need [specific resource / decision / cover]. Without it, the slip extends to [date]."
- **Refusing to over-promise the recovery:** "I want to be honest about confidence: I think there's a 70% chance we hit [recovery date]. I'd rather under-commit and beat it."

### Stakeholder Pushback

- **When they question the approach:** "Here's the alternative we considered, and here's why we didn't pick it. If you want us to revisit, here's what would change in the timeline."
- **When they want more detail than you have:** "I don't know that yet. I'll find out by [date]. What would you do with the answer?"
- **When they want to redirect priorities:** "Got it. If we do X, we will not do Y. Is that the trade you want?"

### Steady-State Communication

- **Weekly written update (for a manager-of-managers):** Headline / Wins / Risks / Asks / Lookahead. Keep it under one screen.
- **Monthly skip-level summary:** Same structure, with one paragraph of "what changed since last month."
- **Pre-meeting note:** "Here's the doc for tomorrow's review. The one decision I'm asking for is [X]. Everything else is context."

## What To Avoid

- **Burying the ask.** Stakeholders should not have to mine for what you need.
- **Status colors that drift green.** If everything is always green, the color is decorative. Use yellow and red when they apply.
- **Surprises.** Lopp's "No Surprises" principle: anything a stakeholder learns from someone else first is a trust transaction you have spent.
- **Detail in place of judgment.** HBR Vol. 2's Anne lost the board chairman by relying on "facts, figures, and spreadsheets" instead of pulling him in as an ally. Stakeholders want your synthesis, not your data.
- **"All talk and little content" (Ibarra).** The opposite failure: a vision-heavy update with no verifiable signal underneath it.
- **Performing alignment you do not have.** If you and a peer disagree on priorities, the stakeholder will notice in the update or in the room. Resolve it first or surface it explicitly.
- **One-way communication.** Updates that do not invite a question produce stakeholders who do not engage until something goes wrong.

## Escalation Path

1. **If the stakeholder is not responding to written updates, switch to a recurring 15-minute live touchpoint.** Some stakeholders read; others process verbally.
2. **If the stakeholder is responding but in ways that suggest they are not absorbing the headline, restructure the update.** The format is wrong.
3. **If the stakeholder is making decisions that contradict the information you have given them, schedule a direct conversation.** Either you are misreading their priorities or they are missing your context.
4. **If you are managing a stakeholder you cannot reach directly** (e.g., a senior exec accessible only through their chief of staff), invest in the chief of staff relationship. HBR Vol. 2's CEO-time research highlights how much of senior leaders' information flow runs through their EAs and chiefs of staff.
5. **If the stakeholder repeatedly surfaces the same concern, the update has not actually answered it** — change the answer or change the framing.

## Follow-Up Actions

- After every update, note: what was the first question it produced? Could you have answered it in the update?
- Quarterly, review whether your stakeholder map is current. Who has joined, left, or changed roles?
- Watch for the absence of questions — silence often means the stakeholder has stopped engaging, not that the update was clear.
- If a stakeholder is consistently surprised by something you knew, audit your distribution list and your headlines.
