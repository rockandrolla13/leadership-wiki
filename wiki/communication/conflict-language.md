# Conflict Language

## Use Case

Two people on your team are in a sustained disagreement. A peer is blocking your initiative. A senior stakeholder has taken a position you cannot move. You are in a meeting where the temperature has risen and someone is about to say something they will regret. This playbook is the language for de-escalating, surfacing the actual disagreement, and moving from heat to resolution.

## Diagnosis

You are in a conflict that needs intervention when:

- The same disagreement is being relitigated in successive meetings without resolution.
- People are routing around each other rather than talking to each other.
- The conversation has moved from "what is the right answer" to "what is wrong with you."
- One party has stopped speaking in the room and is now speaking in hallways and 1:1s.
- The conflict is being absorbed by the rest of the team — three other people are now spending time managing the tension.

Lopp's "Gossip, Rumors, and Lies" frames a related diagnosis: when there is a "gray space" — an information vacuum about a decision or a person — the team will fill it with stories, and those stories will become facts.

## First Principles

- **Conflict is data.** A productive disagreement surfaces information that consensus would have hidden. Your goal is not to eliminate the disagreement but to make it cheap to have.
- **An idea gets better with debate, not agreement.** Lopp: "An idea doesn't get better with agreement; it gets better with debate. It gets better when a diverse set of humans have a chance to stare at it and share their unique, informed perspectives."
- **Heat means the conversation has stopped being about the work.** When the temperature rises, the next move is not louder — it is slower and more specific.
- **Most conflict is about a hidden third thing.** The two parties are arguing about X, but the actual disagreement is about Y (priorities, status, trust, identity). Name Y.
- **Compromise is not the goal.** A bland synthesis that nobody owns is worse than picking one side cleanly. HBR Vol. 2 notes the trap of "false synergy" in paradox-management.

## Step-by-Step Approach

1. **Slow down the conversation.** When the temperature rises, the first move is to reduce pace. Take a breath. Restate the question.
2. **Separate position from interest.** What does each side _want_ versus what do they _need_? Positions clash; interests often do not.
3. **Make each side restate the other's view.** Until each party can state the other's position in a way that party accepts, no one is ready to disagree.
4. **Name the hidden third thing.** "I notice we keep coming back to this. I wonder if what's actually going on is [X]?"
5. **Decide who decides.** Most stuck conflicts are stuck because no one knows who has the decision right. Name the decider.
6. **If the conflict is between two reports, do not mediate it for them on the first round.** Coach each separately, then bring them together. The goal is that they can resolve the next one without you.
7. **Document the decision and the disagreement.** "We're going with X. [Name] disagreed on these grounds; we're proceeding anyway. Here's how we'll know if we got it wrong."
8. **Close the loop.** Two weeks later, check back: is the decision holding? Did the dissenter's prediction come true?

## Suggested Language

### De-escalation in the Moment

- **Slowing the room:** "Let me slow this down. I want to make sure I'm tracking. Can each of you restate where you are?"
- **Surfacing the disagreement:** "I think we're disagreeing about something more fundamental than this decision. Can we name it?"
- **Cooling a personal exchange:** "I notice the conversation has shifted from the work to each other. Can we step back to the work?"
- **Inviting the quiet one:** "[Name], you've been quiet. Where are you on this?"
- **Naming the temperature:** "We're getting heated. I want to keep going, but I want to do it with a lower temperature. Five-minute break?"

### Surfacing the Actual Disagreement

- **Positions vs. interests:** "Tell me what you want from this decision, and tell me what you need. They're different things."
- **Steel-manning:** "Before we keep arguing, can you state [their] position in a way they would agree with? Then they'll do the same for yours."
- **Naming the hidden third thing:** "I keep hearing this come back to [resourcing / status / who got credit last time]. Is that actually what we're working on?"
- **Asking for the evidence:** "What would change your mind on this? If we're not arguing about something that could be settled, we're arguing about something else."

### Resolving and Moving On

- **Naming the decider:** "I'm going to make the call on this. I've heard you both. Here's what we're doing and why."
- **Disagree and commit:** "I hear you don't agree. I'm asking you to commit anyway. If we're wrong, [specific signal] will tell us, and we'll revisit."
- **Documenting the dissent:** "[Name] disagreed with this decision on [grounds]. We're proceeding because [reason]. If [signal] happens, we revisit."
- **Closing a peer conflict:** "Here's what I think we agreed: [restate]. Did I get that right?"

### Coaching Two Reports In Conflict

- **In the 1:1 (separately):** "Tell me what's actually going on. I want your version before I do anything."
- **Bringing them together:** "I've heard both of you separately. I want to spend the next 30 minutes with both of you in the room, working out what to do. I'm not going to decide for you unless you ask me to."
- **Refusing the triangulation:** "I'm not the right person to deliver that message. You need to say it directly. I'll help you figure out how, but I won't say it for you."

## What To Avoid

- **Matching the heat.** When someone raises their voice, raising yours back ends the productive part of the conversation.
- **Mediating prematurely.** If you absorb every conflict between two reports, they will never develop the skill to resolve the next one.
- **Forcing premature consensus.** Lopp: a quick agreement is often a sign that the dissenter has given up, not that they agree. Watch for unusually quiet acquiescence.
- **Triangulation.** Carrying a message from A to B that A should be delivering directly. You become the conflict's conduit and the actual conversation never happens.
- **Resolving symptoms while leaving causes.** If the same two people fight every quarter, the issue is not the quarterly decision; it is whatever sits underneath.
- **Treating dissent as disloyalty.** Lopp: "the humans around you, watching you act, have the additional context and the experience required to make important observations about both your successes and your failures."

## Escalation Path

1. **In-the-room intervention.** Slow down, surface, decide.
2. **Separate 1:1s with each party** within 24 hours of an unresolved conflict.
3. **Mediated conversation with both parties** if the 1:1s do not resolve it.
4. **Decision by the leader** if mediation does not resolve it. Document the decision and the dissent.
5. **Role or structural change** if the conflict is between two people who structurally cannot stop colliding. Sometimes the answer is to redesign the interface.
6. **HR / formal process** only if the conflict involves conduct, harassment, or bullying.

## Follow-Up Actions

- 48 hours after the resolution, check in with each party privately: "How are you sitting with the outcome?"
- Two weeks later, check whether the decision is holding. If the dissenter's prediction came true, name it and learn.
- If the same parties are in conflict again within a quarter, the problem is structural — look at decision rights, role boundaries, or shared incentives.
