# Strategy Communication

## Use Case

When communicating direction, change, or decisions to a team or organization. When you need people to act — not because they were told to, but because they understand why it matters. When you are trying to build alignment rather than compliance.

## Diagnosis

You have a strategy communication problem when:
- People execute the letter of the direction but not the spirit
- People are confused about priorities when they face a choice you didn't anticipate
- Decisions you thought were aligned keep re-surfacing as debates
- Team members cannot explain the strategic direction in their own words
- Motivation is low despite clear direction

The root cause: you communicated WHAT and HOW without communicating WHY.

## First Principles

1. **Start with WHY.** People don't buy what you do; they buy why you do it. The WHAT and HOW are the delivery mechanism; the WHY is what inspires action and creates alignment.
2. **Manipulation produces compliance, not loyalty.** Incentives, fear, and peer pressure can drive behavior once. They cannot sustain alignment through difficulty. Inspiration — connection to a shared WHY — produces people who act even when you're not watching.
3. **Leadership is a conversation, not a broadcast.** (Groysberg & Slind, HBR) One-way communication produces information transfer but not alignment. Alignment requires dialogue — people must be able to interrogate the strategy, raise concerns, and see their input matter.
4. **Organizational conversation has four elements:** Intimacy (getting close), Interactivity (promoting dialogue), Inclusion (expanding employee roles), Intentionality (pursuing a coherent agenda). All four matter; absence of any one degrades the conversation.
5. **Explain the logic, not just the conclusion.** People who understand the reasoning can extend the strategy to new situations. People who only know the conclusion cannot. This is the difference between strategic alignment and tactical compliance.

## Step-by-Step Approach

1. **Start with WHY.** Before announcing the WHAT, articulate why this direction matters — the belief or purpose behind it, the problem it solves for others (customers, team, broader mission), and why you believe in it.

2. **Then explain HOW.** The principles, values, or approach that will guide implementation. What behaviors or decisions will characterize the path.

3. **Then state WHAT.** The specific actions, decisions, or changes you are announcing. Only after the WHY and HOW are clear does the WHAT make sense.

4. **Create dialogue.** Don't broadcast — invite response. "What am I missing?" "What concerns does this raise for you?" "Where do you see gaps in the logic?" Make this genuine: if people raise points that change your view, say so.

5. **Make the conversation interactive.** Town halls, Q&A, small-group sessions, written channels for asynchronous response. People need to be able to engage, not just receive.

6. **Include people in shaping the HOW.** Strategy belongs to leadership; implementation principles can be co-developed. The more people contribute to how the strategy is executed, the more they own the outcome.

7. **Follow through on what was heard.** If feedback was gathered, close the loop. "Last week you raised X — here's what we decided to do with that input." Silence after solicited input is worse than not asking.

## Suggested Language

- **Opening a strategy communication (WHY first):** "Before I explain what we're changing, let me explain why we exist and why this situation puts that at stake. We believe [WHY]. This decision is about protecting and advancing that."
- **When people are focused only on WHAT:** "I want to make sure everyone understands the reasoning, not just the decision — because you'll face situations I haven't anticipated. Here's the logic..."
- **Inviting genuine dialogue:** "I've told you what we're doing. Now I want to hear what you're thinking — not just affirmation. What concerns does this raise? What am I missing?"
- **When acknowledging input changed the direction:** "Several people raised a concern I hadn't considered. That changed our thinking. Here's what we're doing differently as a result."

## What To Avoid

- **Leading with WHAT without WHY.** Produces compliance at best, resistance at worst. People need the purpose before they can commit to the action.
- **Announcing, not conversing.** One-way communication from the top, with no mechanism for response, signals that people's views don't matter. This damages engagement.
- **Fake dialogue.** Soliciting input with no intention of using it. This is worse than no dialogue — it teaches people that speaking up is pointless.
- **Manipulation as a substitute for inspiration.** Price incentives, fear-based messaging, and peer pressure drive one-time behavior but create no loyalty. Under pressure, people who were manipulated into alignment will defect.
- **Assuming alignment from silence.** Absence of objection is not agreement. People who don't trust the environment won't object; they'll quietly comply and then underperform or leave.

## Escalation Path

If strategy communication has failed and the team is not aligned:
1. Name the gap explicitly: "I think we have a communication breakdown. Let me revisit this."
2. Start over with WHY before trying to re-explain the WHAT.
3. Have smaller, more dialogic conversations before trying broad broadcasts again.
4. If one-way communications aren't producing alignment, move to listening sessions — structured conversations where the agenda is to understand concerns, not defend the strategy.

## Follow-Up Actions

- After any major communication: hold small-group sessions to check comprehension and surface concerns
- 2-4 weeks after: assess whether behavior aligns with the strategy. If not, diagnose: Is it a comprehension problem? A motivation problem? A resource problem?
- Build feedback loops into the communication plan from the start

## Sources

Sinek (2009): The Golden Circle — WHY → HOW → WHAT — explains why some leaders inspire action while others produce only compliance. The biology: WHAT and HOW are processed by the neocortex (rational thought); WHY is processed by the limbic system (emotion, decision-making). People can hear your WHAT and know everything about it and still not act. When you reach the limbic system with WHY, they act because they want to, not because they were told to.

Sinek/Mead/Docker (2017): The WHY is the foundation of inspiring communication. Manipulation (price, incentives, fear) produces transactions; inspiration (shared WHY) produces loyalty.

Groysberg & Slind (HBR, 2020): Command-and-control communication is no longer viable. Organizational conversation requires intimacy, interactivity, inclusion, and intentionality. Leaders must speak with employees, not to them. The conversational model is especially valuable because it allows large organizations to preserve the engagement and alignment of small ones.
