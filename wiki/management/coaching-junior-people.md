# Coaching Junior People

## Use Case

You manage early-career engineers, ICs, or first-time leads. They are competent at the work they have been given, but they need to grow into work that is harder, broader, or more ambiguous. Your job is to expand their range without either (a) doing the work for them or (b) abandoning them at the edge of their ability.

## Diagnosis

You are coaching junior people effectively when:

- Their next project is harder than their last, and they know why.
- They bring you problems with a proposed answer, not just the problem.
- Their work in 6 months is visibly different — better, or addressing harder problems — than their work today.
- They are starting to coach the person below them.

You have a coaching problem when:

- They are doing the same level of work they were doing six months ago.
- You are still reviewing line-by-line work you should be reviewing at the design level.
- They come to you with the problem and wait for you to answer.
- They are afraid to make decisions because the last few were "corrected."
- You catch yourself thinking, "It's faster if I just do it" — the Lopp warning signal for the New Manager Death Spiral applies to coaching, too.

## First Principles

- **Coaching is delegation with feedback wrapped around it.** Lopp's "Delegate Until It Hurts" is the operating principle: hand the work over, let the result be a B, and use the gap between B and A as the coaching opportunity.
- **Junior people grow by doing work that is one step above their current ability.** Two steps is overwhelming. Zero steps is stagnation.
- **The leader's job is building the team, not building the product.** Lopp: "their job isn't doing quality work, it's building a healthy team that does quality work at scale." Every hour spent doing junior-level work is an hour not spent coaching the people who should be doing it.
- **Pride is the engine.** Katzenbach & Khan's "pride-builder" framing applies acutely here: junior people need to feel pride in their own work, not just in the team's outcomes.
- **Coaching is a long game.** A 6-month coaching arc is short; a 2-year one is normal. The visible result on day 30 is rarely the signal.

## Step-by-Step Approach

1. **Map the gap.** What can they do today? What do they need to be able to do in 12 months? Write it down so both of you can see it. Lopp's Professional Growth Questionnaire is a useful starting point.
2. **Pick one stretch project.** Not three. One. Big enough that they cannot complete it by doing what they already know. Small enough that you can absorb the worst-case failure.
3. **Frame the project with the WHY, not just the WHAT.** Why does this matter? What does winning look like? What are the boundaries?
4. **Answer questions early; let them run later.** Lopp's Julie example: at the start, answer every question completely. As the project progresses, push more decisions back to them. "What do you think we should do?" replaces "Here's what to do."
5. **When the project goes sideways, coach — do not punish.** Lopp: "When it went sideways, you didn't punish, you coached." Use stories from your own mistakes; do not let the moment become a verdict.
6. **Let the result be a B.** "If it had been me banging on the keyboard, it'd be an A. Shush, little voice." Re-grabbing the work to make it an A teaches them they are not trusted with the next one.
7. **Debrief specifically.** Not "good job, here's what to do better." Specifically: what did they decide, what did they learn, what would they do differently?
8. **Make the next project harder.** Coaching that does not raise the floor is just supervision.

## Suggested Language

### Framing and Delegating

- **Naming the stretch:** "This is bigger than what you've done before. That's intentional. I think you can do it, and I'm going to be available when you need me — but you're going to make decisions you haven't made before."
- **Defining winning:** "Here's what winning looks like: [outcome]. Here's what good-enough looks like: [floor]. Here's what's not negotiable: [constraints]."
- **Inviting questions early:** "Ask me anything. I'd rather you ask me ten questions now than guess wrong for a week."

### When They Come With A Problem

- **Refusing to answer for them:** "What do you think we should do? Start there and I'll push back if I disagree."
- **Building the muscle:** "Don't bring me the problem alone. Bring me the problem and what you'd do about it. If you're not sure, bring two options and your lean."
- **Asking the hard question:** "What's the decision you're avoiding making? Let's talk about why."
- **When they're wrong:** "I see it differently. Here's why: [reasoning]. Walk me through where you would push back."

### Receiving Their Work

- **When the result is a B:** "This is what we needed. Here's what I would have done differently — and that's a coaching note, not a complaint. You shipped it; that matters."
- **Naming what they learned:** "What did you learn from this project that you didn't know three months ago? That's the actual deliverable."

### The Growth Conversation

- **Using the Growth Questionnaire:** "I'd like you to spend an hour answering these questions and bring your notes to our 1:1. The point isn't to grade you; it's to give us a map."
- **Connecting work to growth:** "Here's the project you're on. Here are the skills it's building. Here's the skill it's not building, which is why I'm also putting you on [X]."
- **When they're stuck on a promotion:** "Here's what would need to be true for the next role: [specific behaviors]. Which of those do you think you can demonstrate in the next two quarters?"

### Recognition

- **Specific, timely, selfless** (Lopp's compliment anatomy): "The way you handled [specific situation], specifically [behavior] — that's the kind of judgment that puts you on the path to [next role]."
- **In public, when warranted:** "I want to be specific about what [name] did here. The work you don't see is what made this possible."

## What To Avoid

- **Doing the work yourself.** The single most common coaching failure: stepping in because it is faster. It is faster this week; it is catastrophically slower over a year.
- **Half-delegation.** Lopp's term: giving them the work without the authority or context. They will fail, you will blame them, and neither of you will know why.
- **One-size-fits-all coaching.** Katzenbach & Khan: "good" managers treat the front line as a homogeneous mass. The pride-builder treats each person as an individual with their own definition of success.
- **Conflating coaching with criticism.** Coaching includes naming what worked, not just what did not.
- **Coaching only in performance season.** Lopp: "Lies. It's _always_ performance season."
- **Punishing the first failure on a stretch project.** This teaches them not to stretch.
- **Generic feedback.** "Show more leadership" is not coaching. "In yesterday's design review you asked three good questions and stopped — keep going past three" is.

## Escalation Path

1. **Standard 1:1 coaching cycle** — weekly 1:1s plus stretch projects.
2. **Mentor outside your team.** Lopp: "A mentor is a human you meet with on a regular basis who does not work on or near your team." Especially valuable when the junior person is going through transitions you have not been through.
3. **Stretch role on another team** — when the coaching needed is broader than what your team offers, second them.
4. **If coaching is not landing after 6+ months and the gap is widening**, this has moved from coaching to performance. See [[management/underperformance]].

## Follow-Up Actions

- Every quarter, ask: are they doing harder work than last quarter? If no, why not?
- Every 6 months, do an explicit growth conversation. What changed? What is next?
- Watch for them coaching the next person down. That is the signal that the coaching has compounded.
- Maintain your own list of "future stretch opportunities" so you can match them to the right person when they arrive.
