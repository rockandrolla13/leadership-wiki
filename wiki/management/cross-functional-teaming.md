# Cross-Functional Teaming

## Use Case

You are leading a project that requires people from different functions, different organizations, or different industries to collaborate on a novel problem in a bounded time window. The participants do not share a history, a vocabulary, or a manager. You have no formal authority over most of them. The work cannot be solved within any single discipline. You need them to think *together* — not just pass artifacts back and forth.

This is Edmondson's and Harvey's **extreme teaming** problem: cross-disciplinary, cross-organizational, time-bounded, novel. Stable-team management (Hackman's classical model) does not apply because the team is neither stable nor cleanly bounded. This playbook is the operational guide for that environment.

## Diagnosis

You need this playbook if any of the following are true:

- People show up to the same meeting with the same words meaning different things ("MVP," "owner," "design," "QA-ready" — each function has its own gloss).
- Decisions get made in subgroups and surprise everyone else at the next plenary.
- Sub-teams are blocked waiting for each other but no one has explicitly raised the dependency.
- The first plenary surfaced strong ideas; the second plenary surfaced complaints; the third plenary is silent.
- An expert keeps insisting the answer is in their domain, and another expert keeps insisting it isn't, and you can't tell which is right.
- The project sponsor is asking "where are we?" and the answer changes depending on which sub-team you ask.

These are the predictable interpersonal and technical signals of extreme teaming under-managed.

## First Principles

1. **Diverse expertise is conditional value, not automatic value.** Diverse teams *can* recombine knowledge in ways homogeneous teams cannot — but they will not, unless the leader manages two specific failure modes: people retreating to their silos under stress, and the group discussing only what everyone already knows (the "common knowledge effect").

2. **The leader's job is four functions, not one.** Edmondson and Harvey decompose extreme teaming leadership into a 2×2:

   |              | INTERPERSONAL | TECHNICAL |
   |--------------|---------------|-----------|
   | **MOTIVATE** | Build an Engaging Vision | Empower Agile Execution |
   | **FACILITATE** | Cultivate Psychological Safety | Develop Shared Mental Models |

   The left column generates energy from emotion and relationship. The right column removes barriers around knowledge and skill. The top row produces engagement; the bottom row produces capability. All four must be active; missing any one is a predictable failure pattern.

3. **Vision is built, not pronounced.** In a stable team the vision can be set in advance. In extreme teaming, vision develops as more is learned. The leader's job is to *anchor* the values and the target, then *allow* the details to evolve with input from the diverse camps. Participation in shaping the vision is the engagement.

4. **Psychological safety is the operating system.** Without it, knowledge diversity is wasted. The two specific practices that produce it in extreme teaming: **displaying authentic caring** (visible presence, non-checking engagement, acting on what you hear), and **framing cross-boundary work as a resource** (treating disagreement as "potential for discovery," not as conflict).

5. **Boundary objects move knowledge across disciplines.** Drawings, prototypes, models, shared sketches, even physical artifacts. Verbal coordination across knowledge boundaries fails predictably; an object lets two disciplines stand on either side and point.

6. **Authority must be clear even if the org chart is not drawn.** Project participants must know whom to contact for what kind of decision. Distributed decision-making (Marquet's "I intend to" applied at project scale) only works if the boundaries are explicit.

## Step-by-Step Approach

### Phase 0 — Before the work starts

1. **Identify the four functions of your leadership.** Write down, for this project, what each cell of the 2×2 will look like:
   - What values are you going to make explicit, and what challenging target will you articulate?
   - How will you display authentic caring, and how will you frame disagreement?
   - Where are the disciplinary interfaces, and what boundary objects will you use to bridge them?
   - Which decisions will you delegate to expert clusters, and what room to maneuver will you provide?

2. **Run "get acquainted" sessions.** Edmondson and Harvey's Project Fiona leaders (Marine and Mathieu) hosted two outside events before any technical work began. Cracking jokes, swapping anecdotes, eating together. This is *not* social fluff; it is the construction of the trust that allows technical conflict later. Skipping it predictably costs you in week six.

### Phase 1 — Set the vision

3. **Make values explicit.** State them in *named decisions,* not as bullet points. "We will choose openness over secrecy, even when it costs us speed. To prove this, we just made [specific decision]." Values without specifics are decoration; values demonstrated in a costly choice are culture.

4. **Articulate a challenging target.** Specific number, specific date, specific scope. Example from Project Anna: "$10 million budget, 60,000 square feet, delivery [date], LEED Platinum Certified." Add the *constraints,* the *stakeholders,* and what the project is *not* aiming for ("we are not looking into therapeutic art; we are exploring deeper pediatric implications of immersive technology").

### Phase 2 — Cultivate psychological safety

5. **Display authentic caring through visibility.** Be available; pick up the call; visit subgroups in their offices; participate in their meetings; ask "what can I do to help?" — and mean it. Initiate contact to *offer information,* not to *check on things.*

6. **Frame cross-boundary work as a resource.** When two perspectives clash, name it: "this is exactly the productive disagreement we need. We are not here to settle who is right; we are here to discover what neither of us could see alone." Use phrases like "learning opportunity" and "potential for discovery."

7. **Follow up on what people say.** When a participant raises an idea, return the next week with what you found out about it. This single move signals "your input matters" more effectively than any speech.

### Phase 3 — Develop shared mental models

8. **Diagnose your interfaces.** Where does knowledge from one discipline need to land in another? Draw the interface map (who hands what to whom, when). For each interface, decide whether it needs synchronous face-to-face work, asynchronous artifact exchange, or both. Schedule the face-to-face encounters into the calendar *in advance.*

9. **Co-locate at interfaces.** "The project as we know it today is the result of architects and engineers working side by side at key times in the project." For high-ambiguity work, no email thread substitutes for two experts at the same whiteboard.

10. **Deploy boundary objects.** A live-shared sketch, a working prototype, a wall-sized chart, a clinical role-play, a physical part the engineers can hold while the manufacturing rep explains the defect. The object reduces translation loss between disciplines.

### Phase 4 — Empower agile execution

11. **Form expert decision clusters.** For each non-modular block of work (a coherent piece that doesn't require constant cross-team coordination), name the cluster owner. The cluster makes its own technical decisions and is responsible for outcomes. Maximum autonomy *within* the cluster's domain.

12. **Provide room to maneuver.** Shield the experts from administrative noise. Create formal positions for internal reporting, commercial issues, and interface coordination so the technical specialists are not pulled into them.

13. **Treat discarded options as data, not failures.** When a sub-team kills an option, the leader names it as information: "this told us X about the constraints; we know more now." Project Anna's final design was a fourth option that combined attributes of three rejected ones; the rejections were the path.

### Phase 5 — Operate

14. **Run a clear rhythm.** Daily standups in clusters; weekly cross-cluster syncs; monthly all-hands aligned with sponsor reviews. The cadence is the metronome the team coordinates against.

15. **Manage the second-order signals.** Authentic caring means watching not just the work but the people — who is overworked, who is going quiet, who is being talked over in meetings. The leader sees this; the spreadsheet doesn't.

16. **Celebrate small wins explicitly.** Each interface successfully crossed, each prototype tested, each clarifying conversation. Innovation is the accumulation of small wins; without naming them, the team feels permanently behind.

## Suggested Language

- **Opening a kickoff with values:** "Before we get into the work, I want to be explicit about what we will not trade. We are choosing [value 1] and [value 2], and here is the decision we have already made that proves it. When we hit a hard trade-off later, this is what I will return to."
- **Framing disagreement productively:** "We have two strong views here from people who know more than I do. This is exactly the productive disagreement we need. Let's not try to settle who is right yet — let's lay out what each view assumes and what would change our minds."
- **Inviting input from a quiet expert:** "[Name], I want your view on this specifically because your context is different from the others in the room. What are we missing from where you sit?"
- **Naming an interface:** "There's a hand-off here between the platform team and the integrators. Let's not let it manage itself. Who owns the interface? When will we work on it face-to-face?"
- **Empowering a cluster lead:** "You own this. You don't need my permission to make decisions inside [scope]. Bring me decisions that affect [other scope] or that need resources I haven't provided."
- **Closing a meeting that surfaced disagreement:** "We didn't settle [issue]. That's okay. Here's what we agreed: [list]. Here's what we'll bring back next week: [list]. Here's who owns each."
- **Authentic caring in a 1:1:** "I notice you've been pretty quiet in the cluster meetings. I'm not checking on you — I want to know if there's something I can be helping with. What's going on from your side?"

## What To Avoid

- **Treating the team as if it were stable.** Onboarding rituals designed for permanent teams (six-month ramp, mentorship pairings, career conversations) don't fit. Teaming requires faster, lighter, more visible mechanisms.
- **Letting boundary disputes simmer.** "I thought *you* were doing that" two weeks in is a predictable interface failure. Diagnose interfaces explicitly; do not assume.
- **Using PowerPoint as the only boundary object.** Slides translate poorly across disciplines (engineers want diagrams; clinicians want stories; marketers want narratives). Use multiple objects, including physical ones where possible.
- **Suppressing conflict for "team cohesion."** Cohesion produced by avoiding disagreement is brittle and loses the diversity advantage. Cohesion produced by *productive* disagreement is what you actually want.
- **Confusing authority with information.** Marquet: "those at the top have all the authority and none of the information." Don't make technical decisions you don't have the data for; empower the expert cluster.
- **Skipping the "get acquainted" phase to save time.** Project Fiona's two outside events looked like overhead; they were the foundation that made later technical disagreement productive instead of personal.
- **Optimizing for harmony over learning.** A team that never disagrees is a team that has stopped contributing diverse input.

## Escalation Path

- **If a cluster is stuck for technical reasons:** Convene the relevant interfaces in a single room with the boundary objects. Two days of co-located work usually resolves what two weeks of email cannot.
- **If a cluster is stuck for interpersonal reasons:** Individual 1:1s with the cluster lead and key contributors. Diagnose whether it is a [[principles/psychological-safety|psychological safety]] failure, a vision misalignment, or a personality clash. Each has a different intervention.
- **If two clusters are in conflict over scope:** Return to the [[organization/decision-rights|decision rights]] structure. Either the boundary was unclear (fix it explicitly) or one cluster is overstepping (name it and reset).
- **If the sponsor is losing confidence:** This is usually a vision-articulation failure, not an execution failure. Re-articulate the challenging target and the values, with the specific stakeholder concern named.
- **If you have lost the team's trust:** Authentic caring + framing cross-boundary work as a resource, on repeat, for several weeks. Cannot be shortcut. See also [[principles/trust]] on asymmetric build/destroy.

## Follow-Up Actions

- After each major interface, hold a brief retrospective: what did we learn about this hand-off? What should be different next time? Record it.
- Every two weeks, run a "where are we against the four functions?" check: vision still alive? Safety still intact? Mental models still shared? Execution still agile? Each cell that is fading suggests a specific intervention.
- At project end, document the boundary objects that worked, the interface practices that worked, and the cluster decisions that surprised you. This becomes the playbook for the next extreme teaming engagement — and the institutional memory the organization needs.
