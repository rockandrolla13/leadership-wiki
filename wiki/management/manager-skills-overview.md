# The 17 Manager Skills

## Purpose

The HBR Manager's Handbook decomposes the manager's job into 17 learnable skills, organized in five clusters. The taxonomy is useful for self-assessment ("which of the 17 am I weakest at right now?"), for development planning ("what's the next skill to invest in?"), and for promotion readiness ("what does the next level demand that I haven't yet built?"). It is *behavioral* — each skill is a set of practices, not a personality trait.

## Core Idea

Standing out as a manager does not require exceptional talent in any one skill. It requires above-average competence across the full set, supported by deliberate habits and a working knowledge of how the skills compound. A weak link in any cluster constrains the others: poor delegation undermines time management; poor financial literacy limits influence with senior leaders; poor feedback skill caps the team's development ceiling.

## The Five Clusters and 17 Skills

### Part One — Setting the Stage

1. **Becoming a manager.** Make the transition from individual contributor explicitly. The work is now through others; your output is the team's output.
2. **Managing yourself in the new role.** Understand the new pressures, the loss of IC feedback, and the longer time horizon between action and result.
3. **Building credibility.** Personal credibility — competence + character + connection — is the substrate of authority. Position alone is not enough.
4. **Positioning yourself for success.** Redefine success; understand the organization's strategy; align your team's work to it.

### Part Two — Managing Yourself

5. **Becoming a person of influence.** [[principles/influence]] — positional vs. personal power, managing up, partnering with peers, silo-busting, promoting your ideas.
6. **Communicating effectively.** Finding your voice; mastering written work; persuasive presentations; running effective meetings.
7. **Personal productivity.** Time management essentials, finding focus, stress management, work-life balance.
8. **Self-development.** Career purpose, internal opportunities, feedback from boss and team.

### Part Three — Managing Individuals

9. **Delegating with confidence.** [[principles/delegation]] — benefits of delegation, building a delegation plan, sharing it, providing support, avoiding reverse delegation.
10. **Giving effective feedback.** [[communication/feedback-scripts]] — real-time feedback, difficult feedback, coaching and developing, performance reviews. SBI structure.
11. **Developing talent.** [[management/coaching-junior-people]] and [[coaching/career-development]] — development as a priority, career strategies, high-potential talent, stretch assignments.

### Part Four — Managing Teams

12. **Leading teams.** [[management/team-building]] — team culture and dynamics, cross-cultural teams, virtual teams, productive conflict resolution.
13. **Fostering creativity.** Plan creative sessions, tools for generating ideas, ensuring all perspectives are heard, dealing with negativity.
14. **Hiring and keeping the best.** [[management/hiring]] — crafting the role, recruiting talent, retaining employees, motivation and engagement.

### Part Five — Managing the Business

15. **Strategy — a primer.** [[communication/strategy-communication]] — your role in strategy, what strategy is, developing it, leading change and transitions.
16. **Mastering financial tools.** Basics of financial performance, reading financial statements, budgeting. Non-negotiable for credibility at senior levels.
17. **Developing a business case.** Stakeholder perspectives, clarifying need and value, cost/benefit, risk identification, writing, getting buy-in.

## Steps (How to Use the Framework)

1. **Self-rate each skill 1–5.** 1 = ineffective, 5 = very effective. Be honest. A leader who rates themselves 4+ across all 17 is almost certainly mis-rating.
2. **Get a second opinion.** Ask your manager and one peer to rate the same skills. The biggest learning is usually in the gap between your self-rating and others' ratings.
3. **Pick three skills to focus on.** Two should be where you are weakest; one should be a strength you want to make legendary.
4. **Translate skills into behaviors.** For each focus skill, write three specific behaviors you will adopt. "Be more strategic" is not a behavior. "Spend 30 minutes every Friday updating my one-page strategic alignment doc" is.
5. **Quarterly review.** Re-rate. Adjust focus.

## When To Use

- New-manager-90-day-plan: pick the most urgent three skills based on your team's current needs.
- Promotion preparation: review which skills the next level demands more heavily — financial literacy and business case writing become significantly more important above first-line manager.
- Hiring conversation with your own manager: use the framework to articulate where you need development resources.
- Calibrating direct reports: the framework also works as a competency map for managers reporting to you.

## When Not To Use

- As a pass/fail evaluation. The framework is developmental, not certifying.
- For non-management roles. Senior ICs need a different competency map.
- As an exhaustive list. The framework misses some leadership demands (vision-setting, executive presence, ethical judgment) that emerge at higher altitude. See [[principles/leadership-styles]] and [[principles/leadership-development-stages]].

## Example Application

A 14-month-tenured manager rates herself in the framework. She self-rates 4+ on giving feedback, communicating, and managing yourself. Her manager rates her 3 on those and 2 on developing a business case, mastering financial tools, and becoming a person of influence.

The development plan:

- **Financial literacy:** read the cited HBR Guide to Finance Basics; commit to a monthly 30-minute review of her department's P&L with her finance partner.
- **Business case:** write a one-page business case for an actual current decision and run it past her manager.
- **Influence:** identify two peer relationships she has neglected and schedule a recurring lunch with each.

Six months later her self-ratings and others' ratings have converged. She is invited to present to the leadership team — using the business-case structure she practiced.

## Failure Modes

- **Mistaking the strong cluster for the whole job.** A great communicator who cannot do financial analysis hits a ceiling above first-line manager.
- **Treating "self-development" and "personal productivity" as separate.** In practice they compete for attention. Most managers find they must collapse the two into one weekly hour.
- **Skipping the cross-cluster compound.** Delegation skill compounds with time management, which compounds with strategic alignment. Working on one in isolation produces marginal returns.
- **Avoiding finance because it feels foreign.** Senior leaders read income statements the way engineers read code. A manager who cannot will be perceived as not-yet-credible regardless of other strengths.

## Adaptation For This User

For the wiki's user-archetype (senior IC moving into management), the most underinvested cluster is usually Part Five (Managing the Business). Most new managers over-invest in Part Three (Managing Individuals) because it is closest to the IC mindset, and under-invest in financial tools and business case. Counterintuitive recommendation: spend the next 90 days at 30% on Managing the Business skills, not Managing Individuals.
