# Managing Senior People

## Use Case

You manage one or more senior individual contributors or principal-level engineers — people who, on their core craft, are at or above your level. They may have been at the company longer than you, hold more technical authority than you, or have personal credibility built over years. You need to lead them without pretending to outrank them on the work they do better than you, and without being so deferential that you fail to lead at all.

## Diagnosis

You are in this situation when:

- A direct report has technical authority that exceeds yours on the work in question.
- Your "Old Guard" (Lopp's term) is more credible inside the company than you are.
- You are tempted to defer every contested decision in their domain to them — and they sense it.
- They have been giving you signals (subtle or otherwise) that they think your job is to clear obstacles, not to direct the work.
- You are afraid to give them feedback because the relationship feels asymmetric in their favor.

This is the inverse failure mode from [[management/coaching-junior-people]]: instead of doing the work yourself, you risk over-delegating to the point where you stop leading.

## First Principles

- **Senior people do not need you to do their job. They need you to do yours.** Your job is context, air cover, sequencing, and trust — not technical direction.
- **Authority is not the same as expertise.** HBR Vol. 2's "Authenticity Paradox" (Ibarra) names the tension as "managing distance and approachability." With senior reports, the failure mode is usually too much approachability — you are friends, not their manager.
- **The Old Guard runs on context, not micromanagement.** Lopp: the Old Guard "appears to have a borderline-magical ability to get things done. There is no magic. Just context." Your job is to provide and protect that context, not to compete with it.
- **Feedback flows both ways or it does not flow.** Senior people will give you feedback if you have demonstrated you can take it. If you cannot, they will route around you.
- **Their pride is in the work.** Katzenbach & Khan: pride matters more than money, and senior people in particular are intrinsically motivated by the work itself. Extrinsic carrots ring hollow at this level; meaningful work and visible respect do not.
- **You are accountable for their output, not responsible for producing it.** See [[principles/accountability]].

## Step-by-Step Approach

1. **Establish what you bring before you ask what they need.** Be explicit: "Here's what I'm going to do for you — clear roadblocks, defend priorities, give you the bigger picture. Here's what I'm not going to do — tell you how to design the system."
2. **In the first month, listen more than you talk.** Spend the 1:1s asking them to teach you what you do not know about the work. This is not deference; it is reconnaissance.
3. **Find the things only you can do.** Politics, sequencing, hiring, dependencies, exec context, headcount — the work that senior ICs typically dislike and cannot do without you. Make those things visible.
4. **Give them feedback early, even if it is small.** Lopp's "start with lightweight observations" applies doubly here. The first piece of feedback they receive from you sets the asymmetry of the relationship.
5. **Ask for their feedback. Keep asking.** Lopp: "It can take months. It took a former CEO a year before they took me up on my incessant feedback requests."
6. **When they push back on a direction, distinguish craft objections from priority objections.** If they think the design is wrong, listen carefully — they may be right. If they disagree with the priority, that is your call, and you have to make it cleanly.
7. **Defend their work upward.** Senior people notice when you take the heat for trade-offs they made and when you do not.
8. **Give them work that grows them, not just work that is comfortable.** Senior people stagnate too. The stretch is different — usually scope, ambiguity, or cross-team leadership rather than technical difficulty.
9. **Watch for the Old Guard / New Guard dynamic.** Lopp: when there is a longer-tenured cohort, you have to actively integrate them with the rest of the team, or the team will fork into two cultures.

## Suggested Language

### Setting the Relationship

- **First 1:1 frame:** "I don't think my job is to tell you how to do your job. I think my job is to give you the context, defend the priority, and clear what gets in your way. If I do my job, you'll know more about what's happening across the company than you did, and you'll spend less time on stuff that isn't the work."
- **Asking them to teach you:** "Walk me through how the [system / area] actually works. I want enough context to defend it; I don't want to pretend to understand it more than I do."
- **Naming the asymmetry honestly:** "You know this domain better than I do. That doesn't mean every call is yours. Here's what I think the split is: [propose split]. Push back if you see it differently."

### Giving Feedback Upward in Seniority

- **Lightweight, specific:** "In the architecture review, when [name] proposed the alternative design, you shut it down pretty hard. I get why. I want to give you the read from the rest of the room, which was [observation]."
- **Asking before assuming:** "I want to give you feedback on yesterday's meeting. Is now a good time? It's not urgent."
- **Owning your part:** "I should have brought this up sooner. I noticed it three weeks ago and didn't say anything."

### Asking for Feedback

- **End of every 1:1:** "Do you have any feedback for me?"
- **Lowering the cost:** "I'm not asking you to make a case. If I'm doing something that isn't useful — or that's making your job harder — tell me."
- **Specific solicitation:** "How am I doing on [X]? I think I might be over- / under-rotating, and I want your read."

### Disagreement

- **When they object on craft:** "Tell me what you'd do differently and why. I'd rather change the plan than override the expert."
- **When they object on priority and you can't move:** "I hear you. On craft, you'd win this argument. On priority, this is my call and I'm going to make it. Here's what I'll do: [air cover / sequencing / what changes]."
- **The disagree-and-commit move:** "I'm asking you to commit to this even though you disagree. If [signal] happens, we revisit. I owe you that revisit."

### Growth and Stretch

- **Naming a stretch:** "The thing I want you working on next isn't bigger code; it's bigger scope. Specifically: [cross-team / hiring / mentoring lead / strategy]."
- **When they are bored:** "What would make this job interesting again? I'd rather find that with you than lose you to another team."

### Defending Them Upward

- **In a stakeholder update:** "[Name] made the call on this. I backed it then and I back it now. Here's why."
- **When leadership second-guesses:** "I'd like to bring [name] into this conversation rather than answer for them. They made the decision; they should defend it."

## What To Avoid

- **Pretending to know more than you do.** Senior people detect this immediately. Lopp's rule (Chapter 5, "Assume They Have Something to Teach You") applies — they do.
- **Over-deferring.** If every decision in their domain becomes theirs, you are not their manager — you are their administrator.
- **Avoiding feedback because they are senior.** This is the most common failure mode. Lopp: "the historic professional incentives are designed to prevent us from saying the hard thing." With senior people, your incentives push you the wrong way harder.
- **Treating their disagreement as insubordination.** Disagreement from a senior IC is usually expertise speaking. The question is whether to update your plan or hold your call.
- **Trying to be friends first.** HBR Vol. 2's authenticity-paradox case study (Cynthia, the new manager who shared too much vulnerability too early) maps directly to this: too much approachability undermines your ability to do the job.
- **Letting the Old Guard fork from the team.** Lopp's "Disaster Meeting" — the Old Guard and New Guard not knowing each other's names — is the warning sign.
- **Buying loyalty with carrots.** Katzenbach & Khan: extrinsic rewards can actively reduce intrinsic motivation in people who care about the work.

## Escalation Path

1. **Direct 1:1 conversation** — the default. Most issues with senior reports resolve here if you have built the feedback channel.
2. **Bring a peer or mentor into your own reflection** — when you are unsure whether you are over- or under-leading.
3. **Skip-level conversation** — when you and the senior report disagree on something neither of you can resolve, sometimes the right move is to escalate to your manager together.
4. **Reframe the role** — if the senior person needs broader scope (cross-team, principal track, technical fellow), build that, do not paper over it with a title.
5. **Performance process** — rare for senior ICs, but it does happen. The same rules apply: see [[management/underperformance]].

## Follow-Up Actions

- Weekly: have I given them at least one piece of feedback this week?
- Monthly: am I giving them work they are growing in, or just work they are good at?
- Quarterly: am I defending them upward enough? Do they know I am?
- Annually: would they recommend me as a manager to another senior IC?
- Watch for the silent signal: senior people who are unhappy do not complain — they go quiet, then they leave.

## When a Senior IC Is Structurally Resistant — Use the If…Then Map

A specific case: the senior IC who is overconfident, stubborn, narcissistic, or disagreeable. These are not rare profiles at the top of a technical or functional ladder — they often correlate with the qualities that produced excellence on the way up. Standard feedback moves do not work on them. Use Grant's if…then map (full playbook in [[communication/persuasion]]):

- **The know-it-all senior IC** who rejects suggestions from anyone less expert: ask them to *explain the mechanism* of their preferred approach step by step. The Yale "explain how a toilet flushes" effect generalizes — articulation pressure exposes gaps the conscious mind has glossed over. Wendell Weeks at Corning used this on Steve Jobs.
- **The stubborn senior IC** who treats new directions as attacks: stop pitching conclusions. Re-frame as questions: *"what if we tried…?"* *"could we explore…?"* Let them claim the idea. Mike Bell's Apple TV move is the canonical example.
- **The narcissistic senior IC** whose ego is fused with their technical identity: affirm a real, unrelated strength first. *Do not sandwich.* The strength must be sincere; the substantive concern comes second, from the secure base of the affirmation.
- **The disagreeable senior IC** who challenges every directive: do not back down. Disagreeable people who lose respect for their manager become permanent obstacles. Standing your ground earns respect — even when you lose the immediate argument.

The deeper move: design the conversation to fit the conditional that opens them, not the one that closes them. *Personality is situational; find the if…then condition where they become persuadable.*

## Succession: Coaching a Senior IC Into Your Role

If a senior IC is on a path to your role or a peer's, the Kenneth Freeman model from [[sources/hbr-now-what]] applies even at sub-CEO levels:

- **Force the calendar.** Recurring time, not ad hoc. Freeman: weekly Sunday-afternoon calls for five years with Surya Mohapatra. The calendar discipline is the lever.
- **Look for differences.** The successor whose strengths match where the *team* is going, not where it has been. Promoting the senior IC who resembles you most reproduces your strengths and your blind spots.
- **Make the successor's success your own.** Coach them. Defend them. Be willing to be wrong in front of them. Their growth is your legacy.
- **Don't drag your feet.** Once they are ready, transition. The leader who clings to the role past their successor's readiness creates the bitterest succession failures.

## Managing Senior ICs in an AI-Augmented Environment

A specific challenge from 2026 onward: senior technical ICs whose former craft is increasingly performable by agentic AI. The leadership moves (see [[management/leading-technical-teams]] for the full playbook):

- **Name the shift.** *"The work that used to be your craft is moving to agents. The work that's left — and the work I need you on — is judgment, architecture, supervision, and edge cases."* Do not paper over this; the senior IC has likely noticed before you have.
- **Update the success metrics.** Throughput-of-output gives way to quality-of-supervision, agent-team performance, and judgment-on-edge-cases.
- **Address the identity question directly.** Many senior engineers and analysts find their craft identity threatened by AI. The threat is real; do not dismiss it. Help them locate the new identity — what part of their work is now uniquely human, and how do they double down on it?
- **Watch for atrophy.** Skills delegated to agents will atrophy in humans. Decide deliberately which skills to preserve through practice (and design the practice) and which to let go.
