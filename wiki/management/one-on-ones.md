# One-on-Ones

## Use Case

Weekly recurring meetings with each direct report. The default mechanism for building trust, gathering high-signal information, and enabling coaching. Use this as the first meeting you schedule in a new role and the last meeting you cancel when pressured.

## Diagnosis

You need a 1:1 protocol if any of the following are true:
- You are not sure how your direct reports are actually doing
- Team members bring you surprises rather than early signals
- Your feedback loop runs through group meetings, not individual ones
- You have cancelled 1:1s repeatedly due to schedule pressure

## First Principles

1. **1:1s are the highest signal meeting of the week.** They surface what no other mechanism reliably delivers: the individual's real experience, their perception of blockers, what they're not saying in group settings.
2. **Trust is built through repeated high-bandwidth conversation.** You don't build trust in moments; you build it across hundreds of 30-minute slots where you are reliably present.
3. **The manager's job in a 1:1 is not to talk.** Listening is the primary skill. The content belongs to the direct report. Your agenda is secondary.
4. **Mood sets the agenda.** The stated topic is a delivery vehicle for the actual condition of the person. Read for mood before responding to content.

## Step-by-Step Approach

1. **Schedule immediately upon arrival in any new role.** 30 minutes, weekly, recurring, non-negotiable. Do not start with biweekly.

2. **Open by reading the room.** Ask: "How are you?" and listen carefully to the answer — not the words, but the pace, the word choice, the deflections. This is the real beginning of the meeting. Adjust your agenda based on what you hear.

3. **Let them set the content.** Ask: "What's on your mind?" Let silences breathe. Do not fill them with your own agenda until they have been heard.

4. **Probe one level deeper on anything that matters.** If something registers as important, pursue it. "Tell me more about that." "What made that hard?" You are listening for the important signal, not cataloguing facts.

5. **Cover your items last.** Only after their concerns are on the table do you introduce your agenda. If their concerns consumed the time, that is acceptable — their concerns were the point.

6. **Close with a clear mutual commitment.** What do you each do by next week? State it explicitly. 1:1s compound in value when follow-through compounds.

## Suggested Language

- **Opening:** "How are you?" (not rhetorical — wait for the real answer)
- **When they deflect with "Fine":** "What made this week different from last?" or "What's been on your mind?"
- **When something important surfaces:** "Walk me through that." "What happened when you tried X?"
- **When they seem stuck:** "What would make this easier?" not "Have you tried Y?"
- **Closing:** "So you're going to do X, and I'm going to do Y — let's check in on that next week."

## What To Avoid

- **Cancelling.** Once you cancel a 1:1, the signal is that they are not the priority. Cancel repeatedly and you have taught them you are not available.
- **Making it a status update.** Status is not a 1:1. A 1:1 is for things that don't surface in status reports.
- **Filling silence.** The manager talking 60% of the time is a failed 1:1.
- **Jumping to problem-solving.** Before you advise, confirm you understand the situation. Advice before understanding damages trust.
- **Scheduling too infrequently.** Biweekly means context decays. By the time you meet, the moment has passed.

## Escalation Path

If 1:1s consistently produce no signal: this is a trust failure, not a format failure. Diagnose:
1. Have you been reliable? (Never cancelled, always on time)
2. Have you responded well to what they've previously said? (Did it go badly when they shared something real?)
3. Is there a pattern issue — not a meeting issue?

If still stuck, try a format change: walk instead of sit, different location, specific question to start. Last resort: name the issue directly. "I feel like our 1:1s aren't as useful as they could be. What would make them better for you?"

## Follow-Up Actions

- Maintain running notes on each direct report — not transcripts, but the signal items
- Track what you've committed to; close those loops before the next 1:1
- Review patterns quarterly: what has come up repeatedly? What has never come up that should have?
