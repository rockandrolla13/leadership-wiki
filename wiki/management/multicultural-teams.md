# Managing Multicultural Teams

## Use Case

You are leading a team whose members come from substantially different cultural backgrounds — different national cultures, different professional cultures (engineering vs. marketing), different organizational cultures after an M&A. Friction is showing up in ways that look like personality conflict but trace to cultural defaults around communication, hierarchy, and decision-making. This playbook draws on Jeanne Brett, Kristin Behfar, and Mary Kern's "Managing Multicultural Teams" (HBR Vol. on Building Better Teams) and offers the four-intervention hierarchy they prescribe.

## Diagnosis

Multicultural friction typically takes one of four shapes:

1. **Direct vs. indirect communication.** Some cultures (Anglo, Dutch, German) default to direct feedback; others (Japanese, many Asian cultures, parts of Latin America) communicate around the point and read context. Both are functional within their context; collision produces misunderstanding.
2. **Trouble with accents and fluency.** Native speakers tend to discount the contributions of non-native speakers despite their substantive expertise. The reverse can also happen — over-deference to the native speaker on substantive matters where they have less expertise.
3. **Differing attitudes toward hierarchy and authority.** Cultures with high hierarchy norms (much of Asia, some of Europe) expect senior people to speak first and final; egalitarian cultures (Anglo, Nordic) expect everyone to contribute regardless of rank.
4. **Conflicting norms for decision-making.** Some cultures decide fast and adjust; others decide slowly and once. Some require consensus; others accept majority rule; others defer to seniority.

You are in this playbook's territory when these patterns are visibly disrupting team performance — not when they are merely *present* (they are present in any diverse team).

## First Principles

- **The problem is rarely the difference itself; it's the misinterpretation of the difference.** Brett, Behfar & Kern: "In multicultural teams, the problem isn't usually difference. It's that people cannot interpret the difference correctly."
- **Use the lowest-cost intervention that fits.** The intervention hierarchy (Adaptation → Structural → Managerial → Exit) escalates in cost. Skip levels only when the lower level has failed.
- **Cultural differences interact with power dynamics.** When the senior person comes from a high-hierarchy culture and the junior from an egalitarian one (or vice versa), the friction is amplified.
- **Subgroup formation can be productive or damaging.** Subgroups by culture can produce useful subteams or destructive in-group/out-group dynamics. Structural choices matter.
- **Adaptation requires that team members know cultural differences exist.** "Just trust each other" does not work if neither party understands what they are mis-reading.

## Step-by-Step Approach: The Four-Intervention Hierarchy

### 1. Adaptation (lowest cost)

The team learns about the difference and works around it. Use when:

- Team members can attribute a challenge to culture rather than personality.
- Higher-level managers are unavailable or the team would be embarrassed to involve them.
- The difference is not interfering with critical decisions.

How:
- Name the cultural pattern explicitly. "We have very different defaults on direct feedback. Let me describe both and propose a working norm for this team."
- Establish a debrief loop: when direct feedback lands hard, the recipient can flag it; the giver explains intent; the team learns.
- One example from Brett et al.: an international research team realized their leader's executive status made them go silent in meetings; the leader hired an outside facilitator (a structural move) to free participation.

### 2. Structural Intervention

Reorganize to reduce friction. Use when:

- The team has obvious subgroups, or members cling to negative stereotypes of one another.
- Adaptation has been tried but the friction is sustained.

How:
- Subdivide to mix cultures or expertise rather than concentrate them.
- Rotate facilitation. Have the most senior person not chair every meeting.
- Restructure decision-making: explicit "round the room" before the senior person speaks; written input ahead of discussion to surface less-fluent contributors.
- One example: a software team's lingua franca was English but several members had pronounced accents. The manager directed them to tell customers: "I realize I have an accent. If you don't understand what I'm saying, just stop me and ask questions." Customers complied; performance improved.

### 3. Managerial Intervention

Leader makes final decisions without team involvement. Use sparingly — when:

- A new team needs guidance in establishing productive norms.
- The problem has produced a high level of emotion.
- The team has reached a stalemate.
- A higher-level manager is able and willing to intervene.

How:
- Set the norm directly. Explain the reasoning. Indicate that it is provisional but binding.
- Acknowledge what the team could not resolve itself; do not pretend it was their decision.
- Plan the next step that returns autonomy to the team.

Risk: the team can become overly dependent on the manager. The intervention should be time-bounded and explicit about why managerial decision was required.

### 4. Exit (highest cost)

Voluntary or involuntary removal of a team member. Use only when:

- Emotions are running high and too much face has been lost on both sides to salvage the situation.
- The team is permanent rather than temporary.
- The member cannot or will not adapt to the challenge.

Cost: talent and training are lost; remaining team members observe and update their own behavior accordingly. Reserved for situations where the cost of staying is greater than the cost of leaving.

One example from Brett et al.: two members of a multicultural consulting team could not resolve their disagreement over how to approach problems. One member left the firm. Costly, but the team could move forward.

## Suggested Language

- **Adaptation — naming the difference:** "We have very different defaults on directness in this team. Let me describe both styles. I want to propose that in this team, the norm is direct feedback — and that if it ever lands as personal, we have a debrief loop."
- **Structural — rotating facilitation:** "Starting next week, I'd like each of us to chair a meeting in rotation. The agenda is set by the chair. I'll go last."
- **Structural — coaching the accent issue:** "Your accent is part of who you are and we are not going to apologize for it. When you sense a customer has missed something, you can say, 'Let me say that another way' rather than retreating."
- **Managerial — naming the intervention:** "I'm going to make this call directly. The team has been stuck for two weeks. Here is the decision and the reasoning. I want us to revisit in three months — at that point I want the team making this kind of call."
- **Exit — the conversation:** "We have tried adaptation, we have changed the structure, I have made calls directly. The friction has not resolved. I think the right next step is for us to talk about whether this team is the right fit for you."

## What To Avoid

- **Treating culture as etiquette.** The differences are about defaults of attention and authority, not about which fork to use. Surface-level "cultural awareness" training rarely changes behavior.
- **Defaulting to the leader's culture.** Anglo-default communication norms feel "normal" to Anglo leaders; they are one default among many. Imposing them silently is itself a managerial intervention.
- **Skipping adaptation.** Going straight to managerial intervention or exit because the friction is uncomfortable suppresses learning the team could do itself.
- **Persisting too long with adaptation when emotion has taken over.** Once face has been lost, structural and managerial moves become more appropriate.
- **Naming the cultural pattern as a deficit.** "Your culture's indirect communication is the problem" is unwelcome and inaccurate. The pattern is functional in its context; the team needs to negotiate a working norm.
- **Using exit as an early move.** Multicultural teams are expensive to assemble. Exit destroys the investment.

## Escalation Path

- Each level in the hierarchy is the escalation path for the prior level.
- Above Exit: the issue is likely structural at organizational level — the company is not built to support a multicultural team in this configuration. Reconsider team composition or location strategy.

## Follow-Up Actions

- After Adaptation: monitor whether the agreed norm is being practiced. If not, the agreement was rhetoric.
- After Structural Intervention: monitor whether the new structure has actually relieved the friction or just relocated it.
- After Managerial Intervention: name a follow-up date when the team should be able to make a similar decision itself.
- After Exit: brief the remaining team explicitly about what was tried before exit was chosen. Otherwise people will reasonably wonder if their job is next.
- Quarterly: re-rate the four friction patterns (communication, accent/fluency, hierarchy, decision-making). Are any worsening?
