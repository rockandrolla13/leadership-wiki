# Managing Underperformance

## Use Case

A direct report is not meeting the bar. The gap has been visible for at least one or two cycles. You have been telling yourself the situation will resolve on its own. It has not. You need to act early, before "performance management" — Lopp's loaded phrase — becomes the only option.

## Diagnosis

The clearest diagnostic Lopp offers is the "checklist sentence":

> "Have you had multiple face-to-face conversations over multiple months with the employee where you have clearly explained and agreed there is a gap in performance, and where you have agreed to specific measurable actions to address that gap?"

If the answer is no to any of those clauses — _multiple_, _face-to-face_, _multiple months_, _clearly explained_, _agreed_, _specific measurable_ — you are not yet in performance management, you are still in coaching. The wrong reflex is to skip ahead.

Earlier signals worth watching:

- A drift downward in the quality of work, not a single bad output.
- Missed commitments that they used to hit, with explanations that get less convincing each time.
- Peers and reports starting to route around them, or your own "Spidey-sense" lighting up (Lopp's term for the unstructured intuition you should not dismiss).
- Repeated, soft escalations from peers ("they're great, but…") that are testing whether you have noticed.
- Your own avoidance — you are scheduling around their 1:1 or rehearsing the conversation in your head.

## First Principles

- **The reality is that you are always managing performance.** Lopp: "Your very existence as a leader sets a performance bar. How you act, what you say, how you treat others, how you work, all of your attributes influence how your team performs."
- **Do not let yourself say or think "performance management."** Lopp's hardest piece of advice. The moment you do, the relationship becomes structured, unnatural, and your demeanor flips to "Grim Reaper," which the employee will hear as "I should be looking for a new job" instead of "I need to change this."
- **Almost everything is coachable.** Lopp: "The vast majority of the situations surrounding performance are coachable. The work is complex, uncomfortable, time-consuming, and often hard to measure, but it is during these hard conversations that you become a better communicator."
- **Months, not weeks.** Substantive behavioral change takes months. "Rushing it" — one bad conversation followed by escalation — is the classic entry into formal performance management.
- **The employee must agree there is a gap.** If they don't, no action will follow. The first job is mutual diagnosis, not corrective action.

## Step-by-Step Approach

1. **Write the gap down before you say it.** A clear written sentence forces you to test whether the gap is real, specific, and observable. If you cannot write it, you are not ready to talk about it.
2. **Have the first conversation in a regular 1:1, framed as coaching, not warning.** Demeanor matters: this is "we have a problem we are working on together," not "you have a problem and I am noting it."
3. **Get explicit agreement that the gap exists.** Lopp: "If the employee doesn't agree with your description of the situation, they aren't going to act." Ask: "Is it clear what I'm describing? Did that make sense? Do you agree with my assessment?"
4. **If they disagree, treat that as the start of a conversation, not the end.** Ask: "What wasn't clear? What did you hear me say? What data do I not have? How do our facts differ?"
5. **Agree on a specific, measurable action they will take.** Vague commitments to "do better" do not survive contact with next week.
6. **Have the second conversation 2–4 weeks later, face-to-face.** What happened? What did we learn? Does the action need to be adjusted?
7. **Have the third conversation 2–4 weeks after that.** Pattern check: changing, partially changing, or unchanged?
8. **If after three conversations the message has not landed, write it down.** Lopp: "deliver it in a printed-out form in your next 1:1." The written word removes interpretive drift. This is still coaching, not formal process — but it is the bright line before it.
9. **Only after that, with HR, move to formal performance process** — and at that point, nothing in the formal write-up should be a surprise to the employee.

## Suggested Language

- **Opening the first conversation:** "I want to talk about something I've been noticing. It's not a crisis; it's a pattern I want us to address before it becomes one. Specifically, in [recent situation], I observed [behavior]. Here's the impact I'm worried about: [consequence]."
- **Getting agreement on the gap:** "Is it clear what I'm describing? Do you agree this is what's happening?"
- **When they disagree:** "What did you hear me say? What data do I not have? Where do our reads of this differ?"
- **Naming the next action:** "Here's what I'd like us to try over the next three weeks: [specific action]. Let's check in on it in our 1:1 on [date]."
- **Refusing the Grim Reaper frame:** "I want to be clear: this is not a formal process. I'm bringing this up because I want to fix it, not because I'm building a case."
- **At the second conversation:** "How do you think the last three weeks went? Here's what I observed. Where does that match your read?"
- **When percolation is needed:** "Let's agree to table this discussion for today. Give ourselves a week to let the conversation percolate and pick it up next week in our 1:1. We're not on a timetable."
- **Escalating to written feedback (Lopp's pre-formal step):** "I've put what we've been talking about into writing. I want us to walk through it together so there's no ambiguity. This is still us trying to fix it — but I want both of us to be clear about what I'm seeing."

## What To Avoid

- **Saying or thinking "performance management" out loud.** It changes the conversation in ways you cannot reverse in one meeting.
- **Skipping to escalation after one bad conversation.** "Rushing it" is the classic failure mode.
- **Delivering the feedback by email or Slack.** Lopp: "You just sent them an email with no opportunity to debate and discuss." You also cannot see how it lands.
- **Vague feedback.** "Be more strategic," "show more leadership," "have more presence" are not actionable. Name the behavior and the situation.
- **Stacking the conversation onto a status check.** Give it its own meeting or a dedicated 1:1 segment.
- **Treating disagreement as defiance.** If they push back, that is information — either the gap is real and the framing is wrong, or the gap is not what you thought.
- **Skipping the agreement step and going straight to corrective action.** Action without agreement is theater.
- **Letting empathy delay the first conversation.** "They're new" / "they're going through something" / "we're doing reviews next month" are all forms of the same avoidance.

## Escalation Path

1. **Coaching conversations** — three minimum, face-to-face, across at least 6–12 weeks.
2. **Written feedback in 1:1** (Lopp's intermediate step) — the bright line before formal process.
3. **Formal performance improvement plan** — designed with HR, with specific written measurable expectations and a defined timeline. By this point, nothing should be a surprise to the employee.
4. **Separation** — if the plan does not work, or if the conduct is egregious from the start (stealing, harassment, repeated dishonesty), separate quickly and cleanly. Lopp: "if they are stealing from you, you let them go."

If at any point you cross from coaching into formal process, do it explicitly and in writing. The employee should never wonder whether the conversation they are in is "the formal one."

## Follow-Up Actions

- After each conversation, write a short private note: what did you say, what did they say, what was committed.
- Maintain a "Spidey-sense" list (Lopp's term): a private record of low-grade signals you are not yet ready to act on. Many resolve themselves; the ones that don't are easier to address when you have a trail.
- If the situation resolves, name the improvement explicitly: "Three months ago we talked about [X]. I want to acknowledge what's changed."
- If it does not resolve, debrief yourself: what would you do differently next time? Did you wait too long? Did you skip a step? Was the role itself wrong for the person?
