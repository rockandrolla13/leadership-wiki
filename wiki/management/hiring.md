# Hiring

## Use Case

When building or growing a team. The single highest-leverage activity a manager can do. The quality of the team determines the quality of everything the team produces. Hiring deserves 50% of available manager time when reqs are open.

## Diagnosis

You have a hiring problem when:
- Open roles stay open for more than 60 days without meaningful progress
- You are filling roles reactively (backfilling attrition) rather than proactively (building toward strategy)
- Your interview process does not produce consistent signal about candidates
- You are hiring primarily for skills, not for cultural fit

## First Principles

1. **Hiring is the most important work a manager does.** The people you hire define the culture, the quality of work, and what the team can accomplish. Spending 50% of your time on this when you have open reqs is not excessive — it is correct.
2. **Culture is carried by the people.** You cannot impose culture through memos or values statements. Culture lives in the humans doing the work. Hiring for cultural fit is therefore building the culture.
3. **Most hires come from networks, not postings.** The best candidates are typically already employed and not browsing job boards. You reach them through direct relationships.
4. **Discover → Understand → Delight.** Three phases of candidate experience. Most hiring effort concentrates on Understand (evaluation). The leverage is in Discover (finding the right people) and Delight (making them want to join).

## Step-by-Step Approach

**Discover Phase**

1. Build and maintain a Must List — every person you've worked with whom you'd want to work with again. Name, company, role, why they're on the list. Review monthly regardless of whether reqs are open.

2. Contact Must List candidates when a relevant req opens: "Hi. Got a gig and I must work with you again. Coffee?" Most won't be available. That's fine. Maintain the relationship.

3. During Discover: focus your effort on candidates you have worked with directly. Their output is known. The risk is different from a stranger's.

**Understand Phase**

4. Ensure every candidate who interviews can answer three questions from your team: What is our mission? What do we value? Where are we headed? These should not be an accident of whichever interviewer was scheduled — they should be intentional.

5. Assign one interviewer the explicit job of telling the team story. Not: "Do you have any questions?" but an actual narrative about the team, the work, and the culture.

6. Evaluate the candidate both on skills and on whether they can champion your team's beliefs and values.

**Delight Phase**

7. Know what will make this candidate say yes. Compensation is one factor; the work, the team, the mission are others. Address the actual motivations.

8. Move quickly. Best candidates have multiple offers. Decisiveness signals respect.

**Ongoing**

9. Spend time on recruiting-related activities every day — sourcing, networking, reference calls, process review. This applies even when no reqs are open (network maintenance) and caps at 50% when reqs are active.

## Suggested Language

- **Reaching out to a Must List candidate:** "Hi [Name]. How are you? I've got a gig opening up that I think could be a great fit. I'd love to catch up regardless — coffee this week?"
- **When interviewing for culture fit:** "Before we get into technical questions, can you tell me what drew you to this company specifically? What do you understand about what we're trying to do?"
- **When closing a difficult candidate:** "I want to understand what would make this an easy yes. Let's talk about what matters most to you in your next role."

## What To Avoid

- **Hiring for skills alone.** A candidate who has the skills but doesn't believe what you believe will not compound over time. Cultural misfit creates drag, conflict, and eventual attrition.
- **Outsourcing discovery entirely to recruiting.** Sourcers fill the funnel. But you have unique knowledge of what this team needs and who can thrive in it. That knowledge must inform the pipeline.
- **Letting the Must List go stale.** If you haven't spoken to someone in 90 days, they've drifted. Relationships decay without maintenance.
- **Slowness.** A slow hiring process loses candidates and signals organizational dysfunction. The best candidates will not wait three months for a decision.
- **The lazy close question.** "Do you have any questions for me?" at the end of an interview is a missed opportunity to tell the team's story and differentiate the role.

## Escalation Path

If a critical hire is failing after 60+ days: audit the funnel. Where are candidates dropping? Is the problem discovery (insufficient candidates), evaluation (too many filtered out), or closing (candidates choosing elsewhere)? Address the specific failure point rather than adding general urgency.

## Follow-Up Actions

- Update the Must List after every conversation with a former colleague (status, date, notes)
- Review recruiting funnel metrics monthly: applications → screens → interviews → offers → hires
- After each hire: what did we learn about the process that worked or didn't?
- Onboard with the same intention as you hired: the first 90 days set the pattern

## Sources

Lopp (2020): Hiring is the most important work a manager can do. The best engineering manager Lopp worked with built her reputation with two hires — both people he had described as unattainable. The mechanism was simple: spending time on recruiting every single day. The **Must List** is a core tool: a maintained spreadsheet of every person you'd want to work with again, reviewed monthly, contacted at 90-day intervals. The three phases of the engineering recruiting pipeline — **Discover, Understand, Delight** — are designed to put the candidate's evolving mindset at the center rather than the standard funnel metrics.

Lopp on culture and the hire:

> "The simple fact is that culture is built and cared for by the humans who do the work. Your ability to shape the culture is a function of your ability to hire a diverse set of humans who are going to add to that culture."

Lopp's specific structural fix for the "do you have any questions for me?" failure mode: explicitly assign one interviewer the job of telling the team's story.

> "Your responsibility is to make sure the candidates understand your mission, culture, and values. While they will organically pick up some of this during interviews, you need to make sure it's one person's responsibility to clearly tell the engineering story."

Sinek (2009, 2017): Adds the WHY-fit filter to skills evaluation. The hiring question is not only *"can they do the job?"* but *"do they believe what we believe?"* People hired for skills alone may execute well in the short term; people hired for WHY-alignment compound. Sinek's recommended interview probe: *"Tell me about a time in your career when you felt most aligned — like the work genuinely mattered. What made it feel that way?"* The answer reveals their WHY; you assess alignment with your team's WHY (see [[principles/purpose-and-why]]).

### HBR Manager's Handbook on Craft, Behavioral Interviewing, and Retention

[[sources/hbr-managers-handbook-17-skills]] — the Handbook's chapter on Hiring (Skill 14) adds three structural disciplines that complement Lopp's pipeline-and-relationships framing:

1. **Craft the role before you write the JD.** Most JDs are wishlists. The Handbook's discipline is to write a one-page role definition: the outcome the role exists to produce, the three most important behaviors, the constraints on the role, and the explicit non-goals. From this you derive the JD, the interview rubric, and the success criteria for the first 90 days. Most bad hires are bad role definitions.

2. **Behavioral interviewing as the default structure.** "The single best predictor of future behavior is past behavior in similar situations." Replace hypothetical questions ("how would you handle X?") with behavioral ones ("tell me about a time when you faced X. What did you do? What was the result?"). Each behavioral answer should produce specific situation, action, and result. Vague answers are signal; press for the next layer of specificity.

3. **Retention is part of hiring.** A hire who leaves in 12 months is a recruiting failure, not a "fit" failure. The Handbook prescribes that the interview process include a candid conversation about the role's downsides, the team's known weaknesses, and the realistic 1-2-3 year trajectory. Candidates who join with accurate expectations stay.

The Handbook also names the **motivation and engagement** loop explicitly: hiring without a plan for what motivates the hired person produces silent disengagement within the first year. Connect hiring to career-development practices ([[coaching/career-development]]) from the first day.
