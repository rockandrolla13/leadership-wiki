# SBI Feedback Model

## Purpose

The SBI Model — Situation, Behavior, Impact — is a structured way to deliver feedback that keeps the conversation focused on observable behavior and its consequences rather than on character or assumed intent. Originally developed by the Center for Creative Leadership and codified in the HBR Manager's Handbook, SBI is the default feedback structure across most modern leadership programs because it produces a concrete, defensible, change-oriented message in three sentences.

## Core Idea

Most feedback fails because it conflates three things: what happened, what the person did, and what the consequence was. The receiver hears "you're aggressive in meetings" and has no way to engage — they cannot remember which meeting, do not recognize the behavior, and disagree with the character label. SBI separates the three:

- **S** describes the specific *situation* — when and where.
- **B** describes the observable *behavior* — what they actually said or did, not your interpretation of why.
- **I** describes the *impact* — on you, on others, on the work.

The structure works because it gives the receiver something to engage with at each step. They can correct the situation, verify the behavior, or refute the impact. Character labels offer no foothold.

## Steps (How to Deliver SBI Feedback)

1. **Pick a situation.** Pick one specific instance, not a pattern. "In yesterday's product review at 2 PM…" — concrete enough that they can remember.
2. **Describe the behavior.** Use *observable* language. "You interrupted the engineer twice during her update." Not "you were dismissive" (interpretation) or "you don't respect engineers" (character).
3. **Name the impact.** What did the behavior produce in the world? "The engineer stopped contributing for the rest of the meeting; I had to follow up with her separately to get the data we needed." Make it specific — to the work, to the team, to the speaker.
4. **Pause.** Do not append a request or a solution. Let the feedback land. The receiver may engage with the situation, the behavior, or the impact. All three are valid entry points.
5. **Listen to the response.** They may have context that changes your interpretation (the engineer asked them to interrupt earlier; they had time pressure they didn't communicate). Their context updates your model; your feedback updates theirs.
6. **Collaborate on the change (only if appropriate).** SBI is not a directive structure. Once the feedback has landed, you can move to "what would you do differently?" or "how can I help you next time?" — but only after the SBI has been received.

## When To Use

- Real-time feedback after a specific event. SBI is built for fresh, single-incident feedback.
- Coaching conversations where the report has not seen the impact of their behavior themselves.
- Peer-to-peer feedback in high-trust teams.
- 1:1 settings, especially when the feedback is corrective. SBI works in praise too (situation: yesterday's all-hands; behavior: you opened with the team's contribution; impact: three team members told me they felt seen).

## When Not To Use

- For systemic, pattern-level feedback that spans many incidents. SBI works on incidents; patterns need [[management/performance-management]]'s longer conversation.
- For values or character-based feedback. SBI is about behavior; if the issue is values, name it directly as a values conversation.
- For public delivery. SBI is a 1:1 structure. Public SBI is shaming.
- For feedback you are not sure about. If you cannot describe the *observable* behavior, you may be working from interpretation. Wait until you can.

## Example Application

A senior engineer has been telling the manager that meetings with the product manager are "frustrating." The manager has observed three product reviews in the past two weeks and gives this feedback to the senior engineer in their next 1:1:

> *Situation:* "In the product review on Tuesday — the one about the auth rewrite —"
>
> *Behavior:* "When [PM] asked about the timeline, you said, 'That's a product problem; I'm not going to answer that.' Twice in the same meeting."
>
> *Impact:* "[PM] stopped asking questions, the meeting ended without the timeline we needed, and I had to schedule a separate session to get it. I also heard from two of your reports that they noticed the exchange and were unsure what to make of it."

Pause. The engineer responds: "I was frustrated because [PM] keeps treating engineering capacity as infinitely flexible." That is useful context — the underlying issue is real, but the behavior is corrosive. The manager and engineer then collaborate on a different way to surface the capacity issue.

Counter-example of *bad* feedback for the same situation: "You're being difficult with [PM] and it's hurting the team." The engineer has no entry point: which meeting? what specifically? what is *difficult*? They will defend rather than learn.

## Failure Modes

- **Slipping from behavior to interpretation.** "You were dismissive" sneaks character in under a behavior label. The fix: "you said [exact words] / did [exact action]."
- **Skipping situation.** "You interrupt people" without an anchor — the receiver cannot place it. Always anchor.
- **Inflating impact.** "You destroyed the meeting" overstates and invites rebuttal. Stay specific: "the engineer stopped contributing for the rest of the meeting."
- **Stacking multiple SBIs at once.** "And another time, when you…" The receiver cannot process three incidents simultaneously. One at a time.
- **Following SBI with an immediate prescription.** "So you need to stop interrupting." The prescription pre-empts engagement. Pause, listen, then collaborate.
- **Using SBI as a script rather than a structure.** Mechanical delivery feels artificial. The structure is in your head; the language is yours.

## Adaptation For This User

For the wiki's user-archetype (senior IC moving to manager), SBI is most useful for two specific contexts: (1) giving feedback to a former peer who is now your direct report — the relationship is the most fragile and SBI's structure protects it; (2) receiving feedback yourself — the same structure helps you ask for what you actually need ("can you give me a specific situation, behavior, and impact?").

## Diagnostic Questions

- When I last gave critical feedback, can I name the situation, the behavior, and the impact in three concrete sentences? If not, my feedback was probably interpretive.
- When I have received vague feedback, did I ask the giver to anchor it with situation, behavior, and impact?
- Do I give SBI praise as well as SBI correction? (Most managers do not, which is a missed opportunity.)
- Is there a 360 report or comment about my style that I have not yet decomposed into SBI structure?
- When the receiver pushed back on my last piece of feedback, did they push back on the situation, the behavior, or the impact? Where did the model fail?

## Useful Scripts

- **Standard SBI delivery:** "In yesterday's product review at 2 PM, when [PM] asked about the timeline, you said 'that's a product problem.' The result was that [PM] stopped asking questions, we ended the meeting without the data we needed, and I had to follow up separately."
- **SBI for praise:** "In Wednesday's all-hands, when you opened with the team's contributions rather than your own, three people told me afterward they felt seen. That's the leadership move that builds the team."
- **Asking for SBI when receiving feedback:** "I want to make sure I can act on this. Can you give me a specific situation, the exact behavior you saw, and the impact it had?"
- **Pausing after SBI:** [silence] — the most underused tool. Let the receiver respond.
- **Closing into collaboration:** "What's your view of what happened? And what do you think a different move would have looked like?"
