# Onboarding

## Use Case

You have just hired someone — junior or senior, internal transfer or external — and they start in two weeks (or yesterday). This playbook covers the *first 90 days*: the window in which trust, role clarity, working norms, and a defensible understanding of the work are either established or quietly lost. Use it on top of, not instead of, the broader [[management/new-manager-transition|new manager transition]] playbook if the new hire is themselves a manager.

The single highest-leverage hiring activity is hiring well. The second-highest is onboarding well. Most organizations invest heavily in the first and reflexively in the second — then wonder why the offer-acceptance-to-resignation curve has a 12-month bump.

## Diagnosis

You are in this playbook if any of the following are true:

- A new hire (your own or one you've inherited) starts within the next 60 days.
- A hire who started 60–90 days ago feels like they "haven't quite landed" but you cannot point to a specific reason.
- You hear yourself thinking, "they'll figure it out from context" — they almost certainly will not.
- You delegated onboarding to "the team" with no named owner.
- The hire is more senior than you, or more credentialed in a domain, and you have not decided how to handle that.

## First Principles

1. **The first 90 days determine the next two years.** The HBR Manager's Handbook treats onboarding not as an HR ritual but as a *retention lever*: a person's perception of whether the role, the manager, and the organization match their expectations is largely set in the first quarter. After that, the cost of unwinding a bad start is high — for both sides. > "Your transition from individual contributor to manager is one of the biggest leaps you'll make in your career. As a manager, your success is no longer just about you — it's about what you accomplish through others." (HBR Manager's Handbook) The corollary for the manager doing the onboarding: their success in this role depends on whether they can land this hire well.

2. **Onboarding is a trust-building exercise, not a context-dump.** Trust is built through *repeated small acts of reliability* (see [[principles/trust]]), not through a wiki crawl in week one. The new hire is watching whether you show up to 1:1s, whether you follow through on commitments, whether you respond well when they bring you a problem. Every onboarding decision should be evaluated against: *does this build trust, or just transfer information?*

3. **Information should arrive when the hire can use it.** A new hire who is handed every doc on day one will read none of them. The same docs surfaced in week three, when the hire has questions the docs answer, are absorbed in an afternoon. *Pull beats push.*

4. **The ramp is owned by the manager, not by HR.** HR runs the corporate onboarding (benefits, compliance, accounts). The role onboarding — what good looks like, who they need to know, what the first delivered work is — belongs to the manager. If you do not own it, no one does, and the hire is left to assemble a ramp from rumor.

5. **Premature delegation is as damaging as no delegation.** Lopp's [[principles/delegation|delegation]] model assumes a trust substrate that does not yet exist on day one. Onboarding builds the substrate so that real delegation is possible by month three.

## Step-by-Step Approach

### Phase 0 — Before they start (the week before)

1. **Write the 30/60/90 plan, in writing, before day one.** A short doc (one page). For each milestone:
   - **30 days:** *Learn.* Who they should meet, what they should read, what they should observe. Not "produce." Their job at 30 days is to become someone who can ask good questions.
   - **60 days:** *Contribute.* One concrete piece of delivered work — not a stretch, but real. The point is to ship something so they experience the team's shipping mechanism end-to-end.
   - **90 days:** *Own.* A named area of responsibility, with [[organization/decision-rights|decision rights]] clearly stated. By day 90, they should be able to say: "I own X, and I make decisions A, B, C without checking with anyone."

2. **Pre-write a "How to Work With Me" doc** (Lopp's [[management/one-on-ones|"How to Rands"]] protocol applied as an onboarding tool). One page. Cover: how you run 1:1s, what surprises you don't want, where you are slow vs. fast, your communication channels, your blind spots. Share it on day one. Then ask them to write theirs by week three.

3. **Schedule the first month of 1:1s before they arrive.** Weekly, 30 minutes, recurring. Never cancel one in the first 60 days. Lopp is explicit: *"We'll have a 1:1 every week for at least 30 minutes, except during HIGH ALERT."*

4. **Choose an onboarding buddy who is not you.** Someone on the team who has been around 18+ months. Their job is to answer the questions the hire is too embarrassed to ask the manager.

5. **Identify the 10 people they need to know.** Five inside the team, five across functions. Pre-warm them: "X starts Monday. Can I put 30 minutes on your calendar with them in their second week?"

### Phase 1 — Week 1: Land, don't fly

6. **Day 1 is for landing, not learning.** Laptop works. Accounts work. Calendar is populated. You eat lunch with them. The team eats lunch with them. *Nothing else.* If the hire goes home on day one thinking "I don't think they were expecting me," you have already lost ground.

7. **In the first 1:1, do not deliver context.** Read the room. Ask: "What attracted you to this role, and what are you most worried about?" Listen for what they did not say in interviews. This is the moment to surface anxieties before they harden into stories.

8. **Set the explicit expectation that the first 30 days are for questions, not output.** Many hires — especially senior ones — feel internal pressure to "prove value" in week one. They will start optimizing for visible activity rather than understanding. Naming this is the only thing that prevents it.

### Phase 2 — Weeks 2–4: The pull phase

9. **Run the introduction tour.** The 10 people you pre-warmed. The hire books the meetings; you provide the list and the warm intro language. They surface their own questions. You stay out of the meetings.

10. **Surface a Blue Tape List.** Lopp's [[management/new-manager-transition|Blue Tape List]] applies *to the hire*, not just to new managers. Encourage them to keep a list of everything that looks broken from a fresh-eyes perspective. Do not let them fix anything yet — the list itself is the gift. Review it together at day 30. Many items will resolve with context. The remainder are real and you would not have seen them.

11. **Open the team's documents to them with a guide.** Not "here's the Notion, dive in." Instead: "here are the five docs to read this week, in this order, and here is what you should look for in each."

### Phase 3 — Days 30–60: First real delivery

12. **Give them the 60-day deliverable.** Pick something with these properties: it ships in 3–4 weeks, it requires them to work with at least three other people, and it has a visible outcome (good or bad). The point is not the output — it is the experience of shipping in your environment.

13. **Resist the urge to over-help.** When they get stuck, coach (see [[principles/delegation]]: "Walk me through where things stand. What's working? What's getting hard? Have you seen a situation like this before?"). Do not solve. The B-grade outcome is the right outcome — they have learned the shipping mechanism.

14. **Begin shifting 1:1 content.** Weeks 1–4 are anxiety + context. Weeks 5–8 are work + coaching. By week 8 the 1:1 should look like a normal 1:1, not an onboarding meeting.

### Phase 4 — Days 60–90: Hand over the keys

15. **At day 60, name the area of ownership.** "Starting now, X is yours. You make decisions A, B, C without checking with me. You consult me on D. You inform me on E." This is a [[organization/decision-rights|decision rights]] conversation, not a vague handoff.

16. **At day 90, run a structured retrospective.** Two questions: *"What surprised you?"* and *"What would have made the first 90 days work better?"* Capture the answers. They become the next hire's onboarding plan.

17. **At day 90, schedule a career conversation.** Separate from 1:1s, separate from performance. *"Where do you want to be in three years, and what do you need from me to get there?"* (HBR Manager's Handbook prompt.) This signals: you are here for the long arc, not just the ramp.

## Suggested Language

- **First 1:1 (day 1 or 2):** "I'm glad you're here. Before I tell you anything about the role, I want to hear from you. What attracted you to this work? What did you hear in interviews that you want to verify? What's worrying you that you didn't ask about in the offer stage?"

- **Setting the expectation that learning is the job:** "Your job for the next 30 days is to ask great questions. Output comes later. If anyone — including me — pressures you to produce in the next month, tell me, and I will handle it."

- **Sharing the "How to Work With Me" doc:** "Here's a one-page guide to how I work — how I run 1:1s, where I'm slow, where I'm fast, what surprises I don't want. Read it this week. By week three I'd like one from you. We'll compare notes."

- **Handing over a Blue Tape List:** "Keep a running list of everything that looks broken or weird as you come in fresh. Don't act on it yet — just write it down. We'll review it in a month. Most items will resolve as you learn context. The rest are the real gifts of a fresh perspective."

- **At day 60, transferring ownership:** "Starting now, X is yours. You make decisions A, B, C without me. You consult me on D. You only need to inform me on E. If I find myself making a decision in your area, I want you to call it out."

- **At day 90, the retrospective:** "I want to learn from your first 90 days, not just check on them. What surprised you about how this team works? What would have made these 90 days easier? What do you wish I had told you on day one?"

## Onboarding Someone Skeptical or Overqualified

This is a specific edge case worth naming, because the default playbook quietly fails for it.

A skeptical hire is one who took the role with reservations — about the company, the strategy, or you. An overqualified hire is one who has done a version of this job before and is alert to "explanations" that sound like patronizing context. Both will tune out a generic ramp.

- **Name it directly in the first 1:1.** *"You've done this before. I am not going to walk you through things you already know. Tell me what you actually need from me, and what you'd like to figure out yourself."* The signal that you respect their experience is more important than the actual onboarding mechanics.

- **Make the first delivered work substantive, not symbolic.** A senior hire on a trivial first project reads it as a status test. Give them real responsibility early — calibrated, not careless. If they are not ready, *that* is the data you need.

- **Surface disagreement as a feature, not a problem.** *"You're going to see things I don't see. I'd rather hear your skepticism now than absorb it as resistance in month four. When you disagree, I want it on the table."* Lopp: *"Disagreement is feedback, and the sooner we learn how to efficiently disagree with each other, the sooner (and more) we'll trust and respect each other."*

- **Beware of over-onboarding as a control move.** A common failure pattern: insecure managers schedule heavy onboarding for senior hires as a way of asserting authority. The hire reads it instantly. Lean light. Lean substantive.

## What To Avoid

- **Overloading with context before trust is established.** Sending the hire 40 docs in week one teaches them the team values *appearing* informed over *being* informed. They will not read most of it; they will simply learn to nod.

- **Delegating onboarding to "the team."** Without a named owner, the hire is onboarded by accident. By week three they will have learned a version of the team that no one would have chosen to teach them.

- **Skipping the buddy.** The questions the hire will not ask you are the most important questions for them to get answered. The buddy exists to absorb those.

- **Cancelling 1:1s in month one.** Trust is built by reliability. The first cancellation in the first month carries a multiplier; it teaches the hire that meetings with you are negotiable.

- **Holding back authority "until they prove themselves."** Half-delegation (see [[principles/delegation]]) is the predictable damage pattern. Either give them the authority to do the work or do not assign the work. The middle is corrosive.

- **Treating the 30/60/90 plan as a checklist.** It is a *conversation prompt*. The plan changes by week two. The artifact is the conversation, not the document.

- **Ignoring the hire's existing identity.** A senior hire arriving from another company is grieving the team they left and the identity they had there. That grief shapes their first 60 days. Naming it is a generous and accurate move.

## Escalation Path

- **If the hire is disengaging in weeks 3–6:** This is the most dangerous window — early enough that they will leave, late enough that you have stopped paying close attention. Run a structured 1:1 explicitly about the onboarding. *"How is this matching what you expected? Where is the gap?"* Often this is repairable. Once.

- **If the hire and the team are not gelling by day 60:** Diagnose. Is it the [[organization/informal-organization|informal organization]] rejecting them? Is it a skills mismatch? Is it a style clash? Each has a different intervention. Do not wait for performance-management timelines to address it — onboarding-stage disengagement is a different problem than [[management/underperformance|underperformance]].

- **If you discover by day 30 that the hire is wrong for the role:** Name it. Ninety days is short; six months of pretense is long. Have the honest conversation. The cost of acting at day 30 is far lower than at day 180.

- **If the hire is more senior than you and the dynamic is becoming uncomfortable:** This is a [[principles/influence|influence]] problem, not an authority problem. You will not establish authority by asserting it. You establish it by being reliably useful to them and by being explicit about which decisions are yours and which are theirs.

## Follow-Up Actions

- **At day 30, day 60, day 90:** Run the structured check-ins above. Do not skip the day-90 retrospective.
- **At month 4 and month 6:** Confirm the hire has the relationships, the ownership, and the [[organization/decision-rights|decision rights]] that you committed to in the 30/60/90 plan. If not, that is *your* gap, not theirs.
- **At month 12:** Career conversation, separate from performance. The retention question is decided here.
- **Track the cohort:** Across all hires you onboard, what is the pattern at day 60? At day 180? At year one? Patterns surface that no single hire reveals.

## Diagnostic Questions

- Is this person voluntarily bringing me problems before they become crises? If not, [[principles/trust|trust]] has not formed yet.
- At day 30, can they name three colleagues they would call for help on three different topics? If not, the network-building piece of onboarding has not happened.
- At day 60, have they shipped something real with a visible outcome? If not, they have not yet experienced the team's shipping mechanism.
- At day 90, can they describe their area of ownership in one sentence, including the decisions they make alone? If not, the [[organization/decision-rights|decision-rights]] handoff has not happened.
- Are they finishing the day at a sustainable energy level, or burning the candle to compensate for confusion? Compensatory effort is a leading indicator of attrition.
- Am I cancelling their 1:1s under pressure? If yes, I am training them that they are not the priority.

## Open Questions

- How does this playbook adapt to remote-first or async teams where the "eat lunch together" anchor of week one is unavailable? The mechanisms are different but the trust-building function is the same.
- Does the 30/60/90 framing hold for very junior hires (first job out of school) whose ramp is closer to 6–9 months?
- For hires into a team in active crisis, the "observe before acting" first 30 days may be a luxury the team cannot afford. What is the abbreviated playbook?
