# Decision Rights

## Definition

Decision rights are the explicit allocation of *who decides what, with what authority, on what timeline, and by what process*. They describe — in advance, and ideally in writing — the formal answer to the question "whose call is this?" for every decision class that matters.

Decision rights are distinct from accountability. *Accountability* is who owns the outcome and must explain it ([[principles/accountability]]). *Decision rights* are who has the authority to make the call that produces the outcome. The two should align, but in dysfunctional organizations they routinely do not.

## Why It Matters

Unclear decision rights are one of the highest-cost ambiguities in any organization, and they hide in plain sight. A team that cannot answer the question "who decides X?" loses time in every meeting about X, makes worse decisions because the people with real information are not the people with formal authority, and produces silent resentment because rights that were assumed turn out to belong to someone else.

The HBR Manager's Handbook puts it concretely: > *"Before any meeting, write the decision you want made by the end. If you cannot, the meeting is a discussion, not a decision — set expectations accordingly."* The same logic applies to every project: if you cannot name who will decide, the project will accumulate political friction until decision rights are clarified by force, by accident, or by failure.

Katzenbach and Khan add the structural diagnosis: every organization has both a *formal* and an *informal* structure of authority. The formal structure is the one written in the org chart and policy documents; the informal structure is who actually gets listened to. > *"The informal isn't as easily defined as the formal… its elements often overlap and don't follow the clean principles of 'mutually exclusive, comprehensively exhaustive' that analytical thinkers prefer."* When formal and informal decision authority diverge, *informal* wins — but the cost is paid in confusion, rework, and political friction.

Decision rights are the formal mechanism that, when used well, allows the informal organization to align rather than fight the structure.

## When It Applies

Decision rights matter most — and are most often unclear — when:

- **Cross-functional projects** that span functions, organizations, or geographies. (See [[management/cross-functional-teaming]].) These are the predictable failure cases.
- **Two-way-door decisions** that can be reversed cheaply: low-cost, high-velocity decisions should be pushed to the person closest to the work.
- **One-way-door decisions** that are expensive to reverse: hiring above a certain level, architecture choices, large capital commitments. These warrant a more formal process and more senior involvement.
- **Recurring decision classes** that come up repeatedly: pricing exceptions, hiring approvals, scope changes. Naming them once saves a meeting every time they recur.
- **Moments of organizational change** — restructures, new platform launches, geographic expansion — where prior decision rights have become obsolete but new ones have not yet been articulated.
- **New manager / new hire transitions**, where the prior incumbent's de facto authority disappears and the successor has not been formally granted it.

A working heuristic: if you have had the same conversation about who decides X more than twice, you have a decision-rights problem, not a project problem.

## Key Principles

- **Ambiguity is the cost.** The cost of unclear decision rights is not the bad decision — it is the *meeting about the meeting*, the political negotiation about authority, and the loss of organizational trust as people watch the formal process bypassed.
- **Decisions should be made by the person closest to the relevant information, not the highest title.** Captain Marquet's "push authority where the information is" is the underlying principle. Senior leaders who hoard decisions starve them of information.
- **Different decision classes deserve different processes.** Strategic decisions warrant deliberation; operational decisions warrant speed. Treating them the same is the failure mode in both directions — slow operational decisions throttle the team; fast strategic decisions are reckless.
- **Authority must be paired with accountability.** The decider must also be the one who owns the outcome. Splitting them — one person decides, another absorbs the consequences — guarantees finger-pointing and erodes trust.
- **Decision rights need a publication mechanism.** Until they are written down and visible, they do not exist. The team must be able to look up "who decides X" without having to ask.
- **Decision rights compound with [[organization/operating-rhythm|operating rhythm]].** A decision class that comes up monthly should be slotted into the monthly forum; a decision class that comes up quarterly belongs in the quarterly review. Decision rights without rhythm produce ad-hoc politicking.

## Framework: RAPID

The RAPID framework (originally Bain & Company; widely cited in the HBR Manager's Handbook lineage) assigns five roles to each significant decision:

- **R — Recommend.** Develops the proposal, gathers data, frames the choice. Often more important than the decider — they shape the option set.
- **A — Agree.** Has veto power on specific dimensions (e.g., Legal on contract terms, Finance on capital). Their formal sign-off is required, but they are not the primary decider.
- **P — Perform.** Will execute the decision once made. Their input on feasibility is required.
- **I — Input.** Has perspective worth hearing but no veto and no execution responsibility. *Consulted, not decisional.*
- **D — Decide.** The single person whose call it is. RAPID is explicit that there must be exactly one *D* per decision.

The crucial discipline: the **D** is one person. Multiple D's is the most common RAPID failure mode and produces decision paralysis. If two people both think they decide, the decision will be either contested or quietly remade by whoever moves first.

## Framework: RACI

RACI is the most commonly used decision-rights framework and the one most often misapplied. The four roles, per the HBR Manager's Handbook:

- **R — Responsible.** Does the work. Can be multiple people.
- **A — Accountable.** Owns the outcome. *Exactly one person.*
- **C — Consulted.** Has input before the decision. Two-way communication.
- **I — Informed.** Told after the decision. One-way communication.

RAPID is more precise about the act of *deciding*; RACI is more precise about the act of *executing*. The two are complementary. For high-stakes decisions, use RAPID; for execution clarity on the work that follows, use RACI.

The most common RACI failures:
- Multiple A's (everyone owns it, no one owns it).
- C and I are confused (people who should have been *consulted* are merely *informed*, and they resent it).
- The chart is built but never referenced afterward — it becomes a one-time artifact rather than a living governance tool.

## When Unclear Decision Rights Cause the Most Damage

Unclear decision rights are background friction most of the time and acute pain in specific moments:

- **Cross-functional projects** are the canonical case. Edmondson's extreme-teaming work (see [[management/cross-functional-teaming]]) names *unclear authority across disciplinary boundaries* as one of the predictable failure patterns. Each function assumes its lead has authority over the interface; in practice neither does, and the work stalls.
- **Hiring decisions** where the hiring manager, the function head, the recruiter, and the skip-level all believe they have veto power. Result: 30-day decision processes for what should be a 5-day call.
- **Pricing or contract exceptions** where Sales, Finance, Legal, and the executive sponsor all believe themselves to be the decider. Result: customer-facing deals that take weeks to close while internal teams negotiate authority.
- **Reorganizations**, where the prior decision rights were tied to the old structure and the new ones have not been articulated. Result: a period of decision paralysis that often outlasts the reorg itself.
- **Crisis moments**, where the urgency exposes the absence of pre-articulated authority. Decisions get made by whoever speaks first or loudest — not necessarily by whoever has the right information.

The pattern: the more visible and urgent the decision, the higher the cost of decision-rights ambiguity.

## Practical Behaviors

- **Pre-write decisions before meetings.** Before any meeting intended to make a decision, write the decision you want made. If you cannot, the meeting is a discussion. Communicate that explicitly. (HBR Manager's Handbook.)
- **For each project, complete a one-page RAPID before kickoff.** Name the decision classes the project will face. For each, name the *one* D. Publish it.
- **When you delegate, name the decision rights explicitly.** Lopp's [[principles/delegation|delegation]] principles require an explicit transfer of authority, not just task. "You decide X without me. You consult me on Y. You inform me on Z."
- **Resist the urge to remake delegated decisions.** Once you have granted decision rights to a report and they have used them, do not override the decision unless it is genuinely high-stakes. Overriding teaches the team that delegation is provisional, which guarantees they will route every decision through you in the future.
- **Surface unclear decision rights early.** The first sign of a decision-rights problem is "we keep having the same conversation." Name it: *"I notice we keep coming back to this. Who actually decides? Let's settle it now."*
- **Distinguish input from veto.** People conflate them constantly. *Input* is "I want to hear your view before I decide." *Veto* is "I cannot decide without your agreement." Mixing them is the single most common source of cross-functional resentment.
- **Publish decision rights on a wiki page, not a slide.** Slides go stale; wiki pages get edited. The artifact must be discoverable when the question recurs.

## How to Clarify Decision Rights Without Triggering Political Resistance

Clarifying decision rights is often a [[organization/organizational-politics|political]] act because it *makes explicit* what was previously implicit — and the implicit allocation may have been working in someone's favor. A few moves that lower the political cost:

- **Frame it as an efficiency move, not an authority grab.** "We are spending too much time on the same conversation; let's settle it once" lands differently from "I am going to clarify who decides."
- **Lead with what stays the same.** Most decision-rights conversations are 80% confirmation of existing practice. Start with what is not changing.
- **Negotiate the most contested decision rights first, when energy is high.** If you defer the hard ones, the team will spend its political capital on the easy ones.
- **Use RAPID or RACI to depersonalize.** The framework is doing the work; you are merely applying it. This is a real and underused political move — formal frameworks reduce the felt presence of personal authority in the decision.
- **Get the *D* on record in writing.** Verbal agreements about decision rights decay within weeks; written ones survive personnel changes.
- **Acknowledge what is being given up.** If someone is losing decision authority they had previously, naming the loss is more durable than pretending it is not happening. *"You had been the de facto decider on this. We're moving that to X. I know that's a change. Here's why."*

## Common Failure Modes

- **Multiple D's.** The most common RAPID failure. If two people both believe they decide, neither actually does.
- **The unstated D.** No one is named. Whoever moves first decides; whoever is slower complains afterward.
- **Decision rights divorced from accountability.** One person decides; another absorbs the consequence. Predictable result: finger-pointing.
- **Decision rights divorced from information.** The decider is too senior or too removed from the work. They make the call without the data, and the team executes a decision they know to be wrong.
- **The RACI as theater.** A chart built once, filed, and never referenced. The clarity disappears the moment the chart is closed.
- **Veto inflation.** Too many people have veto. Anything that requires *everyone* to agree usually requires *no one* to commit. The decision shrinks to the lowest-common-denominator option that no one will block.
- **Authority hoarding.** A leader who keeps every decision in their inbox. Predictable result: a team that cannot move, and a leader who cannot scale. (See [[principles/delegation]].)
- **The "I just want input" misframe.** A senior leader says they want input, but in practice their preference dominates. The team learns that "input" means "guess what they already think." Either name the decider clearly or genuinely cede the decision; the middle is poison.

## Diagnostic Questions

- For our top three current initiatives, who is the single *D* on each major open decision? If I cannot name them, we have a decision-rights problem.
- When was the last time we re-litigated a decision that was supposedly made? What did that reveal about who actually decides?
- Are there decisions on my plate that I should not be making — that someone closer to the information should own? (See [[principles/delegation]].)
- Are there decisions on someone else's plate that I am quietly remaking? If yes, I am undermining the decision rights I supposedly granted.
- Does our team know where to find the answer to "who decides X?" without asking me? If no, decision rights are not yet operationalized.
- When veto rights are claimed by Function A on a decision in Function B's domain, is it actually veto, or is it [[principles/influence|influence]] dressed up as veto?
- In our last cross-functional failure, was the root cause an *execution* problem or an *authority* problem? Most "execution failures" on inspection are decision-rights failures.

## Useful Scripts

- **Surfacing an unclear decision in a meeting:** *"I want to pause for a moment. We have been talking about this for 25 minutes and I cannot tell who is going to decide. Who is the D on this? If we cannot name them, this is a discussion, not a decision, and we should plan to come back when we can."*

- **Granting decision rights as part of delegation:** *"Starting now, X is yours. You decide A, B, C without me. You consult me on D — meaning I want to hear your thinking before you commit, but the call is still yours. You inform me on E. If I find myself making a decision in your area, call it out — that's a signal we got something wrong."*

- **Reclaiming a misplaced decision:** *"I think we put this decision in the wrong place. The people with the information are [team]; the people deciding are [you and me]. I'd like to move the D to them. Are you okay with that?"*

- **Declining veto inflation:** *"I want your input on this, and I'll weight it heavily. But this is X's decision to make. I am not going to give it to a vote."*

- **Naming a decision-rights conflict:** *"It sounds like both of us think this is our call. Let's not litigate it in the moment — let's get clear on the principle. For this class of decision, whose call should it be, going forward?"*

- **Closing a decision explicitly:** *"To summarize: the decision is X. The D was [name]. Y and Z provided input. Effective from today. I'll post this in the project channel. If anyone disagrees with the decision, raise it now; once we walk out of the room, we are committed."*

## Source Notes

The RAPID framework is most thoroughly explored in the HBR Manager's Handbook ([[sources/hbr-managers-handbook-17-skills]]), which treats it as one of the manager's core operating tools. The Handbook's central injunction — *"Before any meeting, write the decision you want made by the end"* — is the simplest decision-rights diagnostic available.

The HBR Handbook also articulates the RACI framework in its strategy and project-management chapters, and is direct that the dominant failure is multiple A's: > *"Delegation is not abdication. It is the deliberate transfer of authority along with accountability — and the responsibility to support the person you've delegated to."* The transfer-with-accountability framing is exactly the decision-rights principle.

Katzenbach and Khan ([[sources/katzenbach-khan-leading-outside-lines-2010]]) provide the structural counterpoint: formal decision rights live alongside an *informal* structure of authority that is rarely written down. > *"Leading outside the lines is harder than managing within the formal lines, partly because that territory is less well defined."* The implication: decision rights that ignore the informal organization will be quietly overridden by it. The leader's job is to align the two so they reinforce rather than contradict each other.

Marquet's "push authority where the information is" (cited in the [[management/cross-functional-teaming]] page and via Sinek) is the deeper philosophical claim: senior leaders' instinct is to hoard decisions, and that instinct is structurally wrong because the information they need to decide well lives further down.

## Open Questions

- How do decision rights scale across organizational sizes? RAPID-style formality is appropriate at large enterprise scale and overkill at startup scale. What is the simplest viable artifact at each stage?
- The framework assumes a relatively low-conflict organization where formal decision rights are respected. In high-conflict or low-trust environments, how should leaders sequence the rebuild of decision rights?
- For decisions that span legal jurisdictions, regulatory regimes, or geographies, the *D* may legitimately be different in different places. How do leaders manage decision-rights variation without producing chaos?
- When informal authority structures persist after formal decision rights are clarified, what is the right intervention? Is it patience (informal will catch up to formal), enforcement (formal must be defended explicitly), or honesty (acknowledge that informal is winning and re-formalize accordingly)?
