# Adaptive Organization (Octopus Org)

## Purpose

Jana Werner and Phil Le-Brun's "Become an Octopus Organization" (HBR Nov–Dec 2025, adapted from their forthcoming HBR Press book) provides a model for organizational adaptability built around three principles — clarity, ownership, curiosity — and operationalized through a learning loop and a discipline of *spreading* (not scaling) successful practices. The framework is the explicit alternative to what they call the "Tin Man Org" — rigid, hierarchical, compliance-driven, slow to learn.

## Core Idea

Most companies are not stuck because of unique problems; they make the same predictable mistakes — "antipatterns" — that compromise clarity, undermine ownership, and stifle curiosity. The Octopus Org metaphor (intelligent, fluid, simultaneously observing the horizon while sensing the immediate environment with its tentacles) captures the model: leaders' job is to design the *system* that enables others to excel, not to direct individual decisions. Adaptation is institutionalized through small experiments, double-loop learning, and organic spread of successful practices.

## The Three Antipattern Categories

1. **Behaviors that compromise clarity.** Vague mission statements, abstract goals, hoarded information, message dilution through layers. Result: people lack context to make local decisions well.
2. **Behaviors that undermine ownership.** Treating people as "resources"; eliminating risk through compliance; micromanaging; failure-induced fear of ownership. The cited cost: $8.9 trillion annually in lost productivity (cited from Gallup-adjacent research).
3. **Behaviors that stifle curiosity.** Rewarding predictability; preaching curiosity while punishing dissent. One study cited: 73% of executives recognize curiosity as critical; only 9% of employees feel their leaders support it.

## The Learning Loop

Adaptive organizations institutionalize the cycle: **Hypothesize → Experiment → Reflect/Reframe → Spread**.

### 1. Hypothesize

Define a hypothesis and an experiment about an expected impact or learning. Drawing on environmental scientist Donella Meadows, group interventions into three levels:

- **Adjusting parameters.** Quick, inexpensive tweaks (e.g., reducing approvers for expenses from 11 to two). Visible but limited impact.
- **Tuning the system's engine.** Reshape feedback loops. Strengthen self-correcting balancing loops (customer complaints trigger root-cause analysis). Amplify reinforcing loops (recognition programs). Adjust information flow (live customer-pain feed visible in engineering bay).
- **Rewriting the organization's DNA.** Most transformative. Change rules (principle-based vs. bureaucratic). Reset goals (lifetime customer value vs. quarterly profit). Shift mental models (command-and-control to agency-and-trust). Cheap to introduce, slow to internalize.

### 2. Experiment

Three forms:

- **Stopping something.** Reed Hastings at Netflix eliminated the formal vacation policy and replaced it with "Act in Netflix's best interests." Removed an obstacle; demonstrated trust.
- **Deviating from an existing process.** Google's interview process required executive approval beyond four interviewers — forced decisiveness. Howard Behar moved Starbucks away from prescriptive barista manuals to principle-based guidance.
- **Piloting a new practice or tool.** Coca-Cola's Miriam McLemore shifted IT governance from gatekeepers to guardrails — adversarial "can we get this approved?" became collaborative "how will we get this done together?"

Calibrate timescale to the change. Daily meeting length: a week. Annual to dynamic budgeting: more than a year. Keep first experiments small. Have a "fire extinguisher" — budget reserve, backup team, prewritten "we tried something" memo.

### 3. Reflect and Reframe

Reject single-loop conclusions ("it worked; do it everywhere"). Practice *double-loop learning*: analyze the underlying assumptions and mental models that shaped the experiment. The U.S. Army's after-action review is the master class: don't just ask what happened — ask why a flawed decision seemed reasonable at the time, revealing the systemic issue (flawed intelligence, mis-prioritization) rather than the individual error.

### 4. Spread (not Scale)

The instinct to scale ("roll out this practice organization-wide") is itself a Tin Man move — it strips local ownership and treats teams as order-takers. Octopus Orgs *spread*: create conditions for successful practices to flow organically, pulled from team to team based on need.

Example: Amazon's Stephen Brozovich built a visual-search tool for product images twenty years ago because the command-line interface was wasting his time. The tool was pulled through the organization team by team. No mandate. No promotion. It worked, so it spread.

## When To Use

- When the organization has been doing the same change initiatives repeatedly without sustained behavior change.
- When leaders see good local practices that never propagate to other teams.
- When a top-down change program has stalled and the next move is being debated.
- When you are forming a new business unit and want to set its operating model from the start.

## When Not To Use

- In acute crisis. Adaptive organization is a steady-state pattern. Crisis demands directive leadership; restore stability, then re-introduce experimentation.
- In heavily regulated contexts without adjusting for compliance. Pharma, banking, aviation cannot let frontline teams experiment with safety-critical processes. Apply the model to non-safety-critical operations.
- As a slogan. "We're going to be more adaptive" is the antipattern; "we are running these three experiments this quarter" is the practice.

## Example Application

A 1,500-person SaaS company has been through three failed culture-change initiatives over five years. New CTO inherits a slow-moving engineering org. Diagnosis using the antipattern lens:

- *Compromised clarity:* "increase ARR 30%" but no team knows which customer segments matter.
- *Undermined ownership:* every code change requires four approvers; principal engineers are afraid to commit.
- *Stifled curiosity:* engineers cite "the way it's always been done" as the response to every improvement suggestion.

Three small experiments:
- *Adjusting parameter:* reduce approvers from four to one for routine changes. Track defects.
- *Tuning the engine:* live customer-pain feed on the engineering monitor. Track engineer-initiated bug fixes.
- *Rewriting DNA:* replace performance review's "individual delivery" metric with "team velocity + customer outcome." Track over 12 months.

Year one: defects flat, customer-initiated fixes up 4x, team velocity up 18%. The practices *spread* — other functions adopt the customer-pain feed without mandate. The CTO resists the board's instinct to scale the DNA-rewrite across the company; she lets it stay in engineering for another year while it stabilizes.

## Failure Modes

- **Scaling instead of spreading.** Mandating a successful local practice across the org strips its ownership and produces malicious compliance.
- **Single-loop reflection.** "It worked; do it again." Misses the deeper learning about *why* the conditions matched.
- **Experiments that are too big.** First experiments must be small enough to fail safely. Big experiments at first carry too much risk and produce defensive learning.
- **Leadership impatience.** "We've been doing this for three months — where are the results?" Octopus Orgs operate on multi-year horizons for DNA changes.
- **Antipattern-naming as accusation.** "You're a Tin Man manager" destroys the conversation. The antipatterns are conditions; the people inside them are doing the work of survival in those conditions.

## Adaptation For This User

For a senior leader inheriting an organization, the most useful first move is the antipattern audit. Listen for shrugs and eye rolls in meetings; interview departing employees; review recurring help-desk tickets. Once two or three antipatterns are identified, start with one *small* experiment in the area where ownership naturally gravitates — the person most passionate or the respected skeptic (Jessica Hall's "converting a critic" pattern).

## Diagnostic Questions (Antipattern Audit)

- *Clarity:* Can each of three randomly selected employees state the team's top three priorities in the same words? If not, mission and strategy are not landing.
- *Ownership:* When was the last time a team made a decision that turned out to be wrong, and what happened to them?
- *Curiosity:* When was the last time someone proposed something genuinely new and it was funded?
- *Antipattern recognition:* What recurring frustration shows up in surveys and help-desk tickets that the leadership team has not addressed?
- *Experiment readiness:* What is the smallest experiment we could run this month to test one of those antipatterns?

## Useful Scripts

- **Naming an antipattern:** "I want to test something. I'm seeing a pattern where [X]. Is that landing as I'm describing, or am I missing something?"
- **Proposing an experiment:** "Here's what I'd like to try: [small change], for [time-bound period], measuring [specific outcome]. What could go wrong, and how do we keep it bounded?"
- **Resisting premature scaling:** "Before we roll this out everywhere, let me ask — what was true about that team that made it work, and is that true in the next team?"
- **Inviting spreading:** "We learned something useful. I don't want to mandate it. I want to share it widely and see who finds it useful."
- **Holding double-loop:** "The result was [X]. Before we conclude, let's ask: what did we assume going in that we should question now?"
