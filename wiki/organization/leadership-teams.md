# Leadership Teams: Dysfunction Patterns and Repair

## Purpose

Most CEOs and executives report dissatisfaction with their leadership teams, but they cannot publicly say so. Keil & Zangrillo's 100+ interviews surface three recurring dysfunction patterns and the targeted repairs for each. This framework operationalises their typology so you can diagnose your own team, choose the right intervention, and avoid the common mistake of applying the wrong remedy to the wrong dysfunction.

## Core Idea

Senior teams are made of ambitious, competent people. The dysfunction is rarely a personnel issue at the individual level — it is an interaction-pattern problem. There are three patterns: too much competition (the *shark tank*), too little (the *petting zoo*), and neither competition nor collaboration in healthy form (the *mediocracy*). Each requires a different remedy. Confusing the patterns produces wrong moves: trying to "build psychological safety" in a shark tank just protects the rogue actors; trying to "encourage healthy debate" in a petting zoo without first establishing data-grounded dialogue feels like ambush.

## Steps

### Step 1 — Diagnose the pattern

#### Shark tank — too much competition

Signals:
- Members approach the CEO one-on-one to discuss things that should be in team meetings.
- Decisions made through power struggles outside meetings; the meeting itself ratifies what was already settled.
- Decision-making escalates to shouting or tug-of-war on relatively straightforward issues.
- Executives continue questioning plans after they've been made; resist implementing them unless forced.
- Bad-mouthing of colleagues; alliances against rivals; personal gain prioritised over collective.
- A small number of "rogue actors" who are visibly self-serving and aren't being checked.

Case: A Swiss bank where, after a new CEO was hired with mixed analyst reactions, several leadership team members gunned for the CEO position, badmouthed him internally, slowed his projects, and leaked to the press.

#### Petting zoo — too little competition / conflict

Signals:
- Smooth meetings with little to no real debate; proposals get automatic approval.
- Performance issues rarely discussed openly; focus on positive news.
- Reluctance to challenge the status quo or hold one another accountable.
- Members negotiate / horse-trade offline to avoid conflict in the room.
- A consensus norm that has calcified into mutual-forbearance.
- Pluralistic ignorance — privately, everyone is worried; publicly, nobody says so.

Case: A large European services company. Long-tenured team operating with strong camaraderie. All decisions by consensus. No debate. No accountability for missed goals. Couldn't meet growth/profit targets.

#### Mediocracy — neither healthy competition nor collaboration

Signals:
- Mismatch between what the team needs to do and what it can do.
- Silos; duplicated efforts; missed opportunities.
- Long periods of past success have produced complacency.
- Team that fit a previous era no longer fits the current one (stable environment team facing a turnaround; or vice versa).
- Group has split into two camps — some preferring competition, some preferring collaboration — and neither dominates productively.

Case: A European professional services firm in stagnation. New CEO took three months to assess; concluded "individuals were not strong enough… they didn't have the competencies… didn't have a sense of corporate purpose."

### Step 2 — Apply the targeted repair

#### Shark-tank repair

- **Identify and confront the source.** Often one or two rogue actors. Give them direction and coaching; if they don't change, remove them. Adel Al-Saleh at T-Systems removed disruptive executives early; the team became more productive.
- **Define expected behaviours explicitly.** Morten Wierod (ABB) discusses expected behaviours with every person joining the team. Don't assume implicit norms will hold.
- **Tie compensation and reviews to team behaviours.** John Hinshaw (HSBC) uses 360-degree reviews aligned to defined norms.
- **Model the behaviours you want.** Greg Poux-Guillaume (AkzoNobel): "I try not to use information in tactical ways. I give everybody the same information. And I filter very little. That takes a lot of politics out of the team."
- **Provide regular reinforcement.** Sara Mella (Nordea) identifies team members who engage in healthy debate and prioritises team goals; gradually shifts them into more leadership space.

#### Petting-zoo repair

- **Build the foundation of trust and psychological safety first.** Members need to know they can disagree without exploitation.
- **Insist on good data, distributed in advance.** Pre-read materials with detailed operational, sales, profitability data force discussion to be fact-based rather than opinion-based. Less personal, more about the numbers.
- **Monitor and improve discussion quality directly.** Mario Greco (Zurich Insurance): tracks how much discussion happens, how many people speak up, how many issues are raised, how members react to being challenged. Uses biweekly executive committees as a "temperature check."
- **Less consensus, more decision-making.** A leader's job is to discuss, debate, disagree — and to act as tiebreaker when no clear consensus emerges.

#### Mediocracy repair

- **Significant remake of the team.** Jonathan Lewis (Capita, 2017) removed everyone on the team and hired new executives aligned to defined purpose, values, and strategic commitments.
- **Hire for balance, not just competence.** Dave Fredrickson (AstraZeneca): "I want to have planners and dreamers, mixed with hard-nosed deliverers." Tell people that natural collaborators will need to act competitively sometimes, and vice versa.
- **Define domain-by-domain behaviour expectations.** Erwin Mayr (Wieland Group): IT/sustainability/procurement → coordination and collaborative problem-solving; product portfolio/pricing decisions → competitive accountability per business unit.
- **Articulate a compelling vision and purpose.** Mediocracies often emerge when the team has stopped having a shared purpose. Lewis's Capita transformation started with redefining purpose, values, strategic commitments.

### Step 3 — The four general moves (for all dysfunction types)

1. **Develop a clear vision and purpose** — a roadmap for decision-making.
2. **Focus on alignment** — populate the team with skills and temperament aligned to vision.
3. **Outline responsibilities** — goals, roles, decision authority. Lack of clarity is the seedbed for dysfunction.
4. **Establish behavioural norms** — explicit, coached, modeled, fed back.

## When To Use

- When a new CEO or leadership-team owner takes over an existing team.
- When team performance has plateaued or declined and you can't identify a single cause.
- When meetings have become ritual rather than real.
- After a merger or major restructuring, when previously-separate teams must work as one.
- When you sense the dysfunction but can't articulate which kind it is.

## When Not To Use

- When the underlying problem is structural rather than interpersonal (wrong incentives, wrong strategy, wrong market). Repairing the team doesn't fix the structure.
- When the issue is a single individual issue and the rest of the team is healthy. The targeted repair there is performance management, not team intervention.
- In the first 30 days of taking over the team — do diagnosis first, before action.

## Example Application

A new CEO inherits a team that meets weekly, makes decisions by consensus, never disagrees in the room, and routinely misses targets. The team has been together for seven years and is widely admired internally as "tight-knit."

Diagnosis: petting zoo. The cohesion is real, but it has come at the cost of the debate that produces good decisions.

Repair sequence:
1. Establish data-grounded monthly review meetings with pre-read.
2. Make explicit that disagreement is expected — and protected.
3. As tiebreaker, the CEO accepts that not every decision will be a consensus decision.
4. Track quality of discussion (Greco-style): how many distinct viewpoints in each meeting? Who is speaking? Is anyone *not* speaking?
5. After 6 months, evaluate whether the team's underlying composition is right. Mediocracy may be lurking under the petting zoo.

## Failure Modes

- **Misdiagnosis.** Treating a petting zoo as a shark tank produces overcorrection — too much aggressive debate, breaking trust further.
- **Going straight to firing.** Sometimes appropriate (Capita case), but often the team can be repaired. Diagnose first.
- **Single intervention thinking.** All three dysfunctions require sustained behaviour change, not a single workshop or off-site.
- **Ignoring the CEO's role.** Often the CEO is enabling the pattern — by leading from the role models or rewards system. The first intervention may be on yourself.
- **Building consensus on the diagnosis instead of on the response.** It's tempting to spend time getting everyone to agree on what's wrong. The diagnosis is the CEO's call; the question is which intervention to apply.
- **Treating "psychological safety" as the universal answer.** In a shark tank, more safety enables the rogue actors. The right move is to define behaviours and enforce them.

## Adaptation For This User

- **For founder CEOs:** the original team is often a shark tank (overgrown ambition) that becomes a petting zoo (calcified consensus) and finally a mediocracy (stagnation). Each transition needs a deliberate intervention.
- **For new managers of existing teams:** take the first 60 days to diagnose. Resist the urge to make changes before you've identified the pattern.
- **For boards evaluating CEOs:** team health is a leading indicator of CEO effectiveness. Ask board members what they observe in team meetings.
- **For coaching conversations:** when a user reports "my team is stuck," apply this diagnostic before suggesting interventions.
