# The Informal Organization

## Definition

The informal organization is the aggregate of organizational elements — shared values, informal networks, communities of practice, peer relationships, and emotional commitments — that primarily influence behavior through emotional means rather than through formal directives, rules, or structures. It exists outside the official lines of reporting, authority, and process, and is rarely written down.

It is distinct from but overlapping with culture. Culture is the broader set of shared attitudes and behaviors. The informal organization is the specific set of mechanisms (networks, norms, pride, peer pressure) through which those attitudes and behaviors are transmitted and reinforced.

## Why It Matters

Most major change efforts fail — not because the formal plan was wrong, but because the informal organization was not aligned with it. The informal organization determines how people actually behave, what information actually gets shared, and what they emotionally commit to — none of which is controlled by org charts, scorecards, or job descriptions.

Organizations that can mobilize their informal organizations alongside their formal structures achieve a competitive advantage that is difficult to observe and even harder to replicate.

## When It Applies

The informal organization is always operating. It becomes most visible and most important:
- During organizational change, when the formal structure is redesigned but the informal networks resist
- When formal directives aren't producing the behaviors they were designed to produce
- When high performance in specific pockets of the organization seems inexplicable by formal factors (pay, process, structure) alone
- When information flows or does not flow in ways the org chart would not predict

## Key Principles

- **The formal and informal are complementary, not competing.** The formal provides efficiency, scalability, clarity. The informal provides adaptability, motivation, speed. The best organizations achieve "the best of both."
- **Rationality has limits.** Rational clarity does not produce emotional commitment. You can understand why a change makes sense and still resist it emotionally. Leaders who rely only on the rational case routinely underproduce on change.
- **The informal can be mobilized.** It is not unmanageable chaos — it is a set of mechanisms (networks, norms, pride, communities) that can be understood, cultivated, and intentionally aligned with organizational goals.
- **Pride is the most powerful informal motivator.** When people feel proud of their work — and that pride is recognized by people they respect — it motivates beyond what any formal reward system can reach.
- **Informal networks carry information faster than formal channels.** What the informal network already believes about a change shapes how formal communication is received. Leaders who ignore informal channels are sending messages into an environment they don't understand.
- **Organizational Quotient (OQ).** The ability to use both IQ (formal, rational tools) and EQ (informal, emotional intelligence) and know when each is appropriate. High OQ distinguishes the most effective leaders.

## Practical Behaviors

- **Map the informal network** before launching a major change. Who are the hubs — the people others go to for advice, political wisdom, or sense-making? Engage them early.
- **Find the "fast zebras"** — the self-motivated, values-aligned individuals who will run with a new direction without being pushed. Identify them and remove the obstacles in their path.
- **Create mechanisms for informal information flow.** Regular informal conversations, skip-level sessions, management by walking around, brown-bag lunches — these are not soft activities; they are information infrastructure.
- **Tell stories, not just data.** The informal organization runs on stories. Stories that demonstrate what is valued, what courage looks like, what sacrifice was worth it — these travel farther and last longer than any slide deck.
- **Reward pride, not just performance.** Acknowledge individuals in front of people they respect (peers, customers, families). This is the most potent and underutilized motivational mechanism available to leaders.

## Common Failure Modes

- **Assuming the formal plan is sufficient.** The announcement, the slide deck, the new org chart. Leaders invest all their energy in the formal and are surprised when the informal fights back.
- **Ignoring informal resistance.** When reorganizations succeed on paper but the old informal networks persist and undermine the new structure. The old coalitions are still aligning people — just against the change.
- **Treating the informal as a problem to be eliminated.** The "yelling through the wall" at the call center was a sign of unmet need, not insubordination. Removing the wall (and the need) fixed the problem. Punishing the yellers would have preserved the problem.
- **Over-formalism at scale.** What made an organization great at 100 people (informal, values-driven, relationship-based) can be diluted by over-formalization at 1,000. Leaders must consciously preserve and adapt informal mechanisms as scale increases.
- **Under-formalism in disorder.** The reverse: leaders who celebrate informal culture while failing to build the formal systems (process, accountability, metrics) that allow the organization to function. The best of both is the standard; neither extreme works.

## Diagnostic Questions

- When you announce a major change, what does the informal network say about it before the formal Q&A?
- Who are the informal hubs in your organization — the people others go to for real information and real decisions?
- Is your formal strategy genuinely understood at the frontline, or is it understood in the meeting room and then reinterpreted by each layer as it cascades down?
- What are the informal norms that contradict your stated values? Where does "what we say we do" differ from "what we actually do"?
- Are your formal mechanisms (pay, metrics, titles) aligned with what the informal organization actually values and rewards?

## Useful Scripts

- **When a change is stalling despite clear formal communication:** "I want to understand what people are actually saying about this — not in meetings, but in the conversations that happen outside them. What's the story the informal network is telling?"
- **When engaging an informal hub:** "I'm not asking you to formally endorse this. I want to hear your honest reaction, and I want to understand the concerns your network has about it."
- **When recognizing informal leaders:** "What [this person] did isn't in anyone's job description. They saw something that needed to happen and they made it happen. That's exactly the kind of initiative this organization needs, and I want everyone to know it."

## Source Notes

Katzenbach & Khan (2010): The formal and informal are not opposites — they are complements. The formal provides the logic; the informal provides the magic. The best organizations learn to mobilize both. Key constructs: the informal elements (shared values, informal networks, communities, pride), the Organizational Quotient (OQ), and the distinction between formal best practice and informal best performance. The book argues that most change failures are failures of informal mobilization, not formal design.

## Open Questions

- As organizations become more distributed and remote, how do informal networks function? Are they degraded or simply different?
- Is it possible to deliberately design an informal organization, or does deliberate design inevitably formalize it and destroy what made it effective?
- The book distinguishes between "fast zebras" (who will run with change) and "frozen tundra" (middle managers who resist it). What turns frozen tundra into fast zebras? Is it selection, experience, or circumstance?
