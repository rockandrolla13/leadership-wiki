# Culture

## Definition

Culture is the set of shared beliefs, behaviors, and norms that define how work is done and how people treat each other within an organization. It is not what a company says it values; it is the pattern of what gets rewarded, tolerated, and punished in practice.

## Why It Matters

Culture determines what information surfaces and what gets buried, how people behave when no one is watching, and whether the organization can adapt. A culture with a clear shared WHY produces discretionary effort, cohesion under pressure, and loyalty that survives difficult moments. A culture without one depends on incentives and monitoring to sustain performance.

## When It Applies

Culture is always operating. It becomes most visible:
- During hiring, when the gap between stated and actual values becomes apparent
- During crises, when the unofficial rules of behavior surface
- During onboarding, when new people learn what is actually true about the organization
- During departures, in what people say after they leave

## Key Principles

- **Culture is carried by people, not statements.** The humans you hire are the culture. Every hiring decision either reinforces or erodes it. A values statement on a website does nothing if the people don't embody it.
- **Culture flows from WHY.** Organizations that know their purpose — why they exist, what they believe — attract people who share those beliefs. This creates self-reinforcing culture. Organizations without a clear WHY must rely on rules and enforcement.
- **Culture is revealed in stories.** What stories does the team tell about itself? What behaviors do the stories celebrate? The stories reveal the actual culture, not the aspirational one.
- **The informal organization is part of the culture.** Unofficial networks, norms, and relationships carry culture as powerfully as formal structures. Leaders who ignore the informal organization do not actually understand the culture.
- **Culture Creek:** (Lopp) Culture flows like a stream — from decisions made at the top, through the behavior of managers, to the daily actions of the team. Leaders who want to change culture must change the behavior closest to them first.

## Practical Behaviors

- In hiring: ask "Does this person believe what we believe?" as explicitly as "Can they do the job?"
- In 1:1s: listen to the stories people tell about the team. What is celebrated? What is complained about? This is the actual culture map.
- In onboarding: explicitly articulate the WHY of the team to every new person. Don't leave it to osmosis.
- Reward behavior that aligns with stated values; address behavior that contradicts them, visibly.
- Name and celebrate stories that embody what the team is trying to be.

## Common Failure Modes

- **Values stated but not embodied.** Leaders announce values and then behave in contradiction. People learn to read the behavior, not the statement.
- **Hiring for skills, ignoring fit.** A high-skill, wrong-fit hire creates drag, conflict, and eventual attrition — often after infecting part of the team with misaligned behavior.
- **Assuming culture is set.** Culture drifts with every hire, departure, and significant event. It requires active tending.
- **Ignoring the informal organization.** The unofficial culture — what actually happens in the hallways, in Slack, in lunch conversations — can undermine formal culture if it contradicts it.
- **Culture as decoration.** Swag, slogans, and offsites do not create culture. They reveal it when the content is authentic and expose the gap when it isn't.

## Diagnostic Questions

- What stories does the team tell about itself to outsiders? What do those stories say about actual values?
- When something goes wrong, what is the first reaction? (Blame assignment vs. problem-solving reveals cultural truth.)
- Do people bring problems up voluntarily or wait until they are asked?
- Who gets promoted? What behavior was rewarded?
- When the stated values and the observed behavior conflict, which wins?

## Useful Scripts

- **In hiring (values alignment):** "Tell me about a time you had to choose between doing what was expedient and doing what you believed was right. What did you do?"
- **Naming a cultural observation:** "I've noticed that we celebrate [behavior X] — that feels like it's at the core of who we are. I want to make sure we're deliberate about keeping that as we grow."
- **When culture is drifting:** "The way we make decisions has changed. I want to name that and understand it. What's driving it?"

## Source Notes

Lopp (2020): Culture is built and cared for by the humans who do the work. The story a team tells about itself (Culture Creek chapter) is the most reliable proxy for actual culture. Stories reveal what the team values, what it fears, and what it rewards.

Sinek/Mead/Docker (2017): Organizations that know their WHY attract people who believe what they believe. This is the mechanism of culture-by-alignment rather than culture-by-rules. The WHY creates a filter for hiring — not "can they do the job?" but "do they believe what we believe?" When beliefs align, discretionary effort follows. When they don't, management becomes an enforcement function.

Sinek (2009, *Start with Why*): The manipulation-vs-inspiration distinction underwrites culture maintenance. *"There are only two ways to influence human behavior: you can manipulate it or you can inspire it."* The six common manipulations — **price, promotions, fear, aspirations, peer pressure, novelty** — all work in the short term and erode loyalty over time. A culture maintained by manipulation becomes a culture of transactions; a culture maintained by inspiration (shared WHY) becomes a culture of loyalty.

> "Manipulations are a perfectly valid strategy for driving a transaction, or for any behavior that is only required once or on rare occasions. … But these strategies do not breed loyalty."

Katzenbach & Khan (2010): The contrast between **values-displayed** and **values-driven** organizations: Enron's values were *communication, respect, integrity, excellence* — printed on wallet cards, posted on the website, repeated in speeches. They were not lived. The U.S. Marine Corps's values — *honor, courage, commitment* — guide daily, life-saving decisions. *"A critical difference between these organizations lies in the use of the informal organization to bring the values to life."* Culture is not what is stated; it is what is enforced by peers, *both* through "walking the talk" and "talking the walk."

## Open Questions

- How do you preserve culture-by-WHY as an organization scales beyond 150 people? Dunbar's number suggests informal relationship networks break down at that size.
- Is there a productive tension between strong WHY culture and genuine intellectual diversity? Can you have both?
