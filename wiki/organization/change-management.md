# Change Management

## Use Case

Reach for this playbook when:

- A strategic or structural change has been announced, explained, and resourced — and is still not sticking
- A reorganization has succeeded on paper but the old behaviors, coalitions, and informal networks are still running the show
- A culture change has been proclaimed at the top but has not reached the frontline six months later
- You are about to launch a change initiative and want to avoid the most common failure mode before it happens
- A key change is facing concentrated resistance from a specific layer of the organization (most often: middle management)

The playbook addresses one specific failure pattern: change that is formally correct but informally misaligned.

## Diagnosis

**Signal: formal vs. informal gap.** The change was designed competently — the strategy is sound, the new structure is logical, the metrics are aligned. But something is wrong:

- People follow the new process in meetings and revert to the old one when the meeting ends
- The formal communication says "we're transforming" and the informal conversation says "here we go again"
- Senior leaders see traction in the data; middle managers tell a different story on the ground
- Early adopters feel exposed and unsupported; resisters feel emboldened

**Diagnostic questions before assuming it is a formal problem:**

1. Has the change been explained in terms of why it matters *to the people doing the work*, or only in terms of why it matters to the business?
2. What is the informal network actually saying about this change — not in town halls, but in the conversations that happen afterward?
3. Who are the informal hubs in this organization — the people others go to for real information and real decisions — and what do they believe about this change?
4. Is resistance concentrated in a specific layer? Middle managers, whose identity and power base is tied to the old structure, are the most common source of informal resistance.
5. Are early movers (fast zebras) being recognized and protected, or are they isolated and exposed?

## First Principles

**1. Most change failures are informal failures, not formal ones.**
Katzenbach & Khan's central finding: organizations that fail at change almost always got the formal design right. The strategy was sound. The structure was logical. The metrics were aligned. What failed was the informal organization — the norms, networks, and emotional commitments that actually govern behavior.

> "Culture change can be effected by adjusting the mechanisms of the informal organization in ways that tap into the underlying elements of human behaviors and result in the desired performance improvements." — Katzenbach & Khan, *Leading Outside the Lines*

**2. Rational clarity does not produce emotional commitment.**
You can understand why a change makes sense and still resist it emotionally. These are separate circuits. Leaders who rely only on the rational case — the slide deck, the all-hands, the email from the CEO — repeatedly underproduce on change because they are speaking to the analytical brain and leaving the emotional brain unaddressed.

**3. Change moves through networks, not org charts.**
Information, trust, and behavior change travel along the informal network — peer relationships, communities of practice, the people others go to for advice. An organization's informal network decides how incoming communication is interpreted. Leaders who don't understand the informal network are sending messages into an environment they cannot see.

**4. Kotter's distinction: leadership and management are both required, and most change stalls on the leadership side.**
Kotter: management produces order through planning, organizing, and controlling. Leadership produces change through direction-setting, alignment, and motivation. Change initiatives that fail often have excellent project management (formal) and inadequate direction-setting and motivation (informal/leadership). The formal plan without the leadership moves produces compliance at best and active resistance at worst.

> "Management is about coping with complexity. Leadership, by contrast, is about coping with change." — John P. Kotter, *What Leaders Really Do*

**5. The OQ framework: use both IQ and EQ.**
Katzenbach & Khan's Organizational Quotient (OQ) is the ability to deploy both formal tools (IQ: strategy, structure, metrics, processes) and informal tools (EQ: emotional intelligence, relationship-building, value-alignment, peer recognition) and know when each is appropriate. High-OQ leaders are not either-or; they run both tracks in parallel.

## Step-by-Step Approach

**Step 1: Map the informal organization before announcing anything.**
Before the change is launched, understand who the informal hubs are — the people others go to for real information, political wisdom, or sense-making. What do these people already believe about the change? The informal network will interpret your communication through the lens of what it already believes. If that belief is hostile, the formal communication will be discounted or reframed.

*Do not skip this step.* It takes two to four weeks and saves months of remediation.

**Step 2: Identify the fast zebras and remove their obstacles.**
Fast zebras are self-motivated individuals who are already aligned with the direction of change. They will run with it without being pushed. They exist in every organization; the job is to find them, not create them. Identify them early, name them as change leaders (formally or informally), and clear the obstacles from their path. Do not wait for the frozen tundra to move first.

*Fast zebra signals:* They are already doing informally what the change asks everyone to do formally. They are frustrated with the status quo. They speak in terms of the future, not the past.

**Step 3: Engage informal hubs — not for endorsement, but for honest input.**
Informal hubs are the people with high informal authority — whose opinion others defer to, whose approval matters. They are often not the most senior people in the room. Ask for their honest reaction before the change is announced publicly. This is not asking for permission; it is gathering intelligence and building trust. Hubs who feel consulted become advocates; hubs who feel bypassed become resisters.

**Step 4: Build informal mechanisms alongside formal communication.**
Formal: the all-hands, the email, the new org chart, the new metrics. Informal:
- *Stories* that illustrate the change in human terms — a person who embodied the new behavior and what happened as a result
- *Peer recognition* of early adopters — visible acknowledgment in front of people they respect
- *Communities of practice* — informal groups where people doing the new work can share what's working and what isn't
- *Symbols* — small, visible changes to physical or virtual environment that signal "this is real"

The informal mechanisms carry the change into the lived experience of work. Without them, the formal communication is just noise.

**Step 5: Address the frozen tundra — but not through force.**
The frozen tundra is the layer of the organization — most commonly middle management — whose identity and informal power are tied to the old way of working. They are not malicious; they are protecting something real. Forcing compliance produces surface behavior change and underground resistance. Instead:

- Name what they are giving up and acknowledge it directly
- Show them explicitly what success looks like in the new world — and that there is a place for them in it
- Give them a role in shaping how (not whether) the change happens
- Watch for the moment the informal tide turns — when enough fast zebras are visibly succeeding that it becomes untenable to remain a resister. This is when frozen tundra moves. You cannot rush it; you can create the conditions.

**Step 6: Close the loop — name the informal successes publicly.**
Change stalls when the formal system sends one message (here is the new direction) and the informal system sends another (the people who are changing are not being recognized for it). Close the loop: when someone embodies the new behavior, name it publicly, explicitly, and in front of peers. This creates the serotonin signal (pride) that makes the behavior repeatable.

## Suggested Language

- **When engaging an informal hub before a change is announced:** "I want to ask for something unusual. Before we go public with this, I want to hear your honest reaction — not the polite version, the real one. What are the concerns your network is going to raise? What would have to be true for this to actually work?"

- **When acknowledging what the frozen tundra is giving up:** "I know this changes how your team operates, and I know some of what you've built over the past few years is going to look different. That's real. I don't want to pretend it isn't. What I want to talk about is what your role looks like in the new structure — and make sure you have a voice in shaping that."

- **When recognizing a fast zebra publicly:** "What [this person] did this quarter isn't in anyone's job description. They took a risk on the new approach when most of us were still watching. I want to be explicit: this is exactly what we're asking everyone to move toward, and it's working."

- **When diagnosing informal resistance:** "I'm hearing the formal communication is landing well. I want to do a different kind of check. What's the informal conversation — not in the meetings, but in the hallways and the side chats — what are people actually saying about this?"

- **When the change is stalling:** "We've done the formal work well. The strategy is clear, the structure is right, the resources are there. Something informal is blocking us. Let's stop trying to push harder on the formal levers and spend the next two weeks understanding what the informal organization believes about this change."

## What To Avoid

- **Mandating change without enrolling informal influencers.** An announcement from senior leadership that bypasses the informal hubs will be filtered through whatever the informal network already believes. If that belief is skeptical, the message will be discounted before it reaches the frontline.
- **Treating all resistance as bad faith.** Resistance is often legitimate — the frozen tundra is protecting things that actually mattered. Dismissing it as obstruction produces resentment. Engaging it as information produces insight.
- **Overwhelming the fast zebras.** Early movers are the change's most valuable resource. If they are celebrated without being protected — if they bear the full cost of being early — they burn out and send a signal to everyone watching that change is dangerous.
- **Substituting more communication for different communication.** Sending the same message more frequently and more loudly is not the same as sending the right message through the right channels. The informal organization has a spam filter; it filters out repetition without emotional content.
- **Declaring victory before the informal organization has shifted.** A reorganization that has changed the org chart but not the informal norms is not a completed change — it is a ticking clock. The informal organization will revert to its previous patterns unless actively maintained.
- **Separating formal and informal as sequential steps.** The instinct is: do the formal work first, then address the cultural/informal layer. This is wrong. Both tracks must run in parallel from day one.

## Escalation Path

If Steps 1–6 have been executed faithfully and the change is still stalling:

1. **Run an OQ diagnostic.** Is the problem that the formal design was actually wrong — wrong structure, wrong metrics, wrong incentives — or is it a deeper informal resistance? The diagnostic tells you which lever to pull.
2. **Bring in an outside reader.** When leaders are too close to the change to see the informal dynamics clearly, a trusted outside perspective (executive coach, organizational consultant, peer organization) can surface what is invisible from inside.
3. **Make the informal resistance visible.** Sometimes the frozen tundra's resistance needs to be named directly, in a formal setting, as a behavior the organization is choosing not to tolerate. This is a last resort — it produces compliance, not commitment — but it can break a stalemate that informal approaches have not resolved.
4. **Reconsider the pace.** If the informal organization is not moving at the pace the formal plan requires, the options are: accelerate the informal work (more informal mechanisms, more fast-zebra recognition), slow the formal plan (give the informal time to catch up), or accept that this change will produce surface compliance and manage accordingly.

## Follow-Up Actions

After the first 90 days of a change initiative:

- Pulse-check the informal network: interview five people at different levels who are not in your direct chain. Ask what the informal conversation says about the change now. Has it shifted from the pre-launch read?
- Audit the recognition system: are the fast zebras being visibly rewarded? Are the behaviors you want being celebrated informally, not just in formal reviews?
- Map the frozen tundra: who is still in the "wait and see" posture? What would move them? What is their cost of staying where they are?
- Look for stories: the change has produced at least one story of someone embodying the new behavior and succeeding. Find it. Tell it. Tell it again.

## Sources

**katzenbach-khan-leading-outside-lines-2010:** The primary source. The formal-vs-informal distinction, the fast-zebra/frozen-tundra dynamics, the OQ framework, the pride-builder as the key mobilizer, and the mobilization model (map → identify fast zebras → engage hubs → build informal mechanisms → address frozen tundra) all come directly from this book.

**hbr-leadership-vol1 (Kotter):** Kotter's distinction between leadership (direction, alignment, motivation) and management (planning, organizing, controlling) provides the formal framework within which Katzenbach's informal model operates. The two are complementary: Kotter describes what must be done; Katzenbach describes the informal mechanisms through which it must be done.

**hbr-must-reads-2025 (Sutton & Rao, "Friction Project"):** The "addition sickness" framework — the organizational tendency to add process, approve layers, and increase coordination without removing what's obsolete — is a common formal barrier to change. Good-riddance reviews and subtraction tools are useful complements when formal friction is part of the resistance.

**hbr-change-anyones-mind-2021 (Adam Grant, "Persuading the Unpersuadable"):** Provides the *upward* counterpart to Katzenbach & Khan's *downward/outward* framework. When the change is stalling because a senior leader will not move — not the frozen tundra of middle management, but the boss or peer with formal authority — Grant's if…then map is the diagnostic. Match the resistance profile:

- *Know-it-all who insists the current way is fine* → ask them to *explain the mechanism* of the current system step by step. The articulation pressure exposes the gaps.
- *Stubborn senior who treats every change pitch as an attack* → stop pitching conclusions. Re-frame as questions ("what if?" "could we?"). Plant; let them grow attached; let them claim the idea.
- *Narcissistic founder whose ego is tied to the old design* → praise an unrelated, secure-identity strength first. Deliver the substantive concern from the secure base.
- *Disagreeable executive who blocks every proposal* → stand your ground. Build the multi-week persuasion campaign (Fadell on iPhone: weeks of prototypes, refined pitches, and group pressure to get Jobs to change his mind). Backing down forecloses your influence permanently.

The full playbook lives in [[communication/persuasion]]. Use it when the change is failing *upward*; use Katzenbach & Khan's framework when the change is failing *outward and downward* through the informal organization. Most stalled changes are both — and require both playbooks running in parallel.

## Heifetz: Technical vs. Adaptive Diagnosis (the upstream classification)

[[sources/heifetz-adaptive-leadership-2009]] — most change-management failures are *diagnostic* failures: the leader applies technical solutions to adaptive challenges. The distinction:

- **Technical problems** have known solutions; can be resolved through existing expertise and current structures. The new IT system. The consultant report. The reorganization on paper.
- **Adaptive challenges** require changes in people's *priorities, beliefs, habits, and loyalties* — they generate loss, trigger resistance, and have no expert who can hand down the answer.

The most common mistake: treating an adaptive challenge (cultural transformation, post-merger integration, strategic repositioning) as if it were technical (deliverable by a project plan, a consultant, or a new system). The Katzenbach & Khan framework above is partly the corrective — adaptive change must mobilize the informal organization, not just rewire the formal one. But the *prior* move is the diagnosis: which kind of challenge is this, actually?

### The Balcony Discipline

The leader of adaptive change must continually move between the *dance floor* (where action happens) and the *balcony* (where patterns are visible). From the dance floor, you see the immediate conflict, the resistance, the project plan slipping. From the balcony, you see: who is benefiting from the status quo, who is losing what, which work-avoidance patterns are operating, where the system is over-heating or under-heating. The skill is to do both simultaneously — keep one eye on events around you and the other on the larger pattern.

### Productive Zone of Disequilibrium (PZD)

Adaptive change requires *some* heat — discomfort, urgency, conflict — to overcome the system's preference for the status quo. Heifetz's heat-modulation discipline:

- **Below the PZD:** people are comfortable; the work avoidance dominates; nothing moves. Raise the heat. (Surface a difficult issue. Refuse to defer the decision. Name an interpretation that contradicts the dominant story.)
- **In the PZD:** the discomfort of *not* dealing with the problem is at least equal to the discomfort of dealing with it. The work happens.
- **Above the PZD:** the system breaks down — people panic, exit, blow up. Lower the heat. (Slow the pace. Acknowledge what's being lost. Add structure. Reduce immediate stakes.)

Most change-management failures over-pace (above the PZD; system breakdown) or under-pace (below the PZD; work avoidance dominates).

### Work Avoidance Patterns

Predictable forms include: creating a new committee with no authority; finding a scapegoat; attacking the messenger; demanding more data ("we need more analysis"); insisting on consensus; ritualistic compliance without internalization. The leader's job is to *name* the avoidance pattern publicly and *put the work back to the group that owns it.*

### Pace and Path

Adaptive change is bumblebee-shaped, not linear. *"Once you help unleash the energy to deal with an adaptive issue, you cannot control the outcome."* Patience and persistence are operating requirements; the resolution may differ from what was first imagined. Linear project plans are a category error when applied to adaptive work.

### Engaging Opposition Explicitly

The most under-supplied move in change management: *scheduling the conversation with the person whose loss is greatest.* Ask: what will you lose if this change happens? Acknowledge it. Do not promise to prevent it; do promise to honor it. Most resistance is rational once you understand what the resister is losing.

### Don't Do It Alone

Heifetz's three-relationship discipline:

- **Allies** share your purpose. Build coalitions of allies before raising the heat.
- **Confidants** are outside the system. They can tell you the truth without political cost. Form these in calm times (see [[sources/george-7-lessons-crisis-2009]]).
- **Opposition** must be engaged, not avoided. Treat their resistance as data.

## George (2009) on Using Crisis as the Change Lever

[[sources/george-7-lessons-crisis-2009]] — Lesson 5 ("Never waste a good crisis") supplies the *timing* dimension. Resistance to adaptive change is high in calm times because the losses are concrete and the gains abstract. In crisis, the losses of *not* changing become salient; the political resistance drops; previously-impossible moves become possible. The discipline: identify the structural changes that are blocked by calm-time politics; have the plan ready; execute when the window opens.

Examples in the wiki: Welch's GE transformation (created a crisis to break the bureaucracy); Wendy Kopp at TFA (used 1995 near-collapse to refocus on core mission); Medtronic 1998 (used growth slowdown to acquire seven companies). The cost of crisis is real; the cost of *wasting* a crisis is larger.
