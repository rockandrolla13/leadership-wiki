# The C-Suite as a Team

## Definition

The C-suite as a team is the idea that the executive group must function as a working *team* — with shared identity, mutual goals, joint accountability, and collective performance evaluation — rather than as a coalition of independent functional leaders each optimizing their own group. The concept is operationalized in Gartner's 2023-24 research, summarized in HBR Nov–Dec 2025's *idea*Watch column, which found only 31% of C-suite executives consider their peers their primary team.

## Why It Matters

The "best individual at each functional role does not produce the best top team" finding mirrors the Eisenhardt and Druskat-Wolff research on team effectiveness more broadly. Companies are hiring excellent CMOs, CFOs, CTOs, and so on — but selecting them on functional expertise without weighting their ability to work cross-functionally. The Gartner result:

> "Just 31% of C-suite executives considered their peers in the C-suite to be their primary team. Most CMOs identified primarily with the marketing team, CFOs with the finance group, and so on." — Gartner, cited in HBR *idea*Watch Nov–Dec 2025

The downstream effects: misaligned goals, delayed decisions, executives over- or under-emphasizing core leadership behaviors (collaboration, conflict resolution, decision-making, executive learning, influence), and a top team that the CEO does not trust to handle complexity. Gartner found only 34% of CEOs think their C-suites are prepared for what's ahead.

## When It Applies

- New CEO assessing whether to keep the inherited C-suite. Functional excellence is necessary but not sufficient.
- When the CEO finds themselves brokering every cross-functional decision because the executives cannot do it themselves.
- When strategy execution is failing despite a coherent strategy — the friction is usually at the executive interfaces.
- After a major reorg or M&A event, when the executive team is technically in place but has not yet *become* a team.
- When evaluating an executive's promotion readiness: their ability to work as a team member at the next level is at least as important as their functional capability.

## Key Principles

- **Functional excellence ≠ team excellence.** A CMO who is brilliant at marketing but cannot collaborate with finance and operations will cap the organization's performance, regardless of marketing function quality.
- **Identification follows accountability.** Executives identify with the group whose results they are accountable for. If their compensation, evaluation, and recognition flow only from their function, they will treat their function as their primary team.
- **Behaviors can be diagnosed.** Gartner names five core leadership behaviors of executive teams: collaboration, conflict resolution, decision-making, executive learning, and influence. Over- or under-emphasis of any of these signals misfit.
- **The CHRO has a unique role.** The CHRO is best positioned to monitor team dynamics and intervene. The article frames this as a CHRO-CEO partnership: CHRO surfaces patterns; CEO acts on them.
- **Shared goals are a structural lever.** Set ambitious shared goals at the start of each fiscal year for which C-suite members are jointly accountable. This is not the same as the CEO's annual goals; it is the explicit list of outcomes the *team* owns together.

## Practical Behaviors

- **Annual shared-goals exercise.** At the start of the fiscal year, the CEO and C-suite spend a dedicated session producing a short list (3–5) of strategic priorities the *entire* C-suite is jointly accountable for. These goals are reviewed quarterly as a team, not individually.
- **C-suite review of cross-functional friction.** Monthly or quarterly review of where executive interfaces are slowing decisions. The CHRO presents patterns observed over the prior period.
- **Mutual evaluation.** Each year, the CEO solicits structured peer input on each C-suite member's collaboration, conflict resolution, decision-making, executive learning, and influence behaviors. Used as input to development plans.
- **Intervention escalation.** When an executive's behavior undermines collaboration, Gartner identifies seven escalating interventions: *networking* (least invasive — connecting them to peers at other companies), *peer coaching*, *executive coaching*, *reassignment*, and *dismissal* (most aggressive). The CHRO recommends; the CEO decides.
- **Cross-functional exposure for next-gen leaders.** Identify the next C-suite candidates and ensure they have substantive work experience across multiple functions before they hit the C-suite.

## Common Failure Modes

- **The CEO as the only bridge.** When every decision routes through the CEO because the executives cannot bridge to each other, the CEO becomes the bottleneck and the team has not formed.
- **Individual incentives, team rhetoric.** Saying "we are one team" while compensating purely on functional results. Compensation reveals what is actually believed.
- **Avoiding executive-team conflict.** A C-suite that does not disagree is a coalition, not a team. (See Eisenhardt's "good fight" — productive disagreement is the *output* of a high-performing top team.)
- **Hiring exclusively for functional fit.** Selection criteria treat "executive team membership" as a secondary attribute. Then the team underperforms despite individual excellence.
- **Diagnosing dysfunction as a personality clash.** Often the issue is structural — incentives, goals, or operating rhythm — not personality. Coaching the individuals without fixing the structure does not stick.
- **Premature dismissal.** Jumping to the most aggressive intervention (firing) without first trying networking, peer coaching, and executive coaching. Expensive in talent and signal.

## Diagnostic Questions

- If asked, would each of your direct reports name your function or the C-suite as their primary team? If most name function, you are a coalition.
- Are there explicit shared goals for which the entire C-suite is jointly accountable, separate from individual functional goals?
- When was the last time the C-suite had a productive disagreement about something substantive? If you cannot remember, you may be a polite coalition.
- Is the CHRO actively monitoring team dynamics, or is the CHRO purely a functional executive?
- When two executives cannot agree, does the question route to the CEO immediately, or do they have a pattern of working it out between them first?
- If you needed to fire one executive for behavior (not performance), would you have the evidence and process to do it? If not, you have not been measuring team behavior at all.

## Useful Scripts

- **Naming the team intent (CEO to C-suite at start of year):** "We are going to operate as a team this year. Here are 4 shared goals we are all accountable for. We will review them quarterly *as a team*. Your functional goals matter too, but the team goals come first when they conflict."
- **CHRO surfacing a pattern:** "I've been watching team dynamics for the past quarter. I see [X] showing up consistently in cross-functional decisions. I'd like to share what I'm seeing and propose how we address it."
- **Initiating a peer-coaching intervention:** "I want to set up a structured peer coaching relationship between you and [other executive]. The focus is [specific behavior]. We'll review after six months."
- **Escalating intervention:** "Networking and peer coaching haven't moved the needle. I want to bring in an executive coach focused on [collaboration / conflict resolution / decision-making]. This is investment, not punishment."
- **Holding the line in shared-goal conflict:** "I understand your function's metric goes the other way on this. But we agreed at the start of the year that the shared goal takes precedence. Help me think about how we deliver both."

Primary source: Gartner research (200 U.S.-based CXOs, 303 global CEOs, 153 senior executives, surveyed 2023–24), summarized in HBR *idea*Watch Nov–Dec 2025, "Does Your C-Suite Really Operate as a Team?" ## Open Questions

- Is "team" even the right frame for the C-suite? Some leadership scholars argue the C-suite is structurally a *coalition* with rotating sub-coalitions, and forcing team norms onto it produces theater rather than performance.
- The Gartner research finds CHROs are the natural stewards of executive team dynamics. In organizations where the CHRO is more transactional (compensation, benefits) than strategic, who plays this role?
- How does this framework apply in private-equity-owned companies (see PE article in same issue), where the executive team is often rebuilt in the first 24 months and "team-ness" is a deliberately rebuilt property?
- The 31% finding may understate the issue: of the 31% who said the C-suite was their primary team, how many were the CEO or chief-of-staff role for whom the C-suite *is* their function?
