# Team Design

## Definition

Team design is the deliberate choice of who is on a team, how big the team is, what authority it holds, what cadence it works on, and what type of work it is built for. Most leaders treat team design as administrative ("staffing"); the research treats it as one of the highest-leverage decisions in organizational design — because the *type* of team must match the *purpose* of the work, and most performance issues attributed to "team chemistry" are actually team-design failures.

## Why It Matters

A great virtuoso team configuration applied to steady-state work produces burnout. A great steady-state team configuration applied to breakthrough work produces incremental output where invention was required. Both failures look like team problems but are design problems. Getting team design right is the precondition for the team-level interventions in [[management/team-building]].

## When It Applies

- Forming a new team from scratch.
- Receiving a team and noticing chronic friction the existing members cannot resolve.
- Deciding whether to expand or split a team. (Amazon's "two-pizza rule" is a design heuristic for the split decision.)
- Configuring a team for a specific breakthrough project where the steady-state configuration is wrong.
- After M&A or reorg, when two cultures are now in one team and the design has not been updated to reflect it.

## Key Principles

### Team types are not interchangeable

Fischer & Boynton's "Virtuoso Teams" article makes a sharp distinction between **traditional teams** (built for steady-state, predictable work) and **virtuoso teams** (built for breakthrough, time-bound work). The design choices invert:

| Dimension | Traditional team | Virtuoso team |
|-----------|------------------|---------------|
| Member selection | Choose for availability and past experience | Choose for skill, regardless of familiarity |
| Ego | Repress; emphasize collective | Celebrate; elicit individual brilliance |
| Encouragement | Members get along | Members compete; solo performances created |
| Decisions | By consensus | By merit |
| Trade-off | Efficiency over creativity | Creativity over efficiency |
| Focus | Tasks completed on time | Ideas generated in rich flow |
| Work mode | Individually and remotely; email, phone, weekly meetings | Together, intensively, fast direct dialogue |
| Politeness | Encouraged | Direct dialogue without sparing feelings |
| Customer | Average customer | Sophisticated customer; surprise them |
| Stereotypes | Affirm common ones | Reject common ones |

Most teams in organizations are traditional teams and should be designed as such. Virtuoso teams are deliberately constructed for purposes where breakthrough is required and the cost of the configuration (intense friction, ego management) is justified by the outcome.

### Size constraints have empirical anchors

- **Two-pizza rule (Amazon).** A team that cannot be fed by two pizzas (typically 6–8 people) is too big. Communication overhead grows with the square of the team size; coordination cost dominates output beyond about 8.
- **Hackman's ideal team size.** J. Richard Hackman's research converged on 4–6 as the sweet spot for coordinated team work, with a hard ceiling around 9. Teams of 12+ are nearly always two teams pretending to be one.
- **Conway's law applies.** Team boundaries become product/code boundaries. Designing the team is designing the architecture.

### Role clarity with task ambiguity outperforms its inverse

Gratton & Erickson's research on high-collaboration teams: members need to know *who* does what (role clarity) while leaving *how* it gets done open (task ambiguity). The inverse — fuzzy roles with prescribed tasks — produces confusion and resentment simultaneously.

### Heritage relationships matter

Gratton & Erickson found that high-collaboration teams have at least 20–40% of members with strong prior ties. New teams that are 100% strangers underperform; teams that are 100% incumbents underperform too. Design for a heritage minority and majority.

### Authority must match accountability

A team accountable for an outcome but lacking authority to make the decisions that affect that outcome is structurally set up to fail. Common in matrixed organizations. Diagnose: does the team have decision rights over the dimensions of their accountability?

## Practical Behaviors

- **Before staffing a new team, write a one-page design memo** with: purpose, time horizon, team type (traditional or virtuoso), size, required skills, decision authority, operating cadence, and exit criteria (when does this team stop existing).
- **For new initiatives, decide team type first.** If the work is breakthrough, deliberately configure as virtuoso. If steady-state, traditional. Hybrid configurations are sometimes appropriate (virtuoso core, traditional supporting cast) but should be conscious.
- **Apply the two-pizza test to existing teams.** Teams larger than 8 should be split or restructured. Coordination cost dominates the marginal contributor.
- **Audit role clarity quarterly.** Each member writes their understanding of who owns what; compare the answers. Divergence = design issue.
- **Match authority to accountability.** Where the team owns an outcome, identify which decisions affect that outcome and ensure the team holds them.
- **Engineer at least 20% heritage relationships** when forming a team from scratch; build them deliberately when not naturally present.

## Common Failure Modes

- **Default to traditional team configuration.** Most leaders configure all teams the same way; breakthrough work then underperforms invisibly.
- **Treating headcount as team design.** Adding people without redesigning roles, cadence, and authority does not produce more output; it produces more coordination.
- **Skipping the exit criteria for virtuoso teams.** Virtuoso teams should be transient. Without an exit, they either burn out or normalize into traditional teams, losing what made them virtuoso.
- **Confusing collaboration with consensus.** Gratton & Erickson's research: collaboration is *task-ambiguous* coordination, not group decision-making by vote.
- **Letting team boundaries calcify.** Conway's law cuts both ways: if the team boundary is wrong, the product/system boundary becomes wrong too.
- **Authority/accountability mismatch.** Holding a team accountable for outcomes they do not control is the most common matrix-organization failure.

## Diagnostic Questions

- What is this team's *purpose* and *time horizon*? If we cannot state both in two sentences, the design has not been done.
- Is this a traditional or a virtuoso team? Does the staffing reflect that choice?
- Is the team larger than 8? If yes, what justifies the cost?
- Does each member have role clarity on what they own?
- Does the team have authority over the decisions that affect the outcomes they are accountable for?
- What percentage of the team has worked together before? If below 20% or above 80%, design risk is elevated.
- When was the last time we reviewed whether the team is still the right design for the work it is doing now?

## Useful Scripts

- **Naming team type at formation:** "This is a virtuoso team. We are configuring it for breakthrough on [X] over six months. The norms are different from your default team — fast direct dialogue, ego-celebrating, force proximity. Here is how that translates to our weekly cadence."
- **Splitting a too-large team:** "We have 14 people on this team. The coordination cost is dominating our output. I am proposing to split into [team A] focused on [scope] and [team B] focused on [scope], with [cross-team mechanism] for shared dependencies."
- **Surfacing role-clarity divergence:** "I want each of us to write down, in two sentences, what you think you own and what each other person owns. Then we compare. Where we diverge is where we need to align."
- **Authority-accountability check:** "We are holding the team accountable for [outcome]. What decisions affect that outcome that the team currently does *not* have authority to make? Let's name them and decide whether to delegate the decision or change the accountability."
- **Closing a virtuoso team:** "This team's purpose was [X]. It is complete. What did we learn that should outlast this team? Where does each member go next?"

## Open Questions

- The traditional-vs-virtuoso typology may be a binary that misses hybrid configurations. What about teams that need to oscillate between modes (e.g., research-and-development teams that periodically need virtuoso bursts)?
- Two-pizza rule's optimal size is influenced by communication tooling. Has remote work shifted the optimal size up (better tooling) or down (more communication cost)?
- For globally distributed teams, what is the equivalent of "force proximity" that the virtuoso configuration requires? Is true breakthrough work possible without co-location?
- Authority-accountability mismatch is common in matrix orgs by design. Is the mismatch always a failure, or sometimes a deliberate trade-off (e.g., to maintain functional excellence at the cost of execution speed)?
