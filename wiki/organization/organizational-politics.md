# Organizational Politics

## Definition

Organizational politics is the navigation of competing interests, informal power, and incomplete information in pursuit of organizational and personal outcomes. It is the activity by which decisions get made when the formal authority structure is *insufficient* to make them — which is most of the time, for most consequential decisions. It is not, by itself, a moral category. It becomes corrosive only when it operates against the institution's stated interests; it is *load-bearing* when it operates in alignment with them.

A clean definition: where [[principles/influence|influence]] is the productive use of personal power to shape outcomes outside your direct authority, *politics* is the broader activity of negotiating competing claims on resources, attention, and authority across the organization. Influence is a skill; politics is the terrain in which the skill is exercised.

## Why It Matters

A leader who pretends politics does not exist will lose to one who quietly engages it. Cross-functional projects fail more often from political under-investment than from technical inadequacy. Strategy documents land in coalitions that already exist; the leader who has not mapped the coalitions is communicating into a vacuum. Promotions are won and lost in conversations the candidate never sees.

The HBR Manager's Handbook frames the diagnostic clearly: positional power decays at every interface with someone outside your reporting line, and personal power compounds. Politics is the field in which personal power is built, spent, and replenished. > "Promoting your ideas to others is a discrete skill. Good ideas die if unmarketed." Refusing to engage politics is not neutral — it is unilateral disarmament.

Katzenbach and Khan add the deeper structural point: most consequential change runs through the [[organization/informal-organization|informal organization]], not the formal one. > "Leading outside the lines is harder than managing within the formal lines, partly because that territory is less well defined, less studied, and less written about." Politics is the activity of leading outside the lines.

## When It Applies

Politics is most consequential when:

- **Cross-functional initiatives** require commitment from people who do not report to you. The work cannot happen without their resources or attention, and you cannot order it. Almost all enterprise change is in this category.
- **Resource allocation moments** — budget cycles, headcount planning, prioritization meetings. These are the visible surface of decisions made informally beforehand.
- **Strategy decisions** that affect multiple functions' identities. The decision is rarely about the strategy on its merits; it is about whose function comes out stronger or weaker.
- **Organizational change** — restructures, scope changes, role redefinitions. Katzenbach and Khan: change is stopped by middle managers, not frontlines, because middle managers' identity and power base is tied to the old structure.
- **Promotion and succession**, where the formal review process aggregates a body of informal evidence that was being built for the prior 12 months.
- **Crisis or scarcity**, when the [[organization/incentives|incentive]] structure has not been updated to match the new reality and people fall back on coalition behavior.

## Key Principles

- **Politics exists for reasons.** It is not a bug; it is the system's response to information asymmetry, resource scarcity, and divergent incentives. The three structural drivers:
  - *Information asymmetry:* different parts of the organization know different things; politics is partly how those parts negotiate whose information counts.
  - *Resource scarcity:* there is never enough budget, headcount, or executive attention; politics is how the queue is ordered.
  - *Divergent incentives:* [[organization/incentives|incentives]] are rarely fully aligned across functions; politics is how the gaps are bridged.

  Wishing away these conditions does not wish away the behavior they produce.

- **Influence is politics done well; manipulation is politics done badly.** The boundary is whether the activity creates value for the institution as well as the individual. The same conversational move — pre-selling an idea 1:1 before a meeting — is either influence or manipulation depending on what the goal serves.

- **The informal organization carries the real decisions.** Katzenbach and Khan: > *"Rational clarity does not always create the emotional commitment that motivates a desired behavior."* By the time a decision hits the formal meeting, most of it has already been made in 1:1s, lunches, and hallway conversations. Leaders who only show up to the formal meeting arrive late.

- **Political capital is real, finite, and replenishable.** Every cross-functional ask costs capital. Every favor given builds it. Leaders who do not track their balance over-spend and find themselves powerless at the moment they most need to act.

- **Stakeholder maps are not paranoia — they are infrastructure.** Knowing who decides, who influences the decider, who blocks, and who advocates is not Machiavellian; it is the minimum literacy required to operate above first-line manager.

- **Coalitions form around perceived self-interest, not around stated values.** Kotter's analysis of power and influence in [[sources/hbr-leadership-vol1|HBR Vol. 1]] is direct on this: leaders produce change by *building coalitions* — not by issuing memos. The coalition is what makes the change durable.

## Practical Behaviors

- **Map your stakeholders before any consequential initiative.** For each: what do they want, what do they fear, what is their decision authority, who do they listen to? The map fits on one page. If it does not exist, you are operating blind.

- **Identify the informal hubs.** Katzenbach and Khan: in every organization there are 5–10 people others go to for sense-making, regardless of title. Find them. Engage them early — *before* you need their support, not when you do.

- **Spend political capital deliberately.** Before any cross-functional ask, ask: is this worth the capital it costs? If yes, spend; if no, defer. The leader who spends randomly runs out at the worst possible moment.

- **Pre-sell consequential ideas.** The HBR Manager's Handbook is explicit: socialize ideas 1:1 with the three most-influential people who will be in the decision meeting *before* the meeting. Adjust the proposal based on their reactions. The meeting becomes a ratification, not a debate.

- **Read the room before speaking.** Lopp's "act last" instinct applies: when you do not know the coalition structure of the room, let it surface before committing yourself.

- **Build relationships outside crisis.** Reciprocity is the substrate of political capital, and reciprocity built in calm is the only kind that survives heat. > *"Help precedes ask, sometimes by years."* (See [[principles/influence|Influence and Power]].)

- **Be transparent about the politics you are engaging.** Naming a political dynamic to your team — *"this initiative is going to require us to win over Finance, and that means X"* — is more durable than concealing it. Concealment teaches the team that politics is dirty; naming teaches them it is the work.

## When Navigating Politics Is Necessary vs. When It Becomes Corrosive

This is the most important distinction in the concept, and the one most often collapsed.

**Necessary** when:
- The organization's interests cannot be served without coalition-building, stakeholder management, and influence work.
- A right decision will not happen on its merits because incentives are not aligned, and someone has to do the work of aligning them.
- A new idea cannot land without socialization, because the audience is operating under information you must update.
- A function's value is invisible to other functions and someone must make the case for it.

**Corrosive** when:
- Energy goes into appearing to win rather than into the work itself.
- Coalitions form around individuals, not around the work — "Sam's people" vs. "Pat's people."
- Information is withheld from people who need it in order to preserve leverage over them.
- Disagreement is litigated in the informal channels because it is unsafe in the formal ones (a sign the [[principles/psychological-safety|psychological safety]] of the formal channels has failed).
- The organization's *stated* objectives diverge sharply from the *enacted* objectives, and the gap is exploited.
- The same name appears in every coalition. A serial coalition-builder eventually corrupts the network.

The test: *would I be comfortable describing what I am doing on the front page of the company all-hands?* (George's [[principles/trust|New York Times test]] applied at the organizational scale.) If yes, you are doing influence work. If no, you are doing the corrosive kind.

## Common Failure Modes

- **Pretending politics does not exist.** "I just focus on the work." This is a common engineer-to-manager failure pattern. The work does not move without political investment; the leader who refuses to invest is undermined by colleagues who do.
- **Treating politics as something other people do.** Every leader is doing politics. Refusing to name it does not exempt the leader from the activity; it just means they are doing it badly.
- **Avoidance disguised as integrity.** "I won't play those games" sometimes means "I will not do the necessary work of building coalitions." It looks principled and is often cowardice. (See [[principles/courage|courage]].)
- **Engagement disguised as integrity.** The opposite failure: the leader who spends so much time on the political surface that the underlying work suffers. The work is the proof; without it, political capital evaporates.
- **Coalition-building against the institution.** Building a power base that operates against the organization's stated interests is the version of politics that gives the word its bad reputation. It is also, ultimately, self-defeating — coalitions of this kind are unstable.
- **Mistaking visibility for influence.** Being in every meeting is not the same as moving any of them. (See [[principles/influence]].)
- **Burning capital on small things.** Spending political capital on issues that do not warrant it leaves no capital for the moments that matter.

## Diagnostic Questions

- Am I navigating politics, or am I avoiding them? If I cannot name the last three political moves I made deliberately, I am avoiding.
- For my most important current initiative: do I know who decides, who influences the decider, who blocks, and who advocates? If I cannot draw the map, I am operating blind.
- What is my current political capital balance with the three peers whose support I am most likely to need next quarter? When was the last time I gave them something they did not ask for?
- Where is the gap between the organization's stated objectives and its enacted objectives? That gap is where corrosive politics will grow.
- Who in the room am I afraid to disagree with, and why? Fear of disagreement is the surface of an unsurfaced political dynamic.
- When something I cared about did not happen recently, was it because I was wrong, or because I had not built the coalition? (See [[principles/influence]].)
- Am I building relationships only when I need something? If yes, the relationships do not exist.

## Useful Scripts

- **Surfacing a political blocker in a 1:1 with your boss:** *"I need to tell you what I think is actually blocking X. I don't think the issue is the technical proposal. I think it is that [Function] sees this as a loss of scope for them. If I'm right, I'd like your help thinking through how we address that — because the work isn't going to move until we do."*

- **Naming the political dynamic to your team (without poisoning them):** *"This initiative is going to require us to win over Finance. That is not a side activity — it is the work. Here is what I am doing to engage them, and here is what I'm going to ask you to do as well."*

- **Pre-selling an idea 1:1 (HBR Manager's Handbook):** *"I'm working on a proposal for [X]. Before it goes to the leadership meeting, I'd like your reaction. What concerns do you have? What would make this stronger?"*

- **Surfacing an unspoken coalition concern:** *"I'm picking up that the team is uncomfortable with how this decision is being made. I'd rather hear that directly than guess at it. What's the concern?"*

- **Spending political capital deliberately:** *"I'm going to call in a favor on this one. I know it will cost me with [person], and I am choosing to spend that capital because [reason]. You should know that's the calculation I'm making."*

- **Declining to engage corrosive politics:** *"I am not going to be part of a conversation that happens around the team rather than with them. If we have a concern, let's bring it into the room."*

- **Engaging a skeptical informal hub:** *"I'm not asking you to formally endorse this. I want to hear your honest reaction, and I want to understand the concerns your network has about it."* (See [[organization/informal-organization]].)

## Source Notes

The dominant frame in this page is from Katzenbach and Khan ([[sources/katzenbach-khan-leading-outside-lines-2010]]), who argue that the [[organization/informal-organization|informal organization]] is where most political action lives. > *"The informal isn't as easily defined as the formal, because it does not have the clear structural boundaries that the formal has. Its elements often overlap and don't follow the clean principles of 'mutually exclusive, comprehensively exhaustive' that analytical thinkers prefer."* This is, exactly, a description of why politics resists clean analysis — and why it must be engaged anyway.

Their **Organizational Quotient (OQ)** framework is the most useful diagnostic the wiki has for whether a leader is engaging the informal effectively: OQ is the ability to deploy both *IQ* (formal tools — strategy, structure, metrics) and *EQ* (informal tools — relationship, value-alignment, motivation) and to know when each is appropriate. A leader high in IQ and low in EQ is the archetypal politically-tone-deaf engineer-manager; one high in EQ and low in IQ is the politically-savvy but operationally weak operator. The high-OQ leader uses both.

The HBR Manager's Handbook ([[sources/hbr-managers-handbook-17-skills]]) contributes the positional-versus-personal-power frame, which is the structural reason politics matters at all: > *"As you move up, positional power saturates. Personal power compounds."* Senior leaders who refuse to engage politics are choosing to forfeit the only power they have left.

Kotter's "Power and Influence" lineage, summarized in [[sources/hbr-leadership-vol1|HBR Vol. 1]], adds the change-leadership angle: > *"Management is about coping with complexity. Leadership, by contrast, is about coping with change."* Coping with change requires coalitions, and building coalitions *is* politics. Kotter is explicit that direction-setting is not planning, and that alignment requires winning the informal organization, not just communicating the formal one.

The wiki's [[principles/influence]] page is the productive sibling of this page; the two are deliberately separate so that influence (a discrete skill) does not get collapsed with politics (the broader terrain).

## Open Questions

- The line between necessary politics and corrosive politics is described in this page in normative terms. In practice, where exactly is it? Is the difference always visible to participants, or only in retrospect?
- Some leaders rise to senior altitudes without ever being seen as "political" — they are perceived as substance-driven. Is this a real phenomenon, or are they simply better at making politics invisible?
- In hierarchical cultures (Japan, Korea, parts of continental Europe), the mechanics of politics differ from the implicit US-corporate model in this page. How do the principles adapt?
- The advice to "pre-sell ideas 1:1" can look manipulative to people from cultures or generations that valorize open debate. Where is the line between productive pre-selling and gaming the meeting?
