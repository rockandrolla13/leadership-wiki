# Self-Limiting Beliefs

## Purpose

Muriel Wilkins's "The Hidden Beliefs That Hold Leaders Back" (HBR Nov–Dec 2025) names the seven most common internal blockers she identified across more than 300 coached executives, and offers a three-step self-coaching framework to dismantle them. The framework gives a leader a concrete way to *diagnose their own internal constraint* before reaching for an external solution — and a structured way to coach others whose performance suggests a belief problem masquerading as a skill problem.

## Core Idea

> "The biggest limiting factors for most [leaders] lie *within:* their own unproductive beliefs, which I call hidden blockers. Why hidden? Because these blockers are so ingrained and habitual that most of us aren't even aware that they exist." — Wilkins

Each hidden blocker is a belief that once served the leader — protected them, helped them succeed at an earlier stage — but no longer fits the role they now occupy. Identifying the belief is necessary but insufficient; understanding *why* it formed and *what purpose* it served is what permits a durable reframe. Wilkins draws on Carol Dweck's mindset research and Bob Anderson's organizational consciousness work as the theoretical scaffolding.

## The Seven Hidden Blockers

| Belief | Symptom | Reframe |
|--------|---------|---------|
| **I need to be involved.** | Micromanagement, bottlenecked decisions, less leverage from the team. | *I can do anything, but I can't do everything.* |
| **I need it done now.** | False urgency, rushed execution, increased errors, burnout. | *I need to focus on what truly matters.* |
| **I know I'm right.** | Shuts down collaboration, dismisses input, missed opportunities, reduced innovation. | *My role is to help others find solutions, not to always give them the answers.* |
| **I can't make a mistake.** | Unhealthy perfectionism, indecision, risk avoidance. | *My focus is excellence, not avoiding failure.* |
| **If I can do it, so can you.** | Unrealistic or unnecessary expectations, underestimating others' skills, limited development. | *What worked for me might not work for everyone.* |
| **I can't say no.** | Overwork, blurred priorities, poor boundary setting. | *I can say no to some things.* |
| **I don't belong here.** | Debilitating impostor syndrome, self-sabotage, reduced visibility and influence. | *I belong wherever I am.* |

## Steps: Uncover → Unpack → Unblock

### 1. Uncover

Recognize the symptom and name the underlying belief. Symptoms can be unmistakable (stalled advancement, missed targets, declining team morale) or subtle (nagging sense that something feels off, growing disconnect between intention and impact).

- Write down the recurring frustration in concrete terms — the actual situation, the actual outcome.
- Ask: "What belief about myself, others, or how things should be is driving how I act in that situation?"
- Test against the seven blockers. One usually resonates. Sometimes two interact (e.g., *I can't make a mistake* often pairs with *I need to be involved*).

> "Sometimes the signs of being blocked are unmistakable: stalled advancement, missed targets, or declining team morale or performance. At other times the clues are more subtle." — Wilkins

### 2. Unpack

Understand the belief's origin and purpose. Most hidden blockers were once helpful — they protected the leader from real risk or were rewarded earlier in their career.

- Trace the belief back. When did it first appear? What was happening in your life or career? Who praised you for the behavior it produces?
- Articulate the purpose it once served. Wilkins's example: Kristin's perfectionism (*I can't make a mistake*) was an asset as she was rising through the ranks; Philip's *I know I'm right* earned him praise as a brilliant individual contributor.
- Articulate the cost it imposes now. The behavior that earned promotion at level N often blocks promotion at level N+1.
- Track when the belief surfaces during the day. Kristin found her *I can't make a mistake* was driving overthinking on even minor, low-stakes decisions.

> "Most of us cling to beliefs that at one point helped us succeed or protected us from mental, emotional, or even physical pain or discomfort. When you unpack a belief, you can see how it no longer serves the purpose it once did and why it's time to shift your thinking." — Wilkins

### 3. Unblock

Replace the limiting belief with a supportive one, and tie the new belief to specific behavioral changes.

- Write the productive reframe (see table above for Wilkins's suggested reframes; adapt them to your own language).
- Translate the reframe into one or two observable behaviors. Wilkins's Kristin paired *I'll do the best I can with the information available to me* with a checklist that determined how much time to spend on each decision and how much input to gather.
- Wilkins's Philip paired *I guide others to find the best solutions* with a pre-meeting practice: visualizing himself as an equal collaborator on the same side of the table as his colleagues.
- Practice until the new pattern is automatic. This is slow. Expect months, not weeks.
- Track when the old belief reasserts itself, and what triggered it. The trigger is information about the belief's anchor.

> "To truly overcome a hidden blocker, you need to replace your limiting belief with a supportive one that will promote more-productive, effective behavior. Often the best way to do that is to work back from the outcome you want." — Wilkins

## When To Use

- When you have received the same feedback three or more times and the prescribed behavior change has not held.
- When you are stalled in a role and skill development is not closing the gap.
- When coaching a high-capability direct report whose performance is constrained by something that looks like attitude but might be belief.
- As a self-diagnostic during career development conversations (see [[coaching/career-development]]).

## When Not To Use

- For diagnosing others without their participation. Wilkins is explicit: "I'm not suggesting you function as a therapist or supply answers, just that you put on your coaching hat to help others reflect."
- As a stand-in for performance management. If the issue is competence or commitment, not belief, this framework will not help.
- As a one-time exercise. The blockers reassert. The discipline is in noticing the reassertion.
- As a label. *I don't belong here* is not a diagnosis to apply to others; it is a hypothesis to surface in conversation.

## Example Application

**Kristin** (from Wilkins's article): Recently promoted SVP. Feedback says collaborative and empathetic but indecisive. Her over-consultative process is creating accountability confusion.

- *Uncover:* belief is *I can't make a mistake.*
- *Unpack:* origin is past experiences where errors disappointed others; intensified by leading a division through a reorg.
- *Unblock:* reframe to *I'll do the best I can with the information available to me*; supported by a decision-time checklist.
- Outcome: team has clear direction; peers gain confidence in her; she develops trust in her own judgment.

**Philip** (from Wilkins's article): Technology executive. Brilliant but impatient and dismissive. Peers do not see him as a team player.

- *Uncover:* belief is *I know I'm right.*
- *Unpack:* origin in lifelong praise for having the right answer; reinforced by early IC success.
- *Unblock:* reframe to *I guide others to find the best solutions*; supported by pre-meeting visualization practice and calibration of how much pressure to apply to the situation at hand.
- Outcome: improved peer collaboration; influence grows because others choose to follow rather than because he imposes.

## Failure Modes

- **Skipping Unpack and going straight to Unblock.** The reframe does not stick because the leader has not understood why the belief is so adhesive.
- **Naming the belief without behavioral commitment.** Insight without practice is sterile.
- **Applying the labels to others without invitation.** "You have impostor syndrome" is unwelcome; "what comes up when you think about taking that visibility opportunity?" is helpful.
- **Treating it as therapy.** This is leadership self-coaching, not psychotherapy. Some patterns require professional support; know the limit.
- **Conflating multiple blockers.** Several may be active. Address them one at a time. The most active one will dominate the others; tackle it first.

## Adaptation For This User

For a senior IC moving into leadership, the two most common blockers are *I need to be involved* (default pattern of expert-driven control) and *If I can do it, so can you* (calibrating to one's own past performance rather than to the report's current capability). The *I know I'm right* blocker is the strongest predictor of an Expert action logic (see [[principles/leadership-development-stages]]) and the next development edge for most senior technical leaders.

## Diagnostic Questions

- What feedback have I received more than once in my career that I have not acted on?
- When I act in a way I later regret, what belief was driving the action?
- What did I get praised for early in my career that may now be in my way?
- When I feel resistance to delegating, why? When I feel resistance to saying no, why?
- If a peer described my dominant operating mode behind my back, what would they say — and which of the seven blockers fits that description?
- Whose performance on my team is constrained by something that looks like attitude? What belief might be active for them?

## Useful Scripts

- **Self-coaching prompt (Uncover):** "When I think about the situation where I keep getting stuck, what am I telling myself about what is at stake or what is required?"
- **Self-coaching prompt (Unpack):** "Where did this belief come from? When was it most useful to me? What is it costing me now?"
- **Self-coaching prompt (Unblock):** "If I held a more productive belief instead, what would I do differently in the next 24 hours?"
- **Coaching a report — exploring belief:** "When you think about mentioning all the work you've done to peers and senior leaders, what thoughts and feelings come up?"
- **Coaching a report — naming the pattern:** "I've noticed that when [situation X happens], you tend to [behavior Y]. What's going on for you in those moments?"
- **Naming a collective belief:** "I want to test something. Is there a belief on this team — 'this is the way it's always been done' or 'failure is not an option' or 'that's not our job' — that we should examine?"
