# Career Development

## Use Case

You are managing direct reports who want to grow but are not sure where, or who are growing in a direction the org cannot accommodate, or who are stalled despite excellent performance reviews. This playbook covers the structured conversations, the stretch-assignment design, and the diagnostic for "is the blocker the person, the org, or a hidden belief?" It applies equally to managing your *own* career.

## Diagnosis

You are in a career-development conversation when one of the following is true:

- A report has had two consecutive "exceeds expectations" reviews but no promotion in 12+ months. The performance is not the question; the path is.
- A report has been promoted or recently expanded scope and is now visibly stalled — getting the same feedback they got before the promotion. (Often this is an action-logic transition issue: see [[principles/leadership-development-stages]].)
- A report has signaled exit intent ("I'm thinking about my next move…") but has not yet committed. Window to influence is open and short.
- A report consistently misses opportunities to grow — declines stretch assignments, avoids visibility, defers to others. Wilkins's *I don't belong here* or *I can't make a mistake* may be active.
- You yourself feel professionally stuck despite achieving stated goals. Wilkins: "Falling into a professional rut — where progress feels impossible — is one of the most common challenges faced by executives."

This is *not* a performance-management conversation. If performance is the underlying issue, use [[management/performance-management]] instead. Mixing the two destroys both.

## First Principles

- **Career conversations are distinct from performance conversations.** Performance reviews look backward at delivered outcomes; career conversations look forward at trajectory. Holding them in the same meeting collapses both.
- **The manager's job is to widen the aperture, not pick the destination.** The report owns their career; the manager owns providing context, exposure, and stretch.
- **Most stalls are internal, not structural.** Wilkins's research on 300+ stalled executives: "Their biggest obstacles were often self-imposed." Diagnose the hidden belief before redesigning the role.
- **Stretch assignments are the highest-leverage development intervention.** They produce learning that coaching, training, and feedback alone cannot — because they create the conditions under which a new capacity is required, not optional.
- **Career development is the manager's job, not HR's.** HR provides programs and tooling. The manager provides relationship, exposure, advocacy, and the time to think.
- **People develop on their own timeline.** Action-logic transitions (Rooke & Torbert) take 18+ months. Beware of expecting a transformation in a quarter.

## Step-by-Step Approach

1. **Schedule a dedicated career conversation.** Quarterly, 60 minutes, outside the 1:1. Set an explicit expectation that this is a forward-looking conversation about trajectory, not about delivery.

2. **Open with the right question.** "Where do you want to be in three years, and what do you need from me to get there?" The three-year horizon is long enough to surface aspiration, short enough to remain concrete. Avoid the trap of asking "what's your dream job" — most people freeze.

3. **Surface the constraints.** What is currently in the way? Use this taxonomy:
    - *Skill gaps:* what they don't yet know how to do. (Address with stretch + coaching + training.)
    - *Experience gaps:* what they have not yet been seen doing. (Address with visibility, stretch assignments.)
    - *Belief gaps:* hidden blockers from Wilkins's framework. (Address by working the Uncover/Unpack/Unblock framework in [[coaching/self-limiting-beliefs]].)
    - *Structural gaps:* the org does not have the role they want, or the path is genuinely blocked. (Address by either redesigning role or being honest about the limit.)

4. **Co-design a development plan.** From the conversation produce:
    - One stretch assignment for the next quarter — uncomfortable enough to require new capacity, bounded enough to fail safely.
    - Two specific learning investments (a course, a book, a 1:1 with a senior peer).
    - One visibility move — a presentation to the leadership team, a cross-functional initiative they lead, a customer engagement.
    - One feedback loop — who will give them honest signal during the stretch (often not their direct manager).

5. **Reframe hidden blockers when they appear.** If the report says "I don't think I'm ready," do not validate or refute — *unpack*. "Where does that thought come from? What evidence are you using? What would 'ready' look like to you?" See Wilkins's framework in [[coaching/self-limiting-beliefs]].

6. **Check in mid-quarter.** Not on outcomes — on whether the stretch is the right one. Many stretch assignments need calibration as the work reveals itself.

7. **Close the loop at quarter end.** What was learned. What's the next stretch. What remains in the way.

## Suggested Language

- **Opening the conversation:** "This isn't a 1:1. This is a career conversation. I want to spend 60 minutes thinking with you about where you're heading and what you need from me to get there. Performance is settled; this is forward-looking."
- **Surfacing aspiration:** "If you had three years and unlimited support, where would you want to be? Forget the org chart for a minute — what kind of work do you want to be doing?"
- **Probing for blockers:** "What feels like it's in the way? Don't filter — give me the full list. We can sort it after."
- **Reframing a hidden belief:** "You said you're not ready. I want to ask the underlying question: what would 'ready' look like, and what's the gap between that and where you are?"
- **Offering a stretch:** "Here is what I'd like you to take on next quarter. It's deliberately bigger than what you've done before. I want you to know up front: it is okay if it doesn't go perfectly. The point is the learning."
- **Advocating visibly:** "I want you to present this to the senior team. I'll prep you. I'll back you in the room. The exposure matters as much as the work."
- **Naming a structural limit:** "I want to be honest with you. The role you're describing doesn't currently exist here. I will advocate to create it. But you should also be evaluating outside options — that's not disloyalty, that's responsible career management."
- **For yourself (Wilkins's self-coaching):** "Why am I stuck? Let me uncover the belief, unpack where it came from, and reframe it into something productive."

## What To Avoid

- **Conflating career and performance.** "Let's talk about your development" three minutes after delivering a corrective is a betrayal of the development conversation.
- **Promising what you cannot deliver.** "I'll get you that promotion next cycle" when you do not control promotions destroys trust when it falls through.
- **Designing stretch assignments without stakes.** A stretch that the team will catch if it falls is not a stretch. Calibrate the actual exposure.
- **Telling them what their career should be.** Career direction is the report's; your job is to widen the aperture, surface options, and create conditions.
- **Skipping the belief-gap diagnostic.** Pouring more skill development onto an *I don't belong here* report does not produce a developmental shift. Address the belief or the development will not stick.
- **Treating career development as one-off.** It is a quarterly rhythm. A single conversation produces good intent and no behavior change.

## Escalation Path

- If the structural gap is real (the org cannot grow them into what they want): begin the honest conversation about external options. Lopp's framing: it is better to lose someone with care than to lose them with surprise.
- If the hidden blocker is severe (debilitating impostor syndrome, perfectionism causing visible harm): an external executive coach is appropriate. Frame it as investment, not deficit.
- If the report's aspiration genuinely does not match their capability: a candid conversation about the gap and the alternative path. Wilkins: self-knowledge loosens the grip of a hidden blocker, but it also illuminates a real capability ceiling.
- If you yourself are stuck after one or two rounds of the Wilkins framework: bring in your own coach or trusted senior peer. Self-coaching has limits.

## Follow-Up Actions

- Track the stretch assignment. Is it actually stretching? If the report is comfortable, the assignment is too small.
- Track exposure. Has the report been seen by senior leaders during this quarter? If not, advocacy has failed.
- Re-rate hidden blockers. After 90 days, has any of the seven beliefs (see [[coaching/self-limiting-beliefs]]) loosened? Movement is slow but visible.
- After two quarters, ask the report directly: "Has the development plan we agreed to actually helped? What would you change?"
- For your own career: keep a running document of stretch assignments completed, lessons learned, and current belief blockers. Review semiannually.
