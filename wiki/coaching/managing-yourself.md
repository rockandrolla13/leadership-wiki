# Managing Yourself

## Use Case

You are a knowledge worker — manager, IC, founder, advisor — and you have realised that no organisation is going to manage your career for you. You need a working method for keeping yourself effective across a 50-year working life: knowing your strengths, knowing how you work, knowing your values, knowing where you belong, knowing what to contribute, and knowing how to relate to the people you depend on.

This playbook operationalises Drucker's five questions and the surrounding self-management literature. Pair it with [[coaching/personal-leadership-development]] for the longer-arc strategic frame.

## Diagnosis

Reach for this playbook when:

- You can list what you do for a living but cannot describe what you are actually good at.
- You are performing well but at unsustainable cost — you suspect you are working against your grain.
- Your values feel out of step with your organisation's, but you can't articulate the gap clearly.
- You have been promoted into a role and are quietly failing — you may have moved from "where you belong" to where you don't.
- Your relationships at work are friction-filled and you are not sure why — there may be a relationship-responsibility gap.
- You are entering the second half of your career and not sure how to make it different from the first.

## First Principles

- **You can only perform from strength.** Drucker: "One cannot build performance on weaknesses, let alone on something one cannot do at all."
- **You do not know your strengths until you have measured them.** Feedback analysis (write down what you expect when you make a decision; compare 9-12 months later) is the only reliable method.
- **How you perform is mostly fixed; what you do can be redesigned.** Drucker: "Do not try to change yourself — you are unlikely to succeed. But work hard to improve the way you perform."
- **Values are a check, not a project.** The mirror test — "what kind of person do I want to see in the mirror in the morning?" — is the test. Where your values and the organisation's values are incompatible, you will eventually fail to perform there, however good the role looks.
- **Where you belong is more about where you do not belong.** Most people learn this by being wrong first.
- **Contribution beats achievement as a frame.** "What should I contribute?" is a more useful daily question than "What do I want?" Christensen: the metric that matters is "the individual people whose lives I've touched."
- **Manners are infrastructure.** Drucker: "Manners are the lubricating oil of an organization." Friction often comes from lack of courtesy, not lack of competence.

## Step-by-Step Approach

### Question 1 — What Are My Strengths?

1. **Run a feedback analysis.** Whenever you make a key decision or take a key action, write down what you expect to happen. Date it. Nine to 12 months later, compare actual to expected. The persistent gaps reveal your real strengths.
2. **After two to three years of this practice, identify three patterns:**
   - Where do my predictions consistently match reality? → strength.
   - Where do I consistently overestimate? → likely weakness, or area of intellectual arrogance.
   - Where do I consistently underestimate? → unrecognised strength.
3. **Concentrate on strengths.** Put yourself where they produce results. Improve them with deliberate practice.
4. **Identify "disabling ignorance"** — areas where contempt for the other discipline is hurting you. Engineers dismissing people skills; HR dismissing quantitative reasoning. Repair these.
5. **Identify the bad habits that nullify strengths.** Brilliant plans that never get executed (a follow-through gap). Brilliant work that nobody cooperates with (a courtesy gap).

### Question 2 — How Do I Perform?

6. **Reader or listener?** Eisenhower was a reader; he failed as a listener president. Johnson was a listener; he failed by trying to be a reader. Few people can switch easily. Know which you are.
7. **How do you learn?** Reading? Writing? Doing? Hearing yourself talk? Taking copious notes? Schools assume one method (reading and listening); writers like Churchill learn by writing. Match practice to your mode.
8. **Do you work better alone or with others?** Both are valid; mismatched roles are not.
9. **If with others — in what role?** Subordinate (like Patton), team member, mentor, lead, adviser?
10. **Decision-maker or adviser?** This is the question behind why the "number two" person often fails as "number one." Many people make outstanding advisers and cannot carry the responsibility of the decision.
11. **High-stress or structured environment? Big org or small?** Few people thrive in both ends of a spectrum.
12. **The corollary: don't take on work you cannot perform or will perform poorly.** Saying no to a wrongly-shaped opportunity is part of managing yourself.

### Question 3 — What Are My Values?

13. **Apply the mirror test.** Who do you want to see in the mirror in the morning? This is ethics.
14. **Beyond ethics, identify your operational values.** Examples: short-term results vs long-term growth; hiring from inside vs bringing in fresh blood; incremental improvement vs breakthroughs; people-development vs raw output.
15. **Compare to your organisation's values.** Where there is a fundamental clash, you will be frustrated and underperform. The right move is usually to leave, not to suffer.
16. **Note Drucker's personal example.** He left a successful investment banking role in 1930s London because "I saw no point in being the richest man in the cemetery." Values trumped strengths. The work fit his strengths and didn't fit his values.

### Question 4 — Where Do I Belong?

17. **Start with where you do not belong.** Tasks that don't match strengths. Roles where decision-making is required but you are an adviser. Environments that don't match your performance mode.
18. **Then frame opportunities** as: "Yes, I'll do that — but this is the way I should be doing it. These are the kinds of results you should expect from me, and in this time frame, because this is who I am."
19. **A career is not planned. It develops.** Successful careers happen because people are prepared when opportunities arrive — they know their strengths, methods, and values.

### Question 5 — What Should I Contribute?

20. **Answer three sub-questions:**
   - What does the situation require?
   - Given my strengths, mode, and values, how can I make the greatest contribution?
   - What results have to be achieved to make a difference?
21. **Plan on an 18-month horizon, not longer.** Longer plans usually fail to be specific. Within 18 months, define what to achieve, how to start, what goals to set.
22. **Results should be:**
   - Hard to achieve (stretching) but within reach.
   - Meaningful — they make a difference.
   - Visible and measurable.

### Responsibility for Relationships

23. **Accept that other people are individuals with their own strengths, modes, and values.** A new boss who is a listener requires different communication than the prior boss who was a reader.
24. **Manage your bosses the way you would have them manage you** — observe, adapt to their mode, communicate in their idiom.
25. **Take responsibility for communication.** Most personality conflicts at work are missing-information conflicts. Tell your colleagues: "Here is what I am good at. Here is how I work. Here are my values. Here is the contribution I plan to make."
26. **And ask the same of them.** "What do I need to know about your strengths, how you perform, your values, and your proposed contribution?" The response is almost always: "Thanks for asking — why didn't you ask me earlier?"
27. **Build trust, not affection.** Drucker: "The existence of trust between people does not necessarily mean that they like one another. It means that they understand one another."

### The Second Half

28. **Plan the second half before you enter it.** People who do volunteer work after 60 started before 40. Same with second careers, parallel careers, social entrepreneurship.
29. **Three patterns:**
   - **Second career:** move into a different role or industry at 45-50.
   - **Parallel career:** keep doing your primary work, but add ~10 hours/week of meaningful work in a nonprofit, civic, or other domain.
   - **Social entrepreneurship:** build a second venture alongside the first.
30. **Why?** Setbacks are inevitable. A second domain where you contribute and are respected protects you when the first domain disappoints — the engineer passed over for promotion, the professor who didn't get the chair, the founder whose company failed.

## Suggested Language

- **To a new manager, in the first 30 days:** "Here's how I work best. I'm a [reader/listener]. I learn by [doing/writing/talking]. I'm strongest as [decision-maker/adviser]. I get less effective when [specific condition]. What do I need to know about how you work?"
- **To yourself, before accepting a new role:** "Does this match my strengths? Does the mode of work match how I perform? Are the values compatible with mine? Where doesn't it fit, and how flexible is the role?"
- **In a 1:1 with a report:** "I want to understand how you work. Are you a reader or listener? How do you learn — by doing, writing, or thinking out loud? Who has your back and how do they support you? Tell me one role where you've performed best."
- **To self, when stuck on a decision:** "Run the mirror test. If I do this, can I see the person I want to see when I shave tomorrow?"
- **To a peer who's about to take a wrongly-shaped role:** "That role wants a [decision-maker/adviser]. You're a [the other one]. The work will be there but you'll be miserable doing it. Is there a version of the role that fits how you perform?"

## What To Avoid

- **Trying to fix what you're not good at.** It costs more energy than improving what you are good at. Drucker's rule: don't take incompetence to mediocrity at the cost of taking competence to excellence.
- **Pretending you can switch modes.** Reader/listener, decision-maker/adviser, big org/small — these are mostly fixed. The job is to find the right fit, not to transform yourself.
- **Working in an organisation whose values clash with yours.** Frustration and underperformance follow. The right move is leaving, not gritting your teeth.
- **Treating relationships as automatic.** Drucker: most personality conflicts are missing-information conflicts. Tell your colleagues how you work. Ask how they work.
- **Confusing trust with liking.** You can trust someone you don't like, and like someone you don't trust. The professional version is the first.
- **Waiting until 60 to plan the second half of your life.** By then it is too late.

## Escalation Path

If self-directed work isn't producing change:

1. **Schedule a structured conversation with a mentor** — bring the five Drucker questions, work through your answers out loud. The act of articulation usually reveals the gaps.
2. **Engage an executive coach** for 6 months. The external perspective is what self-talk can't provide.
3. **Run a 360 feedback process.** Other people see your strengths and weaknesses differently than you do. Solicit explicitly.
4. **Take a sabbatical.** If you cannot find the answer inside the current role, time outside it may produce clarity.

## Follow-Up Actions

- Weekly: 15-minute review of your feedback-analysis log.
- Quarterly: revisit the five questions. Has anything changed?
- Annually: write or update a one-page personal operating manual that summarises your strengths, mode, values, contribution, and where you belong.
- On every job change: rerun all five questions before signing.

## Diagnostic Questions

- What three decisions have I made in the last 12 months where I wrote down what I expected? If none — start now.
- Am I a reader or a listener? Have I told my boss?
- What are the three values I would not violate to keep my job? Have I tested whether the organisation respects them?
- If someone asked me where I belong, could I answer in one sentence?
- What is the contribution I plan to make in the next 18 months — and is it stretching, meaningful, and measurable?
- Of my colleagues, how many know my strengths, mode, and values explicitly?
- What is my Plan B for the second half of life — and have I started building it?

## Useful Scripts

- **To self, on accepting a role:** "Will this role let me work in the way I work best? If not, how much of the role is flexible? If the answer is 'not much,' I should not take it."
- **To a junior person:** "Don't try to be Eisenhower if you're Johnson. Find out what mode you actually work in, then design the job around it."
- **To self, on disappointment:** "Did I just underperform, or did I take on work I can't perform? The two require different responses."
- **To a colleague who is about to be promoted:** "Number-two roles need advisers. Number-one roles need decision-makers. Are you sure you want to make that switch?"

## Open Questions

- Drucker's 50-year working life assumption is breaking down — some careers are shorter due to layoffs, some longer due to delayed retirement. Does the framework hold? Probably yes, but the second-half planning timeline shifts.
- Feedback analysis is slow (2-3 years to see patterns). For early-career people, is there a faster proxy? Strengths-based assessments (StrengthsFinder etc.) approximate it but aren't a substitute.
- The reader/listener distinction is empirically supported but feels reductionist. Most people are some mix. The practical move is to know your default and accommodate it.
- Drucker assumed substantial individual autonomy. Where roles offer little flexibility (regulated industries, frontline work, military), the framework partially applies — to mode and values, less to contribution choice.
