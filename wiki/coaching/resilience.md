# Resilience and Sustainable Performance

## Definition

Resilience is the capacity to maintain performance, judgment, and relationships under sustained pressure — not by absorbing more stress, but by reducing the cumulative drag of small chronic stressors (microstress), escaping the mindsets that trap leaders in unsustainable behaviour (ambition, "should," overwork), and structuring a multidimensional life that can absorb shocks.

It is *not* grit-as-willpower. Cross & Dillon's research is explicit: trying harder is often the wrong response. The right response is structural — push back on microstress at source, attune to the microstress you cause others, and build a portfolio of meaningful connections outside work.

## Why It Matters

The popular framing of stress is *macro* — a bad boss, a layoff, a crisis. The much more common cause of leader burnout is *microstress* — dozens of tiny moments daily that don't register as stress in the brain's fight-or-flight system but accumulate as cellular wear. Neurologist Joel Salinas's image: "wind eroding a mountain."

The cost is significant. High performers in Cross & Dillon's study were running close to burnout without knowing it. Some broke down during the interview itself — the conversation was the first time they recognised the load. Once eroded, the consequences cascade: damaged relationships at home, narrowing identity, withdrawal from outside-of-work groups that were supposed to be the recovery surface, and eventual physical or mental health crises.

## When It Applies

- High-performing leaders who feel "fine" but exhausted.
- Anyone in mid-career with growing responsibilities at work and home simultaneously.
- After a major life event (loss, illness, divorce, job change) — when the existing support structure has been disrupted.
- When you notice the same complaint coming from your spouse, your reports, and your friends ("you're not really here").
- When ambition, "should," or overwork stop feeling like a choice and start feeling like a trap.

## Key Principles

### Microstress (Cross & Dillon)

- **Microstress is small, frequent, often invisible** — and far more damaging cumulatively than visible macro-stress, because the brain doesn't trigger its protective response.
- **14 sources across three categories:**
  - **Drains on capacity:** misalignment with collaborators; uncertainty about reliability; unpredictable authority; high-volume collaborative demand; surges in responsibility.
  - **Depletion of emotional reserve:** managing others' wellbeing; confrontational conversations; lack of trust; stress-spreaders; political maneuvering.
  - **Identity challenges:** values mismatch; attacks on self-worth; draining family/friend interactions; network disruption.
- **The closest people are often the biggest source.** Family, close colleagues, and direct reports cause more microstress than abstract antagonists, because the emotional stakes are higher.
- **You cause microstress for others too.** A curt morning text, a last-minute project change, a missed commitment — all microstresses for someone else, which often boomerangs back as theirs.
- **Three strategies:** push back (saying no, managing tech interruptions, recalibrating relationships); attune to the microstress you emit; rise above (perspective, multidimensional life).
- **Two or three outside groups are the antidote.** People who cope best have 2-3 meaningful affiliations outside profession — a cycling group, a religious community, a book club, a volunteer organisation. The mix of perspectives and the trust formed there is what allows microstress to roll off.

### Happiness Traps (McKee)

- **Three traps drag down resilience:**
  - **Ambition trap:** winning at all costs, ruthless competition, blindness to relationships. Damages collaboration, damages self.
  - **"Should" trap:** doing what's expected rather than what you want. The Yoshino/Smith study: 61% of workers actively "cover" parts of who they are to fit in. Costly in identity terms, draining over time.
  - **Overwork trap:** "always on" as identity. More work, more stress, less creativity, less emotional intelligence — a negative spiral. Reid's BU research: people lie about hours worked, suggesting overwork is partly performative.
- **Escape requires three EI competencies:** emotional self-awareness (notice the feeling), emotional self-control (act on the insight), organizational awareness (distinguish your internal driver from external pressure).
- **Build three things:** meaningful work, hope, friendship at work. Achor: "Happiness comes before success." Happiness is a precondition, not a reward.

### Learning to Learn (Andersen)

- **Four attributes are coachable:** aspiration (shifting "I don't need this" to "what would my future look like if I did?"), self-awareness (questioning your own self-talk), curiosity (asking "I wonder why?" instead of "this is boring"), vulnerability (accepting beginner status as a temporary, productive state).
- **Generally we are biased to stay in our zone of competence.** Resilience requires being willing to be bad at new things — repeatedly, deliberately — over a 50-year career.

### Moxie (Dorsey)

- **For those facing impostor syndrome,** the response is moxie — strength of will, self-discipline, persistence — not more self-help affirmations.
- **Four tactics:** use strengths forged through hardship; give yourself permission to play (Ibarra's identity play); tune out the naysayers; know when to walk away.
- **Walking away is not failure.** Sometimes the obstacle is systemic and the right move is to invest elsewhere.

### Strategic Patience (Clark)

- **Progress is barely perceptible for long phases.** Diamandis & Kotler's "deception phase" applies in careers as in technology.
- **Look for raindrops** — small wins that are not the milestone but signal forward momentum (a LinkedIn request, a small unsolicited compliment, an invitation to a committee).
- **Stop moving goalposts.** Once-massive achievements get normalised into "no big deal" if you don't pause.
- **Aim directionally correct, not exactly correct.** Many of the people whose careers we admire ended up doing something close to but not exactly what they originally planned.

## Practical Behaviors

- **Run a microstress audit weekly.** Identify the 3-5 microstresses dragging on you this week. Pick one to push back on. Also identify 1-2 you are causing others — and stop.
- **Build a portfolio of three outside-of-work groups.** Not "I should" — actually schedule time, show up, contribute. The diversity of the group matters: different professions, ages, socioeconomic backgrounds.
- **Notice the happiness trap pattern** you fall into. Once a quarter, ask: which trap am I in right now? Ambition, "should," or overwork?
- **When you find yourself in the "I'm a fraud" loop,** invoke moxie — write down a hard situation you have already overcome, name the strengths you used, and commit to using them again.
- **Use the "future yourself" exercise** when stuck. Write three paragraphs starting "It is now three years since I broke free. I feel… I am now… The people in my life are…"
- **Audit your "raindrops" monthly.** Write down small wins that you haven't credited yourself for. Notice progress that has been normalised.
- **When you are tempted to give up on a long-term ambition,** check: am I in the "deception phase" where progress is real but slow, or am I genuinely on a bad strategy? The two require different responses.
- **Have a "walk away" criterion before you start a big effort,** so you don't escalate-commit out of sunk-cost fallacy when you should have changed direction.

## Common Failure Modes

- **Treating resilience as more grit.** Cross & Dillon explicitly counter the "just be tougher" frame. The right move is often structural, not stoic.
- **Mistaking microstress for "I just need to power through."** Microstress accumulates because it slips past the brain's normal stress response. You don't power through; you push back at source.
- **Building only close intimate connections, no diverse outside groups.** A close-knit family is a recovery surface but it doesn't provide perspective. A bowling team of strangers, a religious congregation, a cycling club — these provide the "right-size" effect that intimate ties don't.
- **Confusing performative wellness with actual recovery.** Posting about your meditation app is not the same as meditating.
- **Letting one identity (work) crowd out all others.** Salinas's research: high achievers in their 20s and 30s are most vulnerable to burnout because identity is monolithic.
- **Walking away too early (failing strategic patience) or too late (failing moxie's "know when to walk away").** Both are real risks; tell them apart by looking at benchmarks and outside view.
- **Self-help loop without action.** Awareness of impostor syndrome alone does not end it. The tactics matter.

## Diagnostic Questions

- In the last week, what were the three smallest moments that drained me — and who or what caused them?
- What microstress have I caused someone else this week? What was the ripple effect?
- Of the three happiness traps (ambition, "should," overwork), which one is most active in my life right now?
- How many groups do I belong to outside work that meet weekly or near-weekly? Are they diverse in age / profession / background?
- When was the last time I was a novice at something — really not good at it, learning publicly?
- What raindrops have I noticed in the last month that I haven't credited?
- Am I in a deception-phase plateau, or on a strategy that isn't working? What benchmarks tell me which?
- What's a "walk away" criterion I should set on my current big ambition?

## Useful Scripts

- **To yourself, at the end of a heavy week:** "Name three microstresses. Pick one to push back on Monday. That's enough."
- **To a report who's showing signs of overload:** "You've been hitting on all eight cylinders for too long. What's one thing we can take off this week — not next quarter, this week?"
- **To a partner / spouse who has flagged that you're not present:** "You're right. The phone in the kitchen is the problem. Starting tonight it stays in the hallway after 6 p.m."
- **To yourself, comparing yourself to a more "successful" peer:** "What's their head-start? Where am I now relative to where they were at the same point? What's the benchmark, not the snapshot?"
- **To a mentor when stuck:** "I think I'm in a deception phase — slow but real progress. But I want to test that read. Here's what I'm seeing — what's the outside view?"
- **To self, when ambition is tipping into ruthlessness:** "What's the long-game version of this? If I win this argument by stomping on her — what is the cost in the relationship across the next five years?"

## Open Questions

- Cross & Dillon's "build 2-3 outside groups" assumes you have the time and money to do so. For early-career professionals or those with high caregiving load, this may be aspirational. What is the minimum viable version?
- McKee's "should" trap assumes you can identify which "shoulds" are external pressure vs internal values. The boundary is often fuzzy.
- Strategic patience can be a rationalisation for sticking with a bad bet. How do you tell strategic patience from sunk-cost reasoning? Clark's research-the-terrain heuristic helps but isn't sufficient.
- Moxie is described as a learnable disposition, but Dorsey's interviewees largely had it from childhood adversity. Can it be cultivated mid-career without the formative experience? The evidence is thinner.
