# Personal Leadership Development

## Use Case

You are a working manager or senior IC who has hit a ceiling, plateau, or transition point — a promotion that did not solve the gnawing question, a midlife pivot, a job loss, an empty nest, a "why am I doing this?" moment. Or: you are at the start of your career and want to deliberately design rather than drift. This playbook is the long-game self-development companion to the management playbooks elsewhere in this wiki.

It is not a productivity hack collection. It is the operating frame for treating yourself as a deliberate project across a 50-year working life.

## Diagnosis

Reach for this playbook when:

- You are good at your job but no longer learning much, and you feel bored or quietly resentful (Drucker's "midlife crisis = boredom").
- You can describe what you do but not why you do it. Coleman: "Most of us feel that we've never found it, we've lost it, or in some way we're falling short."
- You are spending hours on activities that do not match what you would call your priorities if asked.
- You assume the same career path that brought you here will work for the next 20 years.
- Your peers seem more "successful" and the comparison is dragging you down — a symptom that you are measuring yourself on the wrong yardstick (Christensen).
- You suspect you have been doing what you *should* do rather than what you *want* to do (McKee's "should" trap).

## First Principles

- **You must manage yourself; no one else will.** Drucker: "Knowledge workers outlive organizations, and they are mobile. The need to manage oneself is therefore creating a revolution in human affairs."
- **Strengths beat improvement of weaknesses.** "It takes far more energy and work to improve from incompetence to mediocrity than it takes to improve from first-rate performance to excellence" (Drucker).
- **Purpose is plural and built, not singular and found** (Coleman). The Hollywood "I found my calling" arc is rare. Purpose is a thing you construct daily across multiple sources of meaning — work, family, faith, community, craft.
- **Resource allocation is destiny.** Christensen: "Your decisions about allocating your personal time, energy, and talent ultimately shape your life's strategy." If you allocate as if career is the only thing that matters, you will get a career-only life — including the consequences.
- **The marginal cost of "just this once" is a lie.** Christensen: "It's easier to hold to your principles 100% of the time than it is to hold to them 98% of the time."
- **The right metric is the people whose lives you touched** (Christensen) — or whatever truthful metric you set, but not the default career/money/title metric that the world will hand you.
- **Multiple leadership voices, not one** (Amy Jen Su): character, context, clarity, curiosity, connection. Cultivating all five is the work of a lifetime.

## Step-by-Step Approach

### Stage 1 — Know Yourself (the Drucker Audit)

1. **Run feedback analysis on yourself.** When you make a key decision or take a key action, write down what you expect to happen. Nine to 12 months later, compare actual to expected. Do this for 2-3 years. The pattern will tell you your real strengths.
2. **Determine how you perform.** Are you a reader or a listener? How do you learn (writing, talking, doing, hearing)? Do you work well alone or with others? Do you produce results as a decision-maker or as an adviser? Do you thrive under pressure or in structure? Big org or small? "Do not try to change yourself — you are unlikely to succeed. But work hard to improve the way you perform."
3. **Run the mirror test on your values.** "What kind of person do I want to see in the mirror in the morning?" Beyond ethics, what is your stance on long-term vs short-term, breakthrough vs incremental, inside-hire vs outside-hire? Where are you incompatible with your current organisation's values?
4. **Decide where you do not belong** before you decide where you do. Drucker: "Knowing where one belongs can transform an ordinary person… into an outstanding performer."

### Stage 2 — Build Your Purpose

5. **Reject the "I will find my purpose" frame.** Replace with: *I will construct meaning in my work daily, and acknowledge that I have multiple sources of meaning.*
6. **Run Sinek's WHY exercise in parallel** (see [[principles/purpose-and-why]]) but treat the output as a North Star, not a job description.
7. **Write a purpose statement at the intersection of:** what am I good at? what do I value? what activities light me up? what need can I help address? Coleman: do not force this to be one thing.
8. **Schedule one hour per week of explicit reflection.** Christensen did one hour daily as a Rhodes scholar to figure out his life purpose. The lower bound is one hour weekly.

### Stage 3 — Strategize Your Life (the BCG Seven Steps)

The Strack/Dyrchs/Bailey programme adapts corporate strategy steps for personal life. Run it once a year, or at any major transition.

9. **Step 1 — Define a great life.** Use the PERMA-V framework: Positive emotions, Engagement, Relationships, Meaning, Achievement, Vitality. Rate each on importance (0-10) and current satisfaction (0-10).
10. **Step 2 — Articulate your life purpose** (see Stage 2 above).
11. **Step 3 — Set a life vision.** What story would you like people to tell about you 5–10 years from now? What will the 80-year-old you not want to have missed?
12. **Step 4 — Audit your 168-hour week across 16 Strategic Life Units.** Six Strategic Life Areas: relationships; body/mind/spirituality; community/society; job/learning/finances; interests/entertainment; personal care. For each, rate importance, satisfaction, and time spent. Plot on a 2×2 (importance × satisfaction, bubble size = time). The top-left quadrant (high importance, low satisfaction) is where to act.
13. **Step 5 — Benchmark.** Look at role models. Look at large-N research on life satisfaction (German Socio-Economic Panel: significant other, children, friends, sports, spirituality, community involvement, savings all positively correlated). Notice what *kind* of life you want, then learn from those who lead it.
14. **Step 6 — Choose your portfolio changes.** Identify 3-5 specific changes. Reduce, outsource, bundle existing activities to make room. Examples from BCG workshop attendees: reconnecting with three school friends; weekly grandpa visit; trying a meditation app; changing jobs; supporting refugees; starting a side business.
15. **Step 7 — Run OKRs on yourself.** For each change, define an Objective and 2-3 Key Results with deadlines. Add Anchoring (share with someone), Consequences (rewards/penalties), and Check-ins (15 minutes weekly + 1-2 hour quarterly review). Put it on one page.

### Stage 4 — Allocate Resources Like a Strategy

16. **Decide explicitly what does and does not get your time.** Christensen's warning: high-achievement people unconsciously allocate every extra hour to whatever provides the most tangible accomplishment — usually career. Relationships and family pay off on a 20-year horizon, so they get under-invested.
17. **Build a culture in your family before you need it.** Power tools (coercion, threats) work on small children but stop working in adolescence. By then you need an established culture of respect, problem-solving, and self-esteem. This is intentional design starting young.
18. **Develop your second-half-of-life option early.** "If one does not begin to volunteer before one is 40 or so, one will not volunteer once past 60" (Drucker). Same applies to second careers, parallel careers, social entrepreneurship.

### Stage 5 — Cultivate Multiple Leadership Voices (Su)

19. **Voice of character.** What you stand for, constant across situations. Examples: "give the benefit of the doubt," "be direct with respect," "focus on what's best for the business."
20. **Voice of context.** As you grow senior, share the big picture before diving into details.
21. **Voice of clarity.** Help your team filter and prioritise. Empower them to say no.
22. **Voice of curiosity.** Lead with questions when work is interdependent or when you are coaching, not telling.
23. **Voice of connection.** Storytelling, gratitude, rapport-building before transactional work.

## Suggested Language

- **To yourself, mid-career:** "What do I want to be remembered for? Not in a sentimental way — what's the actual metric I'm willing to be judged on?"
- **In a coaching conversation with a peer:** "If you imagine yourself five years from now, looking back on this decision, what would you want to have done? Then work backwards."
- **In a mentoring conversation with a junior:** "What are you good at that you don't yet know you're good at? Try the feedback-analysis exercise for a quarter — write down what you expect every time you make a decision, then look back."
- **To your manager during a 1:1:** "I want to talk about contribution, not just promotion. What is the work this role can do that no one else here can — and is that the work I should be doing?"
- **To yourself, when comparing yourself unfavorably:** "What yardstick am I using? Whose game is this? If I'm losing at a game I don't actually want to win, the right response is to change the game, not to play harder."
- **To a recruiter / new role you're considering:** "Here's how I work best. Here's how I learn. Here's what I value. Here's the kind of contribution I make. Tell me whether this role fits — and where it doesn't, tell me how flexible the role is."

## What To Avoid

- **The Hollywood purpose hunt.** Waiting for a calling. Most days the calling is built, not received.
- **Treating self-development as a project with a finish line.** It is a continuous practice across 50 years.
- **Optimizing weaknesses at the cost of strengths.** Most performance reviews push you to fix weak areas. Drucker: most of the time, this is the wrong investment. Get from competent to star, not from incompetent to mediocre.
- **Comparing yourself to your peers' visible metrics.** Christensen's classmates "graduated with no intention of getting divorced and raising children who would become estranged from them" — and yet a shocking number did. Their visible metrics didn't track what mattered.
- **Confusing résumé virtues with eulogy virtues** (David Brooks, cited by Kouchaki & Smith). The two often diverge. The eulogy virtues come from how you allocate time, not from the work itself.
- **The "just this once" exception.** It is a slippery slope, not a one-off (Christensen).
- **Doing what you should rather than what you want.** McKee's "should" trap is one of the three biggest drags on workplace happiness.

## Escalation Path

If self-directed work is not producing change after 6–12 months:

1. **Engage an executive coach or therapist.** Pay for an external perspective. Self-talk has limits.
2. **Schedule a "life strategy" weekend** with a trusted partner or peer group. Strack et al. report that workshop participants often have breakthrough realisations when they walk through the steps in group.
3. **Consider a major external change** — a sabbatical, a role change, a geographic move. Sometimes the trap is structural and the only escape is structural.
4. **Audit who you spend time with.** Christensen: your culture is set by what behaviour you tolerate. If your daily circle reinforces the trap (overwork, "should," ambition), changing the circle may be the highest-leverage move.

## Follow-Up Actions

- Weekly: 15-minute review of life-strategy one-pager. Did you do what you said?
- Quarterly: 1-2 hour deep review. Update SLU portfolio. Adjust OKRs.
- Annually: rerun Steps 1-7. Update purpose statement and vision.
- After major life events (death, divorce, job loss, child leaving home, illness): re-run earlier than scheduled.

## Diagnostic Questions

- What did I expect to happen when I took this role / made this big decision? What actually happened? What does the gap teach me about my real strengths?
- If I rate the six PERMA-V dimensions (positive emotion, engagement, relationships, meaning, achievement, vitality) on importance and current satisfaction, which one has the biggest gap?
- Whose life that I have observed up close do I admire — and what specifically are they doing that I am not?
- Am I doing what I want, what I should, or what someone else's voice in my head is telling me to do?
- What metric will I want my life judged on — and how does that match the metric I am currently optimising?
- If I had to identify one "marginal cost" rationalisation I have been making in the last year, what would it be?

## Useful Scripts

- **To self, weekly review:** "What did I say I would do this week? What did I actually do? Where did the gap come from — a structural problem with the plan, or a values problem with me?"
- **To self, when tempted by 'just this once':** "If I do this once, will I do it again? What if I do? What is the cost three years from now of having become someone who does this?"
- **To partner, when committing to a change:** "I want to make a specific change. Here is what I am going to do. Here is when I will check in with you. Please hold me to it."
- **To manager, when proposing a role redesign:** "I am most useful when I do [specific work]. I am least useful when I do [specific work]. Here is what I would propose for the next six months."
- **To self, when stuck:** "What kind of empathy is this situation calling for — for myself? Am I being kind to myself the way I would be to a friend, or am I being harsh?"

## Just-Enough Leadership (Mintzberg)

Henry Mintzberg's 2004 article in [[sources/hbr-now-what]] is a useful corrective to the heroic-leader frame implicit in much of the personal-development literature. The argument: leaders who treat themselves as singular saviors — sweeping into companies, formulating grand strategies, downsizing left and right, sprinting between titles — often *succeed* for a year or two and then leave a wreck behind. The deeper practice is "just enough leadership":

- **Care more than you cure.** Treat leadership as an investment in the work and the people, not as a performance of fixing.
- **Connect more than you control.** The work that matters at scale runs through relationships, not commands.
- **Demonstrate more than you decide.** Model the behavior you ask of others.
- **Engage with your industry, your company, your job — seriously, quietly.** Stick around long enough to live the consequences of your own decisions. The career-hopper who sprints between roles never has to.

> *"Unhappy the land that has no heroes." "No. Unhappy the land that needs heroes."* — Brecht, cited by Mintzberg

For a leader at any stage, the diagnostic is: *am I trying to be the hero, or am I trying to make the work and the people succeed?* The two answers produce different choices about how to spend the next decade.

This frame pairs with Drucker's "you cannot improve yourself by being someone you are not" and Christensen's "your real allocation of resources reveals your real strategy": the heroic-leader self-image is often a *cost* of personal development, not a target — pursuing it usually means under-investing in the slower, quieter practices that produce durable contribution.

## Open Questions

- How rigid should the seven-step BCG framework be for someone with structural constraints (caregiving, immigration status, financial precarity)? The framework assumes substantial autonomy.
- Drucker's "you cannot change yourself" claim is contested. Andersen's "learning to learn" argues aspiration is itself coachable. The right read may be: *traits* don't change, but *practices* and *attention* do.
- What is the boundary between honest self-knowledge and self-limiting belief? Drucker says "do not try to take on work you cannot perform"; Andersen says "tolerate beginner status as you build new skills." Both are right at different points in the arc.
- How does this practice work in dual-career partnerships or caregiving contexts where time is structurally non-renewable?
