# Accountability

## Definition

Accountability is the obligation to justify — to give an account of — decisions and outcomes. It is distinct from responsibility, which is the duty to perform a task. Lopp's working definition is the clearest in the wiki:

> "Accountability requires a willingness or obligation to justify (account for) actions and decisions. This means when it goes sideways and everyone's staring at you, you need to be able to explain both how you got there and what is going to be done to fix it."

## Why It Matters

The accountability/responsibility distinction is the central mechanism by which a leader can [[principles/delegation|delegate]] work without abandoning the outcome. A manager who confuses the two reflexively reaches back for the keyboard when things go wrong, signaling to the team that the work was never really theirs. That single move — "If I were the engineer running this, we would've never ended up in this situation" — is, in Lopp's words, where you "watch trust erode."

Accountability also matters upward. HBR Vol. 2's "How Managers Become Leaders" tracks Harald, a high-potential leader whose move to enterprise leadership required him to "interact with various stakeholders but also to proactively address their concerns in ways that meshed with the firm's interests." That is accountability scaling: he could not perform the work himself, but he had to be able to account for it to regulators, investors, and the board.

## When It Applies

- Any time you [[principles/delegation|delegate]] meaningful work. The handoff is incomplete until the accountability boundary is named.
- When a project goes sideways and senior leadership is looking for an answer. The instinct is to say "I" a lot; the productive move is to account for the team's decisions without re-owning the work.
- In [[management/performance-management]], where the question is whether the individual will own the gap or whether you are accounting for them.
- When promoting from individual contributor to manager — the "complete visibility and total ownership" mindset of the IC has to be rebuilt around accountability for others' work.

## Key Principles

- **Accountability without authority is a trap.** If you are accountable but cannot delegate, hire, or set direction, you are being set up to fail. Demand decision rights commensurate with the accountability.
- **Accountability does not transfer downward by announcement.** Telling someone "you own this now" without giving them the context, authority, and time to learn does not produce accountability; it produces blame.
- **The willingness to give an account is the test.** A person who is accountable can answer "how did we get here, and what are you going to do next?" without flinching, deflecting, or restructuring the question.
- **Stop saying "performance management."** Lopp's hard-won advice: the moment you say or think those words, the relationship becomes structured and unnatural. Stay in coaching mode; account for the gap; agree on specific measurable actions.

## Practical Behaviors

- When something fails, ask the team — before the meeting where you have to account — three questions: how did we get here, what do we know now, what are we going to do next? Then answer with their words, not yours.
- Name the accountability explicitly on delegation: "You own the outcome of this. I own that you have the context, the people, and the air cover."
- In a sideways meeting with senior leadership, say "we" when describing the team's decisions and "I" only when describing your own. The reverse — using "I" for the team's work — is the New Manager Death Spiral.
- Distinguish responsibility and accountability in writing. In RACI terms, the accountable person is unique; the responsible person may be plural.
- Treat the "what are we going to do next" answer as the most important sentence. It is the thing senior leaders actually need.

## Common Failure Modes

- **The hero loop.** When the project goes sideways, the manager jumps in and does the work. They get the immediate save and pay for it for years in eroded team capability.
- **Mistaking visibility for accountability.** A weekly status report does not make someone accountable. The willingness to answer "what are you going to do about it?" does.
- **Diffuse accountability.** "The team is accountable" usually means no one is. Name the person.
- **Performance management as a euphemism for firing.** Lopp: "flip-a-switch" performance management is "manager shorthand for 'How do I let this human go?' rather than 'How do I make this human better?'"
- **Confusing accountability with blame.** Accountability is forward-looking — what are you going to do? Blame is backward-looking and produces defensive accounts, not honest ones.

## Diagnostic Questions

- If this work fails next quarter, who will have to explain it to my skip-level? Does that person know they will be the one explaining?
- When I describe the project to senior leadership, do I use "I" or "we"? Why?
- Does the person I have made accountable actually have the authority to change the outcome?
- Am I treating "performance management" as a process or as a synonym for "firing"? If the latter, what coaching have I skipped?
- When my team brings me a problem, am I receiving it or absorbing it?

## Useful Scripts

- **Naming accountability on a hand-off:** "I want to be explicit: you own this outcome. If it ships late or wrong, you're the one who'll need to explain it. I own making sure you have the context and the people to do it."
- **Accounting upward without blame:** "Here's how we got here, here's what we know now, here's what we're going to do next. I can go deeper on any of those."
- **Pulling accountability back without taking the work:** "I'm hearing that the team doesn't think this is their problem to solve. Let's go back and talk about why I think it is, and what you need from me to own it."
- **Refusing the responsibility-grab:** "I notice I want to jump in and fix this myself. I'm not going to. Tell me what you need from me instead."
- **Lopp's "checklist sentence" before any performance step:** "Have we had multiple face-to-face conversations over multiple months where we have clearly explained and agreed there is a gap, and agreed to specific measurable actions to address it?"

## Source Notes

- Lopp ([[sources/lopp-art-of-leadership-2020]]) makes the accountability/responsibility distinction the spine of his chapter on delegation, and returns to it in the closing "Interlude: The Test," where a leader's job is to "give account" — "to tell the tale of why we are here…to make the decision to continue and to explain in understandable detail what changes we'll need to make."
- HBR Vol. 2 ([[sources/hbr-leadership-vol2-2020]]) frames accountability as one of the seismic shifts at the enterprise level. "When CEOs fail to delegate reviews to direct reports who can handle them, they erode the autonomy and accountability of their management teams."
