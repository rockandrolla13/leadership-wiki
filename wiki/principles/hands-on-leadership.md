# Hands-On Leadership

## Definition

Hands-on leadership is the practice of senior leaders — including CEOs — actively shaping *how* work gets done at the operational level: architecting decision rights, modeling the analytical tools, coaching teams on customer-value metrics, and embedding continuous improvement into daily practice. It is the explicit alternative to the "above-the-fray" model in which the CEO is primarily a delegator focused on vision, strategy, and resource allocation. The framework is drawn from Scott Cook and Nitin Nohria's HBR Nov–Dec 2025 study of Amazon, Danaher, RELX, and Toyota.

## Why It Matters

The conventional model says senior leaders should pull themselves *out* of the details to focus on the big picture. Cook & Nohria's research suggests this is incomplete at best and damaging at worst. The top-performing CEOs they studied:

> "Spend an inordinate amount of time… architecting the day-to-day methods of execution in ways that set the standard and teach others to do work well." — Cook & Nohria, "The Surprising Success of Hands-On Leaders"

The result is "a system that performs reliably even when [the leader is] not in the room." This is distinct from micromanagement — the leader is not overriding individual decisions but building the *system* within which individual decisions are made. The four firms studied (Amazon, Danaher, RELX, Toyota) operate in different industries, on three different continents, with diverse histories. Their commonality is the rejection of the CEO-as-delegator model in favor of CEO-as-architect-of-execution.

## When It Applies

- When organizational performance depends on operational consistency across many sites or teams (Toyota, Danaher).
- When you suspect that strategic clarity exists but execution discipline does not — the conventional "we have the right strategy, we just need to execute" framing.
- When the leader is being pressured by board or peers to "let go" of operational details, and the leader's instinct is that doing so would be wrong.
- When the company has plateaued despite a strong strategy, and the issue is the *quality of the operating system* rather than the *direction*.
- When transitioning a founder/operator into the CEO role — the natural inclination is the right one and should not be coached away.

## Key Principles

Cook & Nohria identify **five core principles** that guide hands-on leaders:

1. **Obsess over customer-defined value.** Not customer-acquisition cost or lifetime value — *how the customer measures value*. RELX's Erik Engstrom has been repeating the same questions for 20 years: "How does the customer measure value? How do we know? How do we measure that? How does using this product improve the customer's economics?"

2. **Architect how work gets done.** Shift decision rights closer to the front lines. Equip teams with tools, frameworks, and protocols (customer-value metrics, structured memos, experimentation protocols). Amazon's six-page narrative memos, two-pizza teams, and "two-way-door decisions" are the famous examples.

3. **Run nested experiments.** Decisions are tested, not decided by rank. Even CEOs test their ideas when possible. Toyota's Eiji Toyoda did not declare that Toyota could manufacture cars in the US — he ran an experiment with a single reopened GM plant.

4. **Teach the operational toolkit so leaders do it *with* people, not *to* them.** Larry Culp (former CEO of Danaher, then GE): "We force division presidents to develop a command of the how so that they can teach the how. They shouldn't say, 'Go do that' but instead, 'Come do it with me.'" Danaher flies 100 executives to a single location for kaizen week.

5. **Drive continuous improvement as the everyday standard.** Not occasional heroic transformation. RELX's Engstrom: "Better, faster, cheaper — every year, forever." Toyota: "Problems don't get solved; they get managed with successive countermeasures."

## Practical Behaviors

- Spend a meaningful share of your week on the front lines — observing work, coaching execution, asking why the metric is what it is. Cook & Nohria's leaders make this "inordinate" by conventional standards.
- Build customer-value metrics that measure how the *company benefits the customer*, not how the customer benefits the company. Pair every CAC metric with a customer-savings or customer-delivery-speed metric.
- Insist on structured pre-reads (Amazon's six-page memo standard) for any non-trivial decision. Read closely; debate vigorously; disagree without rank deference.
- Categorize decisions by reversibility. Two-way-door decisions (reversible): decide fast, often by the team closest to the work. One-way-door decisions (irreversible): slow down, gather more input, commit deliberately.
- Reject the word "solution." Frame problems as managed-through-countermeasures, each of which can be improved.
- When proposing a change, run it as an experiment with measurable success criteria — not as a directive.
- Teach the operational tools by doing them with someone, not by writing them down for others to follow.

## Common Failure Modes

- **Confusing hands-on with micromanagement.** Cook & Nohria are emphatic: hands-on leaders *do not* override the decisions of those closer to the work. They build the system in which those decisions are made well. If a leader is correcting individual choices rather than improving the system, they have slipped from hands-on to micromanagement.
- **Borrowing tools without the system.** "You cannot mandate six-page memos and expect to get Amazon's innovation engine. You cannot simply borrow *kaizen* and hope to replicate Danaher's operating consistency." The practices are mutually reinforcing; isolated transplants fail.
- **Heroic transformation thinking.** The hands-on leaders Cook & Nohria studied reject the logic of transformation. Improvement is the everyday standard, not an episode. "If you don't commit to continuous improvement, you eventually set yourself up to need transformation. You get out of shape."
- **Treating hands-on as developmental phase to outgrow.** Culp: "Too many leaders treat this work as developmental. The best leaders don't. They know the real fun is getting in with the team and getting their hands dirty."
- **Switching styles inconsistently.** Hands-on without continuity destroys trust. Teams cannot tell when the CEO is teaching vs. overriding if the engagement is sporadic.

## Diagnostic Questions

- In the past two weeks, how many hours have I spent on the front lines vs. in executive meetings? What is the ratio Cook & Nohria's leaders maintain?
- When I observe work happening, do I improve the system or do I correct the individual decision? (The latter is micromanagement.)
- What is my customer-value metric? Does it measure how *we benefit the customer*, or how *the customer benefits us*?
- When I make a non-trivial decision, do I run it as an experiment with measurable success criteria, or do I commit it as a directive?
- If I left tomorrow, would the team continue to make good decisions in my absence — because the system would carry them?
- Which of the five principles (customer value, architecting work, experimentation, teaching the toolkit, continuous improvement) am I weakest at?

## Useful Scripts

- **Inviting the team to teach you the work:** "Walk me through how this actually gets done today. Don't summarize — show me."
- **Reframing a decision as an experiment:** "Before we commit, let's frame this as an experiment. What's the hypothesis, what would success look like, and what would tell us we got it wrong?"
- **Surfacing customer-value metric:** "How does the customer measure value here? What does success look like *for them*, in their metrics, not ours?"
- **Categorizing reversibility:** "Is this a two-way door or a one-way door? Two-way doors we decide today. One-way doors we slow down on."
- **Teaching by doing (Culp):** "Come do it with me. Let's run the kaizen together this week so you can run it without me next month."
- **Continuous improvement framing (Toyota):** "We don't have a solution here. We have the next countermeasure. What's the experiment that tests it?"

## Open Questions

- The four companies studied all had founder-CEOs or extremely long-tenured CEOs (Bezos 27 years, Culp 14 years at Danaher, Engstrom 15+ years at RELX, Toyoda decades). How does a CEO without that license credibly become hands-on without being perceived as meddling?
- Cook & Nohria explicitly note that the model "cannot be faked." What does the developmental path look like for a leader whose default is the above-the-fray model?
- In regulated industries (banking, pharma) where compliance demands consistency, how do you preserve experimentation discipline without dangerous variance?
- The article frames hands-on as a CEO practice. To what extent does the model translate to division presidents, VPs, and first-line managers? (The Culp quote — "we force division presidents to develop a command of the how" — suggests it cascades.)
