# Goleman's Six Leadership Styles

## Purpose

Daniel Goleman's six leadership styles (introduced in "Leadership That Gets Results", HBR 2000, and reinforced across HBR's 10 Must Reads on Leadership Vol. 1) provide a vocabulary for the *actual behavior* a leader is using in a given moment, the climate that behavior creates, and the consequences for team performance. The framework helps leaders diagnose their default style, recognize when it is failing the situation, and deliberately switch to a more appropriate one. Effective leaders fluidly use four or more of the six styles — and they do not lead with the two styles most likely to damage climate.

## Core Idea

Six distinct styles map onto different emotional intelligence competencies and produce different climates. Each style works in *some* situations and fails in others. The best leaders, drawing on self-awareness and empathy, switch between styles as the situation demands. The styles are not personality traits — they are behavioral repertoires. Treating "I am a coaching leader" as identity is a misuse of the framework.

## The Six Styles

| Style | Modus operandi | Phrase | Underlying EI competencies | Best when | Worst when |
|-------|----------------|--------|---------------------------|-----------|------------|
| **Coercive** | "Do what I tell you" | Demand immediate compliance | Drive to achieve, initiative, self-control | Crisis, turnaround, with problem employees | Almost everywhere else; corrodes climate fastest |
| **Authoritative** (Visionary) | "Come with me" | Mobilize people toward a vision | Self-confidence, empathy, change catalyst | New direction needed; clear vision required; followers need context | Leader is not actually more expert than the team |
| **Affiliative** | "People come first" | Build emotional bonds, harmony | Empathy, relationship-building, communication | Healing team rifts; motivating during stress; building trust | Used alone — produces mediocrity tolerance |
| **Democratic** | "What do you think?" | Build consensus through participation | Collaboration, team leadership, communication | Building buy-in or consensus; getting input from valuable people | Time-critical decisions; team lacks competence to advise |
| **Pacesetting** | "Do as I do, now" | Set high standards through example | Conscientiousness, drive to achieve, initiative | Getting fast results from a highly motivated, highly competent team | Used as a default — burns out the team and produces dependence on the leader |
| **Coaching** | "Try this" | Develop people for the future | Empathy, self-awareness, developing others | Helping an employee improve performance or build long-term capabilities | Employee defensive about learning; leader lacks expertise to coach |

## Steps (How to Use the Framework)

1. **Self-assess.** Record three recent leadership moves. Tag each with the style you used. Most leaders find one or two styles dominate.
2. **Diagnose the situation.** What is the team's competence on this work? What is the urgency? What level of buy-in does the next step require? What is the climate before the intervention?
3. **Match style to situation.** Use the table above. If the chosen style is one you rarely use, that is the development edge.
4. **Switch in moments.** A single conversation may need authoritative framing, then coaching follow-through, then affiliative closure.
5. **Audit climate periodically.** Goleman's research found that climate (employee perception of working conditions) explains 30% of business performance variance. A leader stuck in coercive or pacesetting modes will see climate scores decline first.

## When To Use

- When you find yourself frustrated that "they don't get it" — your style may be mismatched to the team's readiness.
- When you are a new manager defaulting to your IC instincts (often pacesetting): use the framework to deliberately try authoritative or coaching styles.
- When inheriting a damaged team: affiliative first (rebuild relationships), then democratic (rebuild voice), then authoritative (rebuild direction). Coercive last and rarely.
- In a crisis: coercive briefly, then authoritative as soon as the immediate danger is past.

## When Not To Use

- As a typology of *people*. The styles describe behaviors, not personalities. Saying "I'm a democratic leader" treats the framework as identity rather than repertoire.
- To justify staying in your favorite style. The diagnostic question is "what does this situation need," not "what do I prefer."
- In isolation from EI development. Style is the output of EI competencies; switching styles without underlying EI growth produces hollow theater.

## Example Application

A senior IC promoted to manager 90 days in finds the team is missing deadlines. Her instinct (pacesetting, "I'll show them") backfires — the team feels watched and demotivated. She switches:

- *Affiliative* first: 1:1s focused on understanding the human context behind the slippage.
- *Democratic* next: a team conversation about which commitments are realistic and how the team itself wants to prioritize.
- *Authoritative* third: explicit framing of the next quarter's direction, with the *why*.
- *Coaching* throughout: weekly stretch goals tied to individual development.

Pacesetting and coercive remain in the toolkit but are deployed surgically — coercive once when a teammate violated a customer-data policy, pacesetting at the very end of a critical release week.

## Failure Modes

- **Default-style trap.** A leader who has succeeded with one style sticks with it as they move up. Pacesetting at IC translates poorly to senior management; coercive in a turnaround is hard to step back from once stabilization is achieved.
- **Affiliative-only leadership.** Easy to like, slow to confront. Produces a team that hides problems because they like the manager too much to disappoint. (Compare Katzenbach & Khan's "people person" failure mode.)
- **Democratic decision paralysis.** Endless consultation on decisions that are not actually open.
- **Coercive default.** Goleman's research is unambiguous: this is the style with the most negative climate impact. Used habitually, it destroys trust and innovation.
- **Pacesetting masquerading as standards.** "I just hold a high bar" can be unselfconsciously pacesetting. The bar is fine; expecting everyone to clear it the way you do is the failure.
- **Coaching without expertise.** Asking questions when the team needs an answer wastes time and erodes confidence.

## Adaptation For This User

The wiki's general user-archetype is a senior engineer or manager moving from IC to leadership. The most common default for that archetype is **pacesetting** ("I'll just do it the right way and they'll learn") and the under-used styles are usually **authoritative** ("here is the vision and why") and **coaching** ("let me ask you a question rather than answer yours"). Development priority for this user: invest in authoritative and coaching repertoires before optimizing the others.

## Diagnostic Questions

- Of my last five leadership moves, which style did I use? Is one dominating?
- What style is the situation actually asking for?
- When was the last time I used the authoritative style — articulated the *why* and the *where we are going*?
- When was the last time I used coaching — asked a question rather than gave an answer?
- Is anyone on the team trying to give me feedback that I'm in coercive or pacesetting mode? Have I dismissed it?
- If my team described my style behind my back, would they agree on one or would they describe several? (Several is healthier.)

## Useful Scripts

- **Switching to authoritative:** "Let me step back and tell you where we're going and why, so the priorities make sense."
- **Switching to coaching:** "Before I share what I'd do — what's your read on the situation, and what are you considering?"
- **Switching to affiliative after damage:** "I think I came down too hard yesterday. I want to understand what that landed like for you."
- **Holding democratic time-bounded:** "I want to hear from each of you for two minutes. After that I'll make the call and tell you the reasoning."
- **Closing pacesetting deliberately:** "I'm aware I've been setting the pace by example for a few weeks now. That's not sustainable. Starting this week I'm asking you to set the pace and I'll back you up."
