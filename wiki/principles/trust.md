# Trust

## Definition

Trust in a leadership context is the accumulated belief by a person or team that their leader will act consistently, transparently, and in their interests. It is not declared; it is demonstrated through repeated behavior over time.

## Why It Matters

Without trust, information flow breaks down. Team members stop bringing early warnings, honest assessments, and genuine concerns. The leader operates on incomplete information and makes worse decisions. In the presence of trust, people share problems before they become crises, escalate when they're stuck, and take productive risks.

## When It Applies

Trust matters in every leadership relationship. It becomes most critical when:
- A team member has something uncomfortable to share and must decide whether it's safe to do so
- A project goes wrong and the leader is watching
- Delegation is required and the team member must believe they will not be abandoned if they struggle
- Honest feedback is needed in either direction

## Key Principles

- **Trust is built through repeated small acts, not single grand gestures.** Showing up reliably for 1:1s, following through on commitments, being consistent — these compound into trust over months. A single act of betrayal can undo months of accumulation.
- **Trust enables information flow.** When people trust their manager, they report problems earlier, share dissenting views, and are more honest about their own capacity. All of these produce better outcomes.
- **Trust requires visible listening.** It is not enough to listen; people must see that what they said changed something — your thinking, your decisions, your behavior.
- **Trust is asymmetric.** It is built slowly and lost quickly. A manager who cancels 1:1s repeatedly, claims credit for others' work, or fails to follow through on stated commitments can lose months of built trust.
- **Delegation is the concrete expression of trust.** Handing someone work — genuinely, with authority and context — is the most tangible signal that you believe in their capability.

## Practical Behaviors

- Keep every commitment you make, or explicitly renegotiate it before missing it
- Let team members see their ideas change your decisions; say explicitly "you changed my mind on this"
- When someone brings you a problem, respond to the substance, not the messenger
- Protect 1:1s; cancelling them signals the relationship is low priority
- When things go wrong on a delegated project, coach — do not take back control or assign blame

## Common Failure Modes

- **Inconsistency.** The behavior that predictably destroys trust is saying one thing and doing another, especially under pressure.
- **Invisible listening.** Soliciting feedback without visibly acting on it teaches people their input is performative, not real.
- **Proximity bias.** Treating people you see frequently as more trusted and informed than people who are remote. Creates a two-tier team.
- **The illusion of trust through friendliness.** Leaders who are liked are not necessarily trusted. Trust requires predictability and reliability, not warmth.
- **Trust as something claimed.** Leaders who announce "I trust my team" without behaviorally demonstrating it. Trust is shown, not stated.

## Diagnostic Questions

- In the past month, have any team members brought me a problem before it became a crisis?
- When was the last time someone told me something I didn't want to hear? Did I respond in a way that makes it likely they'll do it again?
- Have I made and kept commitments consistently this quarter?
- Have I changed a decision based on someone's input, and did I say so explicitly?
- What signals do I give when a delegated project struggles?

## Useful Scripts

- **When acknowledging that input changed your thinking:** "I've thought about what you said last week, and you were right — here's how I've changed my position."
- **When someone brings bad news early:** "I'm glad you told me this when you did. What do you need to address it?" (Not: "How did we let this happen?")
- **When a delegated project struggles:** "Where are you stuck? Let me tell you how I've seen this kind of problem resolved before — then you decide what to do."
- **When you've cancelled meetings and need to reset:** "I've been inconsistent with our 1:1s and that's on me. I want to restart and commit to making these stick."

## Source Notes

Lopp (2020) treats trust as the central binding agent in leadership. His three small things from the New Manager Death Spiral chapter all resolve to trust: letting others change your mind (trust through listening), building a diverse team (trust through valuing different perspectives), and delegating more than is comfortable (trust through entrusting work). He argues the 1:1 is the simplest and most reliable mechanism for building trust — not because of anything special about the format, but because consistent, high-bandwidth weekly conversation accumulates into a reliable relationship.

Lopp on the slow trust-building loop of feedback:

> "Feedback is a gift. The phrase is designed to remove the fear of receiving critical feedback, to reinforce the fact that you are about to receive a useful thing. … The important part of feedback isn't just that you receive the gift; you also need to unwrap (or unpack) it."

And on the asymmetry of feedback as the test of trust:

> "I am going to ask you for feedback in 1:1s regularly. I am never going to stop doing this, no matter how many times you say you have no feedback for me."

Katzenbach & Khan (2010): Trust at the *team* level — distinct from one-to-one trust — is built through the shared experience of difficulty. *"The Old Guard trust each other because through a combination of luck, smarts, blood, sweat, and tears they managed to bring a new thing into the world."* The implication: trust on a new team is built by surviving something together, not by trust-falls or offsites. When the Old Guard / New Guard split emerges, the cure is a shared real challenge — what Lopp calls "Never Waste a Disaster."
