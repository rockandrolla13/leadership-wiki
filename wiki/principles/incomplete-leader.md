# The Incomplete Leader

## Purpose

Deborah Ancona, Thomas Malone, Wanda Orlikowski, and Peter Senge's "In Praise of the Incomplete Leader" (HBR February 2007, anthologized in HBR's 10 Must Reads on Leadership Vol. 1) argues that the heroic leader who possesses all leadership capabilities is a myth — and a damaging one. The authors propose that effective leadership requires four distinct capabilities (Sensemaking, Relating, Visioning, Inventing) that almost no single person holds at high level. The mature leader's task is to know which capabilities they have, which they lack, and to staff or partner deliberately to close the gap.

## Core Idea

The conventional model of leadership treats it as a person — a heroic figure who can read the environment, build relationships, see the future, and design new ways of working. The authors' decades of research suggests no such person exists. Real leadership is *distributed*: each leader brings two or three of the four capabilities at high level; the remaining capabilities must be brought by the team around them. Acknowledging incompleteness is the first step toward effective leadership. Pretending to completeness is the most common executive failure.

## The Four Capabilities

| Capability | What it means | Example | Symptom of weakness |
|------------|---------------|---------|---------------------|
| **Sensemaking** | Constantly understanding changes in the business environment and interpreting their ramifications | A CEO asking, "How will new technologies reshape our industry? How does globalization of labor markets affect our recruitment strategy?" | Feels strongly that you're always right; frequently blindsided by changes; resentful when things change |
| **Relating** | Building trusting relationships; balancing advocacy (explaining viewpoints) with inquiry (listening to understand others); cultivating networks of supportive confidants | Herb Kelleher's bond-building at Southwest Airlines, including profit sharing and explicit emotional warmth | Blame others for failed projects; feel others let you down; frequent unpleasant, frustrating, or argumentative interactions |
| **Visioning** | Creating credible and compelling images of a desired future that people in the organization want to create together | Pierre Omidyar envisioning eBay as an online community where users took responsibility and had equal access to information | Wonder "why are we doing this?"; can't remember last time you felt excited about your work; lack sense of larger purpose |
| **Inventing** | Creating new ways of approaching tasks or overcoming seemingly insurmountable problems to turn vision into reality | Meg Whitman inventing security, vendor reliability, and product diversification mechanisms at eBay | Difficulty relating company vision to daily work; notice gaps between aspirations and how work is organized; things tend to revert to business as usual |

## Steps (How to Use the Framework)

1. **Self-assess across the four capabilities.** Honest rating, then 360 input from team and peers. The gap between self-rating and others' rating is the diagnostic signal.

2. **Identify your two strongest and two weakest.** Almost no one is strong on all four. Patterns:
    - Strategists tend strong on Visioning + Sensemaking; weaker on Relating + Inventing.
    - Operators tend strong on Inventing + Relating; weaker on Visioning + Sensemaking.
    - Sales-driven leaders tend strong on Relating + Visioning; weaker on Sensemaking + Inventing.

3. **Audit your team for capability coverage.** Whose strengths complement your weaknesses? If a capability is weak both for you and your team, the gap is structural.

4. **Hire, partner, or develop to close the gap.** Three options in order of speed:
    - *Hire* for the missing capability — board member, executive coach, or direct report whose strength is your weakness.
    - *Partner* with a peer who has the missing capability and create a working relationship where they bring it.
    - *Develop* the missing capability in yourself — slow and often impossible at executive altitude; useful at junior altitude.

5. **Sensemaking is the foundational capability.** The authors offer specific practices: (1) Get data from multiple sources; (2) involve others in your sensemaking; (3) use early observations to design small experiments; (4) do not simply apply existing frameworks — be open to new possibilities.

## When To Use

- During executive team composition: are the four capabilities covered across the team?
- During board recruitment: which capabilities does the board need that the executive team lacks?
- During your own development planning: which capability do you need to build vs. acquire?
- When you keep getting the same kind of feedback ("you're not strategic enough," "you're disconnected from customers," "your team is in execution mode but you're not aligned on direction") — these are capability signals.

## When Not To Use

- As a personality test. The capabilities are about behaviors and patterns of attention, not types.
- To excuse weakness ("I'm not a Visioning leader, so I don't have to do that"). The mature reading is: "I'm weak at Visioning; I need to either develop it or staff it around me. I cannot ignore it."
- For first-line managers. The four capabilities scale up; at first-line altitude the more relevant frameworks are [[principles/leadership-styles]] and the [[management/manager-skills-overview]].

## Example Application

A division president has driven strong execution for three years but is told by the board that "the next decade requires more strategic thinking." Self-assessment:

- Strong on Relating (long history with the team), strong on Inventing (rebuilt the operating model).
- Weak on Sensemaking (cannot articulate where the industry is heading) and Visioning (the team executes well but cannot say *toward what*).

Plan:
- *Hire* a strategy chief of staff strong on Sensemaking.
- *Partner* with a board member who is a respected Visionary; create a quarterly cadence around future-state framing.
- *Develop* Sensemaking habits: monthly customer visits, quarterly industry-trends review, deliberate exposure to dissenting analysts.

A year later the division president is still primarily an operator — but the team has the Sensemaking and Visioning it lacked, and the division has direction the board can recognize.

## Failure Modes

- **The completeness pretense.** Senior leader cannot admit weakness in any capability. Team self-censors. Capability gap persists; everyone knows it but nobody says it.
- **Hiring a "mini-me."** Adding someone with the same strengths and weaknesses doubles down on existing gaps. Diversity of capability is what closes them.
- **Confusing capability with skill.** Sensemaking is not a course you can take. It is a pattern of attention. Skill investments help, but the capability requires deliberate behavior over years.
- **Treating Inventing and Visioning as the same.** They are not. Visioning is *where we are going*; Inventing is *how we get there with new methods*. Many leaders have one without the other.
- **Skipping Relating because it feels soft.** Authors are emphatic: balancing advocacy and inquiry, cultivating networks, building trust — these are not optional. Leaders who skip Relating are routinely surprised by the team's reactions.

## Adaptation For This User

For senior IC moving into leadership, the most common pattern is: strong Inventing (you got promoted for finding new ways to solve problems), weakening Relating (the IC pattern of "the work speaks for itself"), weak Sensemaking (you have been heads-down on the work, not on the environment), and weakest Visioning (vision feels like other-people's-territory).

Recommendation: invest first in Relating (it is the precondition for the rest), then build Sensemaking habits (monthly external-view rituals), and explicitly delegate Visioning to a strong partner while you develop it.

## Diagnostic Questions

- Which of the four capabilities am I weakest at, and where does that show up in my team's performance?
- When I make decisions, am I drawing on data from multiple sources, or from the same loop of advisors?
- When was the last time I was genuinely surprised by a customer or competitor move? What was I missing?
- Do I have a peer or partner who balances me — who I trust to challenge my dominant capability?
- Is anyone on my team trying to bring a capability I am weak at, and am I making space for them to do it, or unconsciously suppressing them?

## Useful Scripts

- **Admitting incompleteness:** "Vision is not my strongest capability. I need [name] to bring that to this team."
- **Inviting Sensemaking input:** "What is happening in our industry that I am not seeing? Where am I behind?"
- **Building Relating practice:** "I want to spend the next 15 minutes understanding your view — I am going to ask questions and not advocate. What does the situation look like from where you sit?"
- **Naming a structural capability gap:** "Our team is strong on Relating and Inventing. We are thin on Sensemaking and Visioning. Our next two hires should reflect that, regardless of which functional role we are filling."
