# Influence and Power

## Definition

Influence is the capacity to shape decisions, behaviors, and priorities outside your direct authority. Power is the underlying capacity from which influence is exercised — and it has two distinct sources: *positional power* (granted by the role you hold, finite, formal) and *personal power* (built through credibility, expertise, network, and reciprocity, growing, informal). Senior leaders rely overwhelmingly on personal power because positional power saturates as you rise and reaches its limit at every interface with someone outside your reporting line.

## Why It Matters

A new manager often discovers that authority does not move things. Their team works fine; cross-functional initiatives stall. The reason: most of the work that matters at manager level and above is *outside* your direct authority. Without personal power, you have nothing to deploy. The HBR Manager's Handbook identifies "becoming a person of influence" as the first skill in the Managing Yourself cluster, ahead of communicating, productivity, and self-development — because without it, the other skills cannot land.

## When It Applies

- Managing up: your boss needs context, decisions, and air cover from above your authority. Influence is the only currency.
- Cross-functional projects: marketing, finance, legal, infrastructure, sales — none report to you, but the project requires their work. Positional power does nothing.
- Peer collaboration: peers are your most under-cultivated influence reservoir and the most decisive in performance reviews above first-line.
- Skip-level engagement: your boss's boss decides your promotion. They need to know you exist and trust you.
- Driving change through informal networks (see [[organization/informal-organization]]).

## Key Principles

- **Personal power compounds; positional power decays at every interface.** Your boss's title does not help when the marketing VP says no.
- **Influence is earned through reciprocity, not bartered.** The leaders with the most influence have given the most help without keeping score. Help precedes ask, sometimes by years.
- **Manage up before you manage out.** Time spent understanding your boss's priorities, communication preferences, and pressures yields outsized returns. The Manager's Handbook frames this explicitly: operate one level above your role.
- **Promote your ideas; do not assume merit sells itself.** The Manager's Handbook is explicit: "promoting your ideas to others" is a discrete skill. Good ideas die if unmarketed.
- **Silo-busting is influence work, not org-design work.** Many cross-functional gaps are not structural but relational. The fix is sustained relationships across the gap.
- **Networking is structural, not transactional.** A network you build only when you need it does not exist when you need it.

## Practical Behaviors

- **Build a manage-up page** for your boss. Top three priorities. Communication preferences (channel, frequency, depth). Pressures they are under. Decisions pending. Update monthly. (HBR Manager's Handbook, Skill 5.)
- **Identify your top five peer relationships** and schedule a recurring lunch, walk, or coffee with each. Once a month minimum. No agenda.
- **Identify two skip-level relationships** above you. Engineer one substantive conversation per quarter — present work, ask for advice, deliver value.
- **Help before being asked.** Notice when a peer is struggling. Offer a specific resource, person, or hour. Reciprocity will return.
- **Promote your ideas systematically.** Before you take an idea to a decision meeting, socialize it 1:1 with the three most-influential people who will be in the room. Adjust based on their reactions.
- **Track who you owe and who owes you.** Not for transactions — for awareness. The map shows where the network is thin.
- **Read the room.** The Manager's Handbook on persuasive communication: tailor the message to the audience's existing concerns, not yours.

## Common Failure Modes

- **Confusing authority with influence.** "I told them to do it" is a positional-power move that produces compliance, not commitment, and that limits your range to direct reports.
- **Hoarding favors and information.** Power held tightly evaporates. Power lent generously compounds.
- **Building a network only when you need something.** Visible self-interest erodes the relationships you are trying to leverage.
- **Skipping peer relationships in favor of upward focus.** Promotions are decided by your boss but seconded by your peers. Peer skepticism kills more careers than boss disappointment.
- **Treating influence as manipulation.** The most influential leaders are also the most trusted. (See [[principles/trust]] — trust is the substrate of personal power.)
- **Mistaking visibility for influence.** Being seen in meetings does not equal being able to move them. Visibility without substance is theater.

## Diagnostic Questions

- If I needed a favor from three peers outside my function tomorrow, would they show up? Why?
- Who has helped me in the past six months whom I have not thanked or reciprocated?
- What is my boss's top three priorities right now? If I cannot answer, my manage-up game is weak.
- Who in the organization knows my work that I have never spoken to directly? Who works near a decision I want made and does not know me?
- When I last lost a cross-functional argument, was it because I was wrong, or because I had not built the relationships to be heard?
- Where in the organization do I have *no* relationships at all? That is the next investment.

## Useful Scripts

- **Initiating a manage-up conversation:** "I want to make sure I'm helping with what matters most. Can we spend 15 minutes on your top three priorities for the quarter, and where I can add the most leverage?"
- **Building a peer relationship:** "I notice we keep colliding on [topic]. I'd like to spend 30 minutes understanding how you think about it before we hit the next decision."
- **Pre-selling an idea (Manager's Handbook):** "I'm working on [proposal]. Before it goes to the leadership meeting, I'd like your reaction. What concerns do you have? What would make this stronger?"
- **Offering help unprompted:** "I saw you're working on [X]. I have [resource / contact / experience] that might help. Want me to send it over?"
- **Engaging skip-level:** "I'd value 30 minutes with you next month. I want to share what we're working on and get your perspective on where we might be missing something."
- **Naming a relationship gap:** "I realize I haven't built much of a relationship with you and I'd like to fix that. Can we get coffee?"

## Source Notes

The positional-vs-personal-power frame is from HBR's Manager's Handbook chapter on "Becoming a Person of Influence" (Skill 5). The Handbook also provides the explicit manage-up, partner-with-peers, silo-busting, and idea-promotion sub-skills. Goleman's emotional-intelligence work (HBR Vol. 1) provides the deeper substrate: social skill ("managing relationships to move people in desired directions") is one of the five components of EI and is the most directly relevant to influence.

## Open Questions

- The Manager's Handbook prescription is largely Western corporate. Does the manage-up / promote-your-ideas pattern translate to cultures with stronger hierarchy norms (Japan, Korea, parts of continental Europe)?
- How much influence work is *too* much? At what point does relationship investment start to read as self-promotion?
- For introverted leaders, what is the most effective form of influence-building that does not require sustained social energy? (Written promotion of ideas, async network maintenance, depth-over-breadth relationship investment.)
