# Crisis Leadership

## Use Case

The organization faces a sudden material threat — external (market collapse, regulatory action, reputational incident, system failure) or internal (product failure, ethical lapse, leadership departure, governance failure). The standard operating procedures are insufficient; the leader is now in personal exposure; many people are watching every move; and the wrong response in the first 72 hours can compound the damage for years.

## Diagnosis

You are in a crisis (not just a problem) when:

- **The cost of inaction now exceeds the cost of imperfect action.** Decision-paralysis is itself a failure mode.
- **The organization's continued viability is genuinely at risk.** Not "we'll miss the quarter"; "we may not exist in this form a year from now."
- **The leader is personally in the spotlight.** Internal and external audiences are watching every signal, including non-verbal ones.
- **The information available is incomplete and adversarial.** Media, regulators, plaintiffs' attorneys are constructing their own narratives.
- **The Cynefin domain is Chaotic** ([[principles/cynefin-decision-framework]]) — no clear pattern, immediate threat, need to stabilize before analyzing.

If only one or two of these conditions hold, you have a hard problem, not a crisis. The playbook below is calibrated for the full crisis.

## First Principles

- **The crisis is the *defining moment*.** Crises do not reveal a leader's quality; they *forge* it. The leader who emerges with reputation and authority intact is the leader who can lead what comes next.
- **Failed leadership, not failed circumstances, causes most crisis-damage.** External shocks are the catalyst; internal denial and slow response are what destroy the firm. Bear Stearns, Lehman, Citigroup in 2008 — the catalyst was the same; the response was the differentiator.
- **The leader's first job is to define reality** (Max DePree). Before acting, before communicating, before recovery — the leader must acknowledge that the crisis is real, what its dimensions are, and what their own role in it is.
- **You cannot get through it alone.** The Atlas posture (carrying the problem in silence) is the most reliable way to lose the firm. Reach out to your team, your board, your peers, your support network — early, not late.
- **Survivability first.** Cash, continuity, communication. ROE, growth, optics second.
- **Use the crisis.** When stabilized, use the crisis to make changes that would have been politically impossible in calm times.

## Step-by-Step Approach

### Hours 1–24: Stabilize

1. **Acknowledge the crisis internally.** Convene the executive team. Say plainly: *"We are in a crisis. Here is what we know. Here is what we don't know. We are going to work this together."* Resist the temptation to project false confidence.
2. **Establish a single command structure.** One incident commander. Clear roles for legal, communications, operations, finance, HR. The matrix that runs the company in normal times is too slow for crisis pace.
3. **Take cash and continuity actions immediately.** Whatever your business equivalent of "ensure we can operate next week" is — do it now. Goldman in early 2007 marked subprime to market daily and exited the business while peers kept dancing.
4. **Issue first internal communication.** Even with incomplete information. The vacuum will be filled by rumor if you don't fill it. *Format: what we know / what we don't know / what we are doing / when we'll update again.*
5. **Issue first external communication if required.** Coordinate with legal. Acknowledge reality. Avoid speculation. Commit to a next-update cadence.

### Days 1–14: Dig Deep

6. **Find the root cause, not the symptoms.** Crises have long roots (Mulcahy: "ten to fifteen years"). Pull the thread. Honeywell's $25M overrun was the tip of a $450M iceberg. The temptation to declare resolution prematurely will be intense; resist it. Better to disclose progressively ("tip of the iceberg, investigating further") than to declare a number that proves wrong.
7. **Trust but verify.** Direct contact with the front line. Don't rely solely on what the hierarchy is reporting up. (Terry Lundgren at Macy's pops into stores unannounced; George at Medtronic spent a thousand hours in operating rooms.)
8. **Engage truth-tellers; protect them.** Jamie Dimon: *"If you have ten people around you and only one of them is telling you the truth, you have a real problem, because everyone has to do it."* Publicly thank the messenger of bad news. The cost is paid by everyone watching.
9. **Maintain communication cadence.** Stakeholders need to hear from you on a predictable schedule, even when nothing has changed. *"We are still investigating. We expect to update by [date]. We are committed to full transparency as soon as the picture stabilizes."*
10. **Run the support team.** Lean on your spouse / mentor / peer group / board. This is the moment they were assembled for. Don't perform invulnerability in private — you'll perform it in public too.

### Weeks 2–12: Stabilize and Reframe

11. **Resist declaring premature victory.** "We're through the worst" is the statement most likely to be falsified in week 14. Hold the position of cautious optimism without false confidence.
12. **Accept responsibility.** Not "the contractors failed us" (Mattel); not "I made decisions with the information I had" (Lehman); but *"we owe this to our customers / employees / investors and we are accountable."* JetBlue's David Neeleman model: video apology, customer Bill of Rights, public accountability. (He still lost the CEO role; but he kept the company's reputation.)
13. **Reframe the crisis into an opportunity (Lesson 5 from George).** What change in the organization was previously politically impossible that is now possible? Shed weaknesses. Restructure. Reset expectations. Welch created a crisis at GE to do this; Wendy Kopp used the 1995 TFA near-collapse to do this; Medtronic used the 1998 growth slowdown to transform into the world's leading med-tech company.
14. **Go on offense.** Cease defensive posture. Reshape the market to play to your strengths. Goldman, Intel, and Exxon all *invested* during their respective downturns and emerged dominant.

### Months 3–12: Recover and Transform

15. **Build the new operating model.** What changed permanently? What temporary measures should become permanent? What policies, structures, and roles need to be re-architected for the post-crisis environment?
16. **Run the post-mortem rigorously.** Not "who's to blame" but "what was the actual sequence of failures, and what system change prevents recurrence?" Medtronic's defective-batteries crisis of the 1970s recurred a decade later because the post-mortem didn't reach the root cause.
17. **Restore organizational energy.** People are exhausted. Celebrate the team. Acknowledge the cost. Re-orient toward the future.

## Suggested Language

- **First internal address (Day 1):** "We are in a crisis. Here is what I know: [3-5 facts]. Here is what I don't know: [3-5 unknowns]. Here is what we are doing in the next 48 hours: [3 actions]. I will update everyone at [time]. I need each of you to [specific ask]. Bring me your worst news first — we don't have time for filtered information."

- **Receiving bad news:** "Thank you for bringing this. What else do I need to know? What do you need from me to address this?"

- **First external statement:** "We are aware of [event]. We are taking [actions]. We are committed to [outcome]. We will provide our next update at [time]. We acknowledge that [stakeholders] need [specific reassurance], and our commitment to them is [specific]."

- **When asked something you don't know:** "I don't know. I will find out and follow up by [time]."

- **When tempted to declare victory prematurely:** "We've made progress on [specific items]. We have not concluded that the underlying situation is resolved. Here is what we are continuing to monitor: [list]."

- **Accepting responsibility:** "We — and I personally — own this. The systems and decisions that led here were ours. We will fix the system, we will be accountable, and we will not repeat this."

- **Asking for support:** "I need your help. Specifically: [what]. I cannot do this alone." (Said to the right small group, not in public.)

## What To Avoid

- **Hunkering down.** Going quiet to avoid saying the wrong thing. The vacuum will be filled by adversaries.
- **Premature declarations.** "We're through the worst." "This is contained." "We've identified the root cause." Each is a hostage to fortune.
- **Blame-shifting.** Mattel's "Chinese contract manufacturers" framing made things worse and damaged the brand. Even when the blame is partially elsewhere, you own the response.
- **Atlas posture.** Carrying the problem alone. Performing invulnerability. Refusing help.
- **Cutting too little.** When cash is the priority, half-measures consume the optionality required for recovery. Better to cut once, completely, and recover than to salami-slice and erode confidence.
- **Wasting the crisis.** Recovering to the *prior* state instead of using the disruption to reach a better state.
- **Conflating the technical and adaptive layers.** Many crises have *both* a technical layer (which requires fast action) and an adaptive layer (which requires diagnosis and pacing — see [[sources/heifetz-adaptive-leadership-2009]]). Handling one without the other is a common failure mode. Bear Stearns failed on technical action; many post-crisis reorganizations fail on adaptive work.

## Escalation Path

If the standard playbook is failing:
- The crisis is becoming Chaotic and stabilization is not holding → bring in outside crisis-management expertise; sequester legal-, comms-, and operations-decisions to separate teams.
- The leader is becoming the story → the board must consider whether new leadership is required to lead the recovery. (Mulcahy at Xerox, Gerstner at IBM, Mulally at Ford — successor CEOs whose first move was to acknowledge what the prior CEO could not.)
- The fact pattern is metastasizing (new revelations every week) → assume the iceberg is deeper than known; commission an independent investigation.

## Follow-Up Actions

After the crisis is resolved:
- **Run the structural post-mortem.** Not "who," but "what system."
- **Identify the changes that *should* persist** (operating model, comms cadence, decision rights).
- **Re-form your personal support network.** It paid for itself; reinforce it for the next time.
- **Refresh resilience practices.** Crisis exhausts. Recovery is intentional.
- **Write the case.** While memory is fresh, document what worked, what didn't, and what you'd do differently. This becomes institutional knowledge.
