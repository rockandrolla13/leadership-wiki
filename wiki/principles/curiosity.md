# Curiosity

## Definition

Curiosity is the deliberate practice of asking questions about things you do not yet understand — about your work, your colleagues, yourself, and the world — and following those questions with action. As a leadership skill, it is the disposition that produces better questions, better learning, better networks, and better judgments under uncertainty.

It is not a personality trait you either have or don't. Andersen's research is explicit: aspiration, self-awareness, curiosity, and vulnerability are *coachable through self-talk*. The shift is from "this is boring" to "I wonder why others find it interesting?"

## Why It Matters

The half-life of skills is now under five years (HBR 2025 reskilling article). A leader who cannot make themselves curious about a new domain — AI, a new market, a new function — will be obsolete by the next cycle. Beyond individual learning, curiosity is the disposition behind:

- **Better strategic decisions** (HBR 2026): the five-domain question framework requires you to ask questions in categories where you have no current expertise.
- **Better networks** (Kuwabara et al.): a growth mindset about relationships — and the question-asking that comes with it — predicts diverse, productive networks. A fixed "chemistry or nothing" mindset halves your bridge-building.
- **Better conflict resolution** (Gallo): "Certainty is the enemy of change" (Minuchin, cited by Gallo). Curiosity replaces "this person is the problem" with "what's going on for them?"
- **Better feedback receiving** (Heen & Stone): unpacking criticism requires curiosity about where it comes from and where it goes, before reacting.

Without curiosity, leaders default to pattern-matching from the past — which works until the pattern breaks.

## When It Applies

- In any role involving knowledge work, especially as senior leaders accumulate the temptation to "have answers."
- When working with people who think, look, or work differently from you.
- When evaluating a domain you don't know yet (AI, regulation, a new function you'll lead, a culture you're entering).
- After a hard feedback moment when your first instinct is to reject the message.
- When stuck — curiosity is the move that breaks rumination.
- When the team is in a petting-zoo state (consensus too quickly; HBR 2026): curiosity is the antidote to suppressed dissent.

## Key Principles

- **Coachable, not fixed.** Andersen's central claim: the *desire* to learn is malleable. Shift "I don't need to learn this" → "What would my future look like if I did?" Shift "This is boring" → "I wonder why others find it interesting?"
- **Five question domains** (Chevallier, Dalsace, Barsoux):
  - *Investigative* — what's known?
  - *Speculative* — what if?
  - *Productive* — now what?
  - *Interpretive* — so what?
  - *Subjective* — what's unsaid?
  Most leaders default to two domains and miss the rest. The discipline is to deliberately add the ones you skip.
- **Follow-up questions outperform new questions.** Brooks & John: people interacting with a follow-up-heavy questioner feel "respected and heard" and disclose more.
- **Tone matters as much as content.** "How come?" lands differently from "Why?" — the latter often sounds like accusation in performance contexts.
- **Curiosity protects against confirmation bias.** Gallo: once you stop being curious about someone's behaviour, you start interpreting all of it through your existing model.
- **Curiosity in relationships is a growth-mindset proxy.** Kuwabara et al.'s five-statement scale measures whether you think relationships require effort or just chemistry. The growth view leads to deliberate, diverse networking; the fixed view leads to echo chambers.
- **Curiosity needs vulnerability.** Andersen: being willing to be the novice — to ask the "dumb" question — is the price of admission to new learning.

## Practical Behaviors

A curious leader:

- **Asks one follow-up question** for every assertion they make in a 1:1.
- **Schedules a regular "what's new?" conversation** with someone outside their function or hierarchy — Dyer/Gregersen/Christensen's "idea networking."
- **In meetings, asks the missing-domain question.** If everyone is investigative ("what's the data?"), they bring the speculative ("what if we did the opposite?") or the subjective ("what isn't being said?").
- **Catches themselves in "I already know" or "this won't apply to me" reactions** — and asks "what would I have to believe for this to matter?"
- **In conflict, asks "what's going on for them?"** before "how do I push back?"
- **Receives feedback by unpacking, not defending.** "Tell me more about that. When did you first notice it? What specifically would you have done differently?"
- **In a new domain (new function, new technology), commits to one beginner step** — a class, a one-hour conversation with an expert, a book — within a week of identifying the gap.
- **Resists closure.** When the team converges too fast on an answer, asks "what would have to be true for the opposite answer to be right?"
- **Notices when self-talk is closing curiosity** ("I'm bad at this," "I'll never get this") and reframes ("I'm new at this, and I'll get better with practice").

## Common Failure Modes

- **Curiosity-as-stalling.** Asking endless questions to avoid making a decision. Curiosity should converge on action, not replace it.
- **Performative curiosity.** Asking questions to look engaged while not listening to answers. Brooks & John's data: people can tell.
- **Interrogation-mode questions.** "Why did you do it that way?" with the wrong tone shuts down the answer. The same content, asked as "How come you took that approach?" or "Walk me through your reasoning," opens it.
- **Closed questions disguised as open.** "Don't you think we should…?" is a statement.
- **Curiosity in self-talk but not in conversation.** You wonder about something but never ask. The information stays in your head and the decision stays uninformed.
- **Specialist contempt.** Drucker's "disabling ignorance" — being curious about your own domain but dismissive of others. First-rate engineers proud of not knowing anything about people, etc.
- **One question too many.** In sensitive moments, the third "tell me more" can feel intrusive. Read the room.

## Diagnostic Questions

- In my last 1:1, what fraction of the time was I asking versus telling?
- Which of the five question domains (investigative, speculative, productive, interpretive, subjective) do I skip most often?
- When I disagree with someone, do I get curious about why they disagree, or do I prepare a rebuttal?
- When did I last be a novice at something professionally — really not good at it?
- Which of my colleagues do I assume I already understand, and might I be wrong about?
- When was the last time I changed my mind based on a question I asked?
- In meetings with junior people, am I asking their views, or am I performing seniority?

## Useful Scripts

- **In place of "Why did you do that?":** "Walk me through what you were thinking when you decided to do that."
- **To open a coaching conversation:** "What's been on your mind? Anything you've been trying to figure out?"
- **To break a too-fast consensus:** "Let me ask the dumb question — why are we doing this at all? What would have to change for it to be a bad idea?"
- **When receiving criticism:** "Help me understand. When you say [X], what specifically did you see?"
- **To a new colleague:** "What do you wish more people here asked you about your work?"
- **In a stuck meeting:** "We've been answering the same question for an hour. Is it the right question? What question would unstick us?"
- **In self-talk, when bored or resistant:** "I wonder why other smart people find this interesting?"
- **To a network contact:** "What's something you're working on right now that you wish more people understood?"

## Open Questions

- Curiosity in senior roles can feel like indecision to subordinates expecting answers. How do you signal that you are curious-and-still-decisive?
- Andersen's claim that curiosity is coachable is supported by lab evidence, but durability is less clear. Does the new self-talk hold under pressure, or revert?
- The five-domain question framework is well-supported for strategic decisions. Does it apply at the tactical or interpersonal level? Probably yes but less rigorously.
- Asking too many questions can be culturally read as evasion ("doesn't this person have a view?"). Where is the right balance, and is it culture-dependent?
