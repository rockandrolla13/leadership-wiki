# Psychological Safety

## Definition

Psychological safety is the shared belief that the team is safe for interpersonal risk-taking — that asking a question, admitting an error, disagreeing with a senior person, or surfacing a half-formed idea will not be punished. In the wiki's terms, it is the operating condition under which feedback flows in all directions and bad news travels as fast as good news.

## Why It Matters

When psychological safety is absent, the data a leader needs travels slowly or not at all. People withhold the dissenting view in the meeting, then have it in the hallway. Engineers ship work they suspect is wrong because surfacing the doubt costs more than the rework. As Casciaro, Edmondson and Jang put it in HBR Vol. 2:

> "Not asking questions is a big mistake many professionals make. Expert advisers want to offer a solution. That's what they're trained to do."

Lopp ties the same idea to feedback specifically: people are "extremely slow to trust" and the leader has to demonstrate, repeatedly, that feedback in all directions is no big deal before anyone will give it.

## When It Applies

- New team formation, especially when an [[organization/informal-organization]] "Old Guard" and "New Guard" coexist and the New Guard has slipped into learned helplessness.
- Cross-silo work, where what is obvious on one side of an interface is invisible on the other.
- Post-incident reviews, where the cheapest information lives in the throat of the person who saw it coming.
- Any [[communication/difficult-conversations]] where the receiver outranks the speaker.
- Performance discussions — see [[principles/accountability]] — where the cost of disagreement feels like the cost of a job.

## Key Principles

- Safety is built by behaviors, not by posters. It is created in 1:1s, staff meetings, and the way a leader receives the first piece of hard feedback they get.
- Safety is asymmetric: the senior person has to spend the cost first. Asking the team to "be open" while punishing the first open person erodes safety faster than silence would.
- Safety is not niceness. Lopp is explicit that "saying the hard thing" is a sibling of safety, not its opposite — the goal is "to make all feedback in all directions no big deal."
- Humility is structural. Bock (cited in HBR) calls humility a strength because it produces realism: "if we don't work together, we don't stand a chance."

## Practical Behaviors

- Ask "Do you have any feedback for me?" at the end of every 1:1, and keep asking even when the answer is no — Lopp notes it took a former CEO a year before they took him up on the offer.
- When the first piece of critical feedback arrives, adopt a listening posture (Lopp: "crossed legs, folded hands, head slightly tilted") and resist the brain's "high alert" defense.
- Repeat back what you heard before responding. This both clarifies and signals that the feedback was received.
- Start meetings with open-ended questions ("What does your group see as the key opportunity here?") rather than yes/no questions that close down the room.
- Acknowledge publicly when you do not know the answer. HBR cites Bock: leaders who openly admit ignorance license everyone below them to do the same.
- Run cross-silo dialogues like Children's Minnesota's "focused event analysis" — after a failure, each function tells its version of events before any cause is named.
- Never punish the messenger of bad news in front of others. The cost is paid by everyone watching.

## Common Failure Modes

- Confusing safety with consensus. Safety is the right to disagree; it does not mean every disagreement is resolved by accommodation.
- Asking for feedback once, getting silence, and concluding the team has none. They have it; they are testing you.
- Reacting defensively to the first piece of hard feedback. Lopp: "the giver is taking a risk because you're the boss and they've seen prior bosses lose their s—t when they've received feedback."
- Treating safety as a feature of the room rather than of the relationships in it. Offsite trust falls do not create it; weekly 1:1s and visible follow-through on feedback do.
- Confusing personal warmth with safety. The "people person" manager (Katzenbach & Khan) who is easy to like can still produce a team that hides problems because they like the manager too much to disappoint them.

## Diagnostic Questions

- When was the last time someone on the team told me I was wrong, and what happened to them afterward?
- Do bad-news items show up in 1:1s, or only after they have become incidents?
- Who on the team has not spoken in the last three meetings, and is that because they have nothing to say or because the room is not safe for them to say it?
- When I ask "any feedback for me?", do I get specifics or pleasantries?
- After the last incident, did anyone surface that they saw it coming? If not, why not?

## Useful Scripts

- **Inviting first feedback:** "Do you have any feedback for me? I'm not looking for anything in particular — and I'm going to keep asking, so you don't have to come up with something today."
- **Receiving hard feedback:** "Just so I'm clear what you're saying — you're saying that I didn't perform well at the all-hands?" (Lopp's "repeat what you heard" technique.)
- **Admitting ignorance in a meeting:** "I don't know the answer to that. What do you see?"
- **Inviting dissent:** "Before we land on this — what does this plan look like from where you sit? What am I missing?"
- **Post-incident:** "Let's go around the room. I want each function's version of what happened before we try to name a cause."

## Source Notes

- Lopp ([[sources/lopp-art-of-leadership-2020]]) does not use the term "psychological safety" but the chapters "Say the Hard Thing" and "Trust Falls for Everyone!" describe exactly the same mechanism: feedback as a slow trust-building loop, anchored in 1:1s.
- HBR Vol. 2 ([[sources/hbr-leadership-vol2-2020]]) uses the term directly in "Cross-Silo Leadership," tying it to the leader's role-modeling of inquiry: "leaders can encourage inquiry in two important ways — and in the process help create an organization where it's psychologically safe to ask questions."
- Katzenbach & Khan ([[sources/katzenbach-khan-leading-outside-lines-2010]]) approach the same territory through "pride-builders" — managers who hold high standards while making it safe to bring half-finished ideas forward.

## Open Questions

- How does psychological safety scale beyond ~30 people, where the leader can no longer model it personally in every relationship? Lopp's "Rule of 3 and 10" suggests the mechanism breaks at predictable thresholds, but the wiki has no playbook yet for re-establishing it at scale.
- How do you build safety with senior ICs who have been burned by previous regimes? See [[management/managing-senior-people]].
