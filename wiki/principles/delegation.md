# Delegation

## Definition

Delegation is the act of entrusting a task or responsibility to another person, with sufficient authority and context for them to complete it. The key word is *entrust* — genuine delegation requires a transfer of both the work and the decision-making authority, not just the task with strings attached.

## Why It Matters

A manager who cannot delegate does not scale. Their output is bounded by their own capacity. Worse: a manager who does not delegate signals to the team that the team is not trusted, stifles their development, and occupies time that should be spent building the conditions for others to succeed. The manager's job is not to do quality work; it is to build a team that does quality work at scale.

## When It Applies

- Any time work lands on your plate that someone on your team could do with coaching or guidance
- When a team member is ready for a stretch assignment
- When you are overloaded (and especially when you are *not* overloaded — proactive delegation builds capacity before a crisis)
- When you find yourself doing the same type of work you were doing before your promotion

## Key Principles

- **Accountability ≠ Responsibility.** The manager is *accountable* — they can explain what happened and what will be done about it. The team member is *responsible* for executing. Conflating the two leads to the manager doing the work.
- **A B on a delegated project is better leadership than an A on a solo project.** The team member gained experience. Trust formed. Next time, the probability of an A goes up.
- **Half-delegation is not delegation.** Giving someone the task without the authority and context produces predictable failure and erodes trust twice — once because they failed, once because you set them up to.
- **Delegation scales with leadership level.** Manager: small, well-defined projects with significant oversight. Senior Manager: significant projects with some oversight. Director: large projects with little oversight. Executive: large, complex, cross-functional projects with little oversight.

## Practical Behaviors

- Before taking a task yourself, ask: "Who on my team could do this with guidance?" Then delegate it and provide the guidance.
- When delegating, provide: the goal, what winning looks like, resourcing, timeline, and an invitation for questions.
- Do not solve problems that surface mid-delegation. Coach instead. Tell the story of how you solved a similar problem; let them work through their version.
- When the project ends imperfectly, debrief on what went well and what to do differently — not on whether you should have done it yourself.
- Schedule delegation check-ins, not delegation oversight. The distinction: check-ins are about helping, oversight is about control.

## Common Failure Modes

- **Half-delegation:** Assigning the work but retaining decisions. The delegatee can't succeed because they don't have authority to act.
- **Reclaiming the work mid-stream:** Taking back a struggling project is the fastest way to teach the team to wait for rescue rather than solve problems.
- **Delegating tasks without context:** "Go figure out the API design" without explaining the product goals, the trade-offs already considered, or the constraints produces rework and confusion.
- **Delegating only the undesirable work:** If you only hand off tasks you don't want, you are not developing people — you are offloading.
- **The instinct: "I could do this better."** True, possibly. Irrelevant. Your job is not to produce the best single piece of work; it is to produce leaders who produce good work at scale.

## Diagnostic Questions

- When was the last time I delegated something that made me uncomfortable?
- Is there work on my plate today that someone on my team could do with coaching?
- If I looked at my calendar this week, what percentage of the time did I spend on work that only I can do?
- Have I handed off entire projects, or only tasks within projects I control?
- When something goes wrong on a delegated project, is my first reaction to coach or to take back control?

## Useful Scripts

- **When delegating a stretch project:** "This is a stretch for the team, and I know it. I'm giving it to you because I trust you can do it, and I'll be here to coach you through the parts that get hard. Let's start by talking through what winning looks like."
- **When a delegated project struggles:** "Walk me through where things stand. What's working? What's getting hard? Have you seen a situation like this before?" — then share a relevant story from your own experience.
- **When reclaiming feels tempting:** (Say to yourself) "If I take this back, what have I taught them? What have I given up?"
- **When they deliver a B:** "Here's what worked and what I'd do differently next time. You got further than I expected. Now you know what this looks like from the inside."

## Source Notes

Lopp (2020) calls Delegation the most important leadership merit badge, regardless of role. His central argument: earning it means you've learned that your job is to build a team capable of doing quality work, not to do quality work yourself. He describes half-delegation (assigning work without authority) as a specific failure pattern that produces failure and erodes trust. The progression from manager to executive is described explicitly as a ladder of increasing delegation scope and decreasing oversight.
