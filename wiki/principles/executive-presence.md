# Executive Presence

## Definition

Executive presence is the ability to command attention, inspire confidence, and influence others at scale — in large groups, across functions, and through indirect communication channels. It is not a fixed personality trait; it is a set of learnable behaviors and mindsets that evolve as leaders move to larger roles.

## Why It Matters

At the executive level, the leader's influence is primarily indirect. They cannot be in every room or personally supervise every decision. Presence is what allows their values, standards, and direction to propagate through people who then spread it further. Without it, strategy lives in documents but not in behavior.

## When It Applies

Executive presence is most critical during:
- Leadership transitions (moving from functional leader to enterprise leader)
- Moments of organizational uncertainty or change
- Large-group communications (all-hands, board meetings, cross-functional forums)
- Situations where the leader must inspire without being the decision-maker

## Key Principles

- **Presence is not personality.** Charisma and executive presence are not the same thing. Presence is the ability to project authority and authenticity in ways that are appropriate to context — which can be learned and calibrated.
- **Authority and approachability exist in tension.** (Ibarra, HBR) Too much approachability (e.g., sharing vulnerability prematurely in a new role) undermines credibility. Too much authority creates distance that stifles information flow. Finding the right balance requires reading the context and the audience.
- **Adaptively authentic ≠ fake.** Learning new behaviors that don't feel natural is not inauthenticity; it is growth. Leaders who insist on behaving only in ways that "feel natural" are using authenticity as an excuse to avoid development. True authenticity is a "work in progress" — a process of trying behaviors, observing results, and keeping what works.
- **The seven seismic shifts** (Watkins, HBR): Moving from functional leader to enterprise leader requires becoming: specialist → generalist, analyst → integrator, tactician → strategist, bricklayer → architect, problem solver → agenda setter, warrior → diplomat, supporting cast member → lead role. Each shift requires new skills and a new mindset, not just more of the same.
- **Role model effect is magnified at the executive level.** Every word, tone, and decision you make is observed and interpreted for signal. The things that worked in a functional role — depth of technical engagement, proximity, personal relationships — do not scale. New mechanisms (video, brown-bag sessions, clear narratives) must replace them.

## Practical Behaviors

- Manage the authority-approachability balance explicitly in new roles: err toward authority initially, then move toward approachability as credibility is established
- Experiment with different leadership styles rather than locking into one; treat leadership development as "trying on possible selves"
- Replace direct supervision with system design: who controls what, how decisions are escalated, what metrics surface what information
- At the enterprise level: carve out time for the six strategic leadership skills — Anticipate, Challenge, Interpret, Decide, Align, Learn — rather than defaulting to tactical execution
- Create mechanisms for frontline information to reach you: brown-bag lunches with frontline employees, skip-level conversations, direct engagement at site visits

## Common Failure Modes

- **Oversharing vulnerability in a new role.** Being too open about uncertainty before building credibility undermines it. First build the credibility; then be appropriately transparent.
- **Staying in functional comfort zones.** Enterprise leaders who over-manage the function they know and under-manage the others (classic trap in the specialist → generalist shift).
- **Too tactical, not enough strategic.** Being seduced by the concreteness of tactical work. The executive's job is to define what the organization should be working on, not to solve the problems themselves.
- **Confusing authenticity with rigidity.** Using "that's not my style" as a reason to avoid behavior that the role requires. Style must evolve with level; the alternative is being outrun by the role.
- **Role model signal neglect.** Not realizing that every visible action — a casual aside, a tone in a meeting, a decision made under pressure — broadcasts to the entire organization what is actually valued.

## Diagnostic Questions

- When I walk into a meeting, what does my presence signal to the room?
- Am I managing the authority-approachability tension intentionally, or defaulting to one end?
- Which of the seven seismic shifts (specialist → generalist, etc.) am I struggling with most?
- What information do I not have because the hierarchy filtered it out? How am I addressing that?
- What do my frontline employees think I believe? Is that what I actually believe?

## Useful Scripts

- **When entering a new senior role:** "I'm going to be listening hard for the first 60 days. I want to understand what's working, what's at risk, and what you need from me before I start making changes."
- **When facing a question outside your functional expertise:** "I'm not deep enough in this area to have a confident opinion yet. What are the two or three things I should understand about this before forming a view?"
- **When getting feedback that you're too distant:** "I've heard that I'm not as accessible as people need me to be. Tell me where the gap is — is it about physical access, or about feeling like your concerns are heard?"
- **When you need to be more confident in uncertainty:** "I don't have full certainty about how this will unfold, but here's the logic of why I believe this is the right direction, and here's what I'll be watching to know if I'm wrong."

## Source Notes

Ibarra (HBR 2015, "The Authenticity Paradox"): Authenticity is not about always expressing your natural preferences — it's about developing an evolving leadership identity through experience and experimentation. The "true-to-selfer" who refuses to change style because it doesn't feel authentic stagnates. The "chameleon" who experiments and adapts advances faster, though risks being perceived as lacking a moral center if not anchored in genuine values. The resolution: "adaptively authentic" — a playful frame of mind that treats leadership development as trying on possible selves.

Watkins (HBR 2012, "How Managers Become Leaders"): The seven seismic shifts define the cognitive and behavioral reorientation required when moving from functional to enterprise leadership. Each shift is a distinct mindset change, not just a skill increment. The biggest risk: executives default to the functional behaviors that earned them the promotion and under-invest in the genuinely new capabilities the enterprise role requires.

Lopp (2020): Executive presence at the senior level includes "acting without asking" (doing things within your authority without waiting for permission), protecting unstructured time (the source of the most important executive thinking), and finding high-signal people in your network who will tell you the truth.

## Open Questions

- Is "adaptively authentic" a sustainable frame for leaders who change styles significantly across contexts? At some point does the chameleon lose the moral center Ibarra warns about?
- The seven seismic shifts assume a linear career path. Does the model apply to executives who arrive at enterprise leadership through founder paths rather than functional ladders?
