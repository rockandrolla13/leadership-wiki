# Seven Action Logics

## Purpose

David Rooke and William Torbert's "Seven Transformations of Leadership" (HBR April 2005, anthologized in HBR's 10 Must Reads on Leadership Vol. 1) provides a developmental framework for understanding *who a leader is becoming*, in contrast to style frameworks that describe *what they do in the moment*. Each "action logic" — how a leader interprets their surroundings and what they react to when their power is challenged — is a stable operating frame. Movement between stages is rare, slow, and developmental rather than skill-based.

## Core Idea

Through a 25-year study of thousands of leaders, Rooke and Torbert identified seven action logics. Most leaders plateau at Expert (38% of the sample) or Achiever (30%). Only about 15% reach the post-conventional stages (Individualist, Strategist, Alchemist) where leaders can drive sustained organizational transformation. The framework's central claim: a leader's *level of development* — not their style or skill set — predicts whether they can lead organizational transformation. Development is possible at any age but requires confronting one's current frame, which usually means crisis, deliberate developmental work, or both.

## The Seven Stages

| Logic | % of leaders | Defining behavior | Strengths | Limits |
|-------|--------------|-------------------|-----------|--------|
| **Opportunist** | 5% | Wins any way possible; manipulative; "might makes right" | Decisive in emergencies, good in sales | Few will follow long-term; ego-driven |
| **Diplomat** | 12% | Avoids conflict; obeys group norms; doesn't rock the boat | Supportive glue on teams | Can't give hard feedback; conflict-averse |
| **Expert** | 38% | Rules by logic and expertise; uses hard data to gain consensus | Good individual contributor | Lacks EI; disrespects others' expertise; defends being right |
| **Achiever** | 30% | Meets strategic goals; promotes teamwork; juggles managerial duties | Well-suited to managerial work | Inhibits "out of the box" thinking; defends the system that works |
| **Individualist** | 10% | Operates in unconventional ways; ignores rules they regard as irrelevant | Effective in venture and consulting roles | Irritates colleagues by ignoring key organizational processes |
| **Strategist** | 4% | Generates organizational and personal transformations; highly collaborative; weaves visions with pragmatic initiatives | Generates transformation over short and long term | (None identified in research) |
| **Alchemist** | <1% | Generates social transformations; reinvents organizations in historically significant ways | Leads society-wide change | (None identified in research) |

## Steps (How to Use the Framework)

1. **Honest self-placement.** Read the descriptions and identify the logic that best describes how you respond when your power or expertise is challenged. The answer is rarely the most flattering one.
2. **Identify the developmental transition.** The next stage is usually visible — Experts move toward Achiever by focusing on delivered results rather than perfected knowledge; Achievers move toward Individualist by questioning whether the goals themselves are right, not just whether they are met.
3. **Engineer developmental experiences.** Rooke & Torbert prescribe specific transitions:
    - **Expert → Achiever:** Focus on delivering results over perfecting knowledge. Practice phrases like "You may be right, but I'd like to understand what leads you to believe that." Take a delegation or leading-high-performing-teams course.
    - **Achiever → Individualist:** Reflect on the *worth* of the goals themselves, not just on whether they were achieved. Use annual development planning to set the highest-impact goals rather than the most-achievable ones.
    - **Individualist → Strategist:** Engage in peer-to-peer development. Establish mutual mentoring with people who can challenge your assumptions (board members, top managers, industry leaders).
4. **Check progress over years, not months.** Action-logic shifts take 18+ months. Behavioral change without underlying frame change reverts.

## When To Use

- During a leadership development planning conversation, especially for senior managers who keep doing well at the same level but cannot get promoted further.
- When you notice you keep getting the same feedback ("brilliant but doesn't collaborate"; "great at execution but no vision") despite trying behavior changes — the issue is action logic, not skill.
- For executive coaching engagements where the question is "why am I plateauing?"
- When evaluating candidates for senior roles: the role may demand a higher action logic than the candidate's prior history shows.

## When Not To Use

- As a label or scorecard. The framework is developmental, not categorical. Calling someone "an Expert" can become an excuse not to develop them.
- For first-line managers. Diplomat and Expert are appropriate developmental stages at that altitude; the framework's leverage is largely at senior-leader transitions.
- For technical performance reviews. Action logic does not predict short-term technical performance — that's what Achievers are great at.

## Example Application

A senior engineering manager has held the same level for four years despite excellent reviews. Feedback consistently notes "could be more strategic." Investigation reveals an Expert action logic: she rules by depth of technical knowledge, defends her positions with data, dismisses peers who lack her depth. The development plan:

- Coach engagement focused on the Expert → Achiever transition.
- Practiced behavior: ending more meetings with the question "what do you see that I don't?" rather than the answer she came in with.
- Stretch assignment: leading a cross-functional initiative where she lacks the deepest expertise.
- 18-month horizon. After 12 months her peers describe her as "collaborative"; after 24 she is promoted.

The mistake the prior coach made was to coach behaviors (do more 1:1s, give more credit) without naming the underlying logic. Behaviors reverted whenever stress rose.

## Failure Modes

- **Mis-placing yourself one stage too high.** Most leaders rate themselves Strategist or above. The research data says only ~5% of senior leaders are there. Honest placement is harder than it looks.
- **Treating action logics as personality.** They are *developmental* — they describe where you are *now*, not where you must remain.
- **Skipping stages.** You cannot move from Expert to Strategist without passing through Achiever and Individualist. Each transition rewires what the leader pays attention to.
- **Confusing high action logic with goodness.** Strategists and Alchemists drive transformation; they are not necessarily kinder, wiser, or more ethical than Achievers. The Opportunist column even includes some highly effective sales leaders.
- **Coaching without confrontation.** Developmental movement requires the leader to confront a frame they currently live inside. Comfortable coaching does not produce action-logic transitions.

## Adaptation For This User

For the wiki's archetype user (senior IC or manager moving up), the most relevant stages are usually **Expert (current default)**, **Achiever (next stage, often where promotions stall)**, and **Individualist (where strategic capacity opens up)**. The most useful single intervention is the Expert → Achiever practice: noticing the impulse to defend a position with expertise and substituting the practice of asking for the dissenting view first.

## Diagnostic Questions

- When my expertise is challenged, what do I do — defend, ignore, inquire, or invite?
- When I'm in a conflict, am I trying to win, avoid, prove, deliver, transform, or hold competing logics simultaneously?
- Where do I get my sense of professional identity — from being right, from achieving goals, from being unique, from generating transformation?
- Do I tend to optimize the goals I'm given, or to question whether they are the right goals?
- When a peer succeeds in a way I would not have, do I feel curious or threatened?

## Useful Scripts

- **Expert → Achiever practice:** "You may be right, but I'd like to understand what leads you to believe that. What am I missing in my framing?"
- **Achiever → Individualist practice:** "Before we commit to this goal — let's spend ten minutes on whether it's the right goal. What would matter most a year from now if we got it perfectly?"
- **Individualist → Strategist practice:** "I'd like to set up a regular conversation with [board member / senior peer] where the agenda is challenging my assumptions, not aligning on tactics."
- **Defending position vs. surfacing logic:** "I notice I'm defending my answer. Let me try again — here is the logic I'm using; tell me where it breaks down."
