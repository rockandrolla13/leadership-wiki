# Workplace Courage

## Definition

Workplace courage is the willingness to take action you believe is right despite real risk to reputation, relationships, or position. Detert's research is explicit: most acts of workplace courage do not come from whistleblowers or martyrs — they come from respected insiders who have built credibility, choose their battles, manage the persuasion well, and follow up to preserve relationships. It is a competence, not a personality trait, and the people who succeed at it are *competently courageous*.

## Why It Matters

Many of the highest-leverage moments in a leader's career require courage: speaking truth to a more senior leader; challenging a flawed strategy; naming a values violation; standing up for a colleague; making an unpopular bet; refusing to participate in something ethically dubious. The absence of courage is a quiet but enormous tax on organisations — the harassment that goes unreported, the strategy that goes uncorrected, the underperformer who stays on the team for years.

Kouchaki & Smith's ethical-careers framing makes the bigger point: ethical lapses are usually not about whether you have good values — most people do — but about whether you have the practical mechanisms (if-then plans, mentors, ethical fitness) to act on them when it costs you.

## When It Applies

- When you see behaviour that violates your values and silence would be complicity.
- When a strategic decision is being made on bad reasoning and you have evidence it is wrong.
- When a colleague is being treated unfairly.
- When you are asked to do something that crosses your ethical line.
- When a culture of toxic positivity or "petting zoo" consensus is suppressing necessary dissent.
- In your own life: when ambition, "should," or fear of disapproval is keeping you in a role that does not fit your values.

## Key Principles

### Competent Courage (Detert) — four phases

- **Lay the groundwork.** Build idiosyncrasy credits — a track record of competence, fit, and reliability — before you spend them. Build options (financial cushion, network, external visibility) so the cost of failure is bounded. Catherine Gill at Root Capital spent two years excelling and demonstrating commitment before launching a culture conversation that her credits could support.
- **Choose your battles.** Two questions: is this really important? Is this the right time? Mandy at the apparel company waited six months before challenging a bad vendor relationship — she had relationships built up, evidence collected, alternative vendors identified.
- **Persuade in the moment.** Frame the issue in terms the audience cares about. Use data, not assertion. Manage your emotions (Erik at the multinational reminding himself executives' defensiveness was "fear of the unknown"). Mel Exon at BBH framed an internal innovation lab as alignment with the firm's contrarian DNA, not a critique of it.
- **Follow up.** Repair relationships with those who might feel attacked. Persist if the first attempt didn't succeed. Fred Keller at Cascade Engineering relaunched a welfare-to-career program after the first version failed; the second version succeeded.

### Ethical Career (Kouchaki & Smith)

- **Moral humility.** Recognise that you have the capacity to transgress under pressure. Don't trust your future self to "just do the right thing."
- **If-then plans (implementation intentions).** "If my boss asks me to massage this number, then I will…" Specific, voiced aloud, decided before you encounter the situation.
- **Three decision-time tests:** *Publicity* (would I be comfortable seeing this on the front page?); *Generalizability* (would I be comfortable if everyone in this situation did this?); *Mirror* (would I like the person I see tomorrow?). If the answer to any is no, slow down.
- **Don't be rushed.** People are more likely to act unethically under time pressure. Use the delay to consult mentors or sleep on it.

### Voice in Conversation (Su)

- **Voice of character** is the constant. "Give the benefit of the doubt." "Be direct with respect." "Focus on what's best for the business." When you walk into a hard conversation, the voice of character is what keeps you from drifting into fight-or-flight reactions.

### Self-management Courage (Drucker, Christensen)

- **Walking away is courageous.** Drucker left investment banking in the 1930s because his values didn't match. Dorsey: in some situations the moxie move is to walk away, not to keep climbing the wrong hill.
- **"Just this once" is a slippery slope.** Christensen: "It's easier to hold to your principles 100% of the time than it is to hold to them 98% of the time."

## Practical Behaviors

- **Audit your idiosyncrasy credits** before a big stand. Are you stocked with this audience, or have you recently spent down your account?
- **Identify your top three predictable ethical pressures** for your current role. Write if-then plans for each. Voice them aloud or to a trusted peer.
- **Identify one or two ethical mentors** outside your reporting chain. Tell them what you're working on; ask for advice when you face a hard call.
- **Before a difficult ask, walk through Detert's four phases.** Have I built the credit? Is this the right battle and timing? How will I frame it? What is my follow-up?
- **In emotionally heated moments,** invoke the voice of character (Su): "Right now, what kind of person do I want to be in this room?"
- **Notice the small daily courage moments.** Detert's Workplace Courage Acts Index: many situations require courage (telling your boss a hard truth, defending a junior colleague, naming an uncomfortable observation in a meeting) and most go unrecognised as courage.
- **Start with the smaller acts of courage,** not the largest. Build the muscle before you face the test.

## Common Failure Modes

- **Spending credits before you have them.** A new joiner who challenges the senior team in the first month rarely succeeds, regardless of how right they are. Build first, spend later.
- **Battle selection failure.** Picking the wrong fight — too small (you spend credits on something not worth it) or too large (you take on the entire institution and lose).
- **Bad timing.** Right issue, wrong moment. Wait for the moment of organisational receptivity (a leadership change, an external trigger).
- **All sincerity, no framing.** Speaking truth is necessary but not sufficient. The Mel Exon move — connecting the proposal to existing values — multiplies impact.
- **No follow-up.** A single act of courage rarely changes a system. Most successful courage is sustained over months, with multiple touch-points.
- **Self-righteousness disguised as courage.** Detert's research is clear: the people who succeed are competent and respected; the people who fail and pay the highest price often lack that foundation. Self-righteous public stands without credits, framing, or follow-up usually fail.
- **Ethical exhaustion.** Doing the right thing constantly is tiring. Build the if-then plans so the moments don't require fresh willpower.
- **Confusing comfort with safety.** A "safe" choice that violates your values isn't actually safe — Christensen's marginal-cost compounding will catch you later.

## Diagnostic Questions

- What is one thing I have been wanting to say at work for the last 90 days and haven't? What's the actual cost of saying it?
- What is my current idiosyncrasy-credit balance with the audience I would need to persuade?
- What are the two or three predictable ethical pressures in my current role? Do I have a plan?
- If a junior person on my team faced what I'm facing, what would I tell them to do?
- Am I rushing this decision? Is there time to consult someone or sleep on it?
- If this went on the front page tomorrow, would I be comfortable?
- Have I built the small-courage habit, or am I trying to start at the highest level?

## Useful Scripts

- **Naming an uncomfortable observation in a meeting:** "I want to flag something that I think we're not addressing directly. [Observation]. I might be wrong about this, but I want to put it on the table."
- **Pushing back on a senior leader's plan:** "I want to make sure I understand what you're proposing — and then I want to share a concern I have. [Restate]. The concern is [specific]. Here's what I'd suggest instead."
- **Refusing an unethical request:** "I'm not going to do that. I want to be straight with you about why. [Reason]. I'd like to find a different way to solve the underlying problem you're worried about."
- **Defending a colleague who has been mischaracterised:** "I want to add context to what was just said about [name]. [Specific observation]. I think that's important for us to be accurate about."
- **Asking for time before acting on a request that feels wrong:** "I want to think about this overnight before responding. Is that OK?"
- **In your own life, deciding to walk away:** "I've thought hard about this. The values mismatch isn't going to resolve. I'm going to look for another role."

## The Heavy Lifting of Leadership (Charan / Brown)

A specific application of courage that the wiki should name directly: *delivering candid feedback to subordinates*, especially negative feedback. Dick Brown at EDS called this "the heavy lifting of leadership," and Ram Charan in *Conquering a Culture of Indecision* identifies it as the single most under-supplied act of courage in organizational life.

The deeper failure: a manager who gives an inflated review last year because the conversation was uncomfortable has *deprived* the report of the information needed to improve, and has made *next year's* conversation impossible. Brown's response to a confused employee who thought he'd been doing well: *"if you're staying the same, you're falling behind."*

The discipline:

- **A leader should be constructing the appraisal all year long.** *"You have 20, 30, 60 opportunities a year to share your observations. Don't let those opportunities pass."* (Brown)
- **Surprise in the annual review is a leadership failure.** *"If, at the end of the year, someone is truly surprised by what you have to say, that's a failure of leadership."* (Brown)
- **Avoidance "sentences the organization to mediocrity."** (Brown) The cost is paid by the team, not just the leader.
- **Leaders get the behavior they tolerate.** (Brown's central aphorism) If you tolerate dangling dialogue, information clogs, and free-for-all meetings, you produce them.

This is courage of the *quiet, recurring* kind — not the one-off heroic stand. The leader who gives one brave speech but cannot deliver weekly candid feedback is, on the empirical evidence, the less courageous leader.

> *"Critical feedback is part of what Brown calls 'the heavy lifting of leadership.' Avoiding it sentences the organization to mediocrity. What's more, by failing to provide honest feedback, leaders cheat their people by depriving them of the information they need to improve."* — Charan, summarizing Brown

## Source Notes

- [[sources/hbr-communication-vol2]] — Detert's "Cultivating Everyday Courage" is the primary source for the four phases of competent courage, the idiosyncrasy-credits framing, and the Catherine Gill / Mel Exon / Erik / Fred Keller examples.
- [[sources/hbr-managing-yourself]] — Kouchaki & Smith's "Building an Ethical Career" provides the moral humility / if-then planning / three-tests / ethical-mentor framing. Christensen's "How Will You Measure Your Life?" provides the marginal-cost warning. Drucker provides the values-mismatch / walk-away example. Dorsey's "moxie" provides the impostor-syndrome / walk-away tactic. Su's "voice of character" provides the constancy idea.
- [[sources/hbr-decision-making]] — Charan's *Conquering a Culture of Indecision* and Dick Brown's EDS practice supply the "heavy lifting of leadership" framing for candid feedback as the under-supplied form of recurring courage. The four dialogue-killers (dangling, information clogs, piecemeal, free-for-all) are the failure modes the courageous leader actively works against in their meetings.
- [[sources/george-7-lessons-crisis-2009]] — adds the *structural substrate* of courage: it requires the support team (intimate partner, mentors, peer groups) and resilience practices built *in calm times.* George's Lesson 1 ("face reality, starting with yourself") is courage applied to the leader's own role in the crisis. The Tad Piper case (sharing chemical-dependency story with branch leaders during near-bankruptcy) shows that *vulnerability is a form of courage* — strategic disclosure of one's own struggle, used sparingly, builds support more powerfully than projecting invulnerability. Medtronic's "you'll never get fired for having a problem, but you will get fired for covering one up" is the cultural counterpart of individual courage. The "heavy lifting" maxim now has a complement: in normal times, deliver candid feedback recurringly; in crisis, *acknowledge your own role* before asking others to act.
- [[sources/heifetz-adaptive-leadership-2009]] — sharpens the operational definition of courage: *"finding a way to disappoint people without pushing them completely over the edge."* Adaptive leadership *necessarily* disrupts authorizers' expectations because it challenges priorities, beliefs, habits, and loyalties — exactly what they hired you not to do. The courage required is the willingness to *raise the heat into the productive zone of disequilibrium* and tolerate the resulting backlash. Heifetz's "don't do it alone" discipline (allies, confidants, opposition) is the structural support that makes this courage sustainable.

## Open Questions

- Detert's framework assumes you have time to build idiosyncrasy credits. What about acute situations (whistleblowing, harassment) where waiting is itself complicity?
- The four phases work well for moderately-risky stands (policy change, strategic dissent). For truly high-risk stands (legal violations, institutional cover-ups), the cost-benefit is different — sometimes the responsible move is to leave and speak externally.
- "Choose your battles" can be a rationalisation for never fighting. How do you distinguish strategic patience from quiet capitulation? Probably: are you accumulating credits while *also* preparing for action, or are you accumulating credits and never acting?
- Kouchaki & Smith's if-then plans are well-supported in lab studies. Durability under sustained organisational pressure is less clear.
