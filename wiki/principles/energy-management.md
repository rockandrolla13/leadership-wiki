# Energy Management

## Definition

Energy management is the discipline of treating personal capacity for work as a renewable, multi-dimensional resource — physical, emotional, mental, spiritual — rather than as a fixed time budget. The Schwartz/McCarthy thesis: time is finite, but energy can be expanded and renewed through specific, deliberately practised rituals.

## Why It Matters

The default leadership response to rising demands is to put in more hours. Empirically, this backfires: declining engagement, higher attrition, soaring medical costs, and degraded decision quality. Time is the wrong variable to manage. People who manage their energy outperform peers on financial metrics (Wachovia's pilot showed 13-percentage-point higher year-over-year loan revenue gains and 20-point higher deposit revenue gains over a control group) and report higher engagement.

When energy is mismanaged, the manager:

- Spends fight-or-flight reserves on every conversation, leaving none for the hard ones.
- Loses the ability to focus deeply because of constant context-switching (25% loss per switch).
- Brings their worst self home, draining the family relationships that were supposed to be the point.
- Ends up performing at 60% on Day 60 of a stretch they thought they were performing at 100% on.

## When It Applies

- Any leadership role with sustained intensity — not just the visible "crunch" period.
- Roles where decision quality matters more than hours logged (almost all knowledge work).
- Transitions: new role, new geography, new caregiving load, new health condition.
- Whenever you find yourself defaulting to "I'll just work harder" as the answer to a slipping outcome.
- For organisations: when you are asking employees for sustained performance, energy management has to be built into the operating model, not just offered as a perk.

## Key Principles

- **Four energy wellsprings, all renewable** (Schwartz & McCarthy):
  - **Physical** — sleep, nutrition, exercise, breaks every 90-120 minutes.
  - **Emotional** — positive emotions; managed reaction under stress.
  - **Mental** — single-task focus; concentration as a trainable skill.
  - **Spiritual** — meaning and purpose; alignment of time with values.
- **Rituals beat willpower.** A behaviour intentionally practised and precisely scheduled becomes automatic. The point of a ritual is that you do not have to decide each day whether to do it.
- **Ultradian rhythms are biological, not optional.** Bodies move through 90-120 minute cycles of high energy then a recovery trough. Ignoring the trough produces declining performance, fatigue, and eventually burnout.
- **Recovery quality > recovery length.** A few minutes of true disengagement (walking outside, listening to music, a real conversation about something else) is more restorative than a longer break spent on email.
- **The most important thing first, before email.** Sony's Jean Luc Duquesne: spend the first hour on the most important task. By 10 a.m. you have had a productive day; email finds you when you have already done what mattered.
- **Choose your story.** The Energy Project's "lenses" exercise: reverse lens (what would the other person say, and what in that is true?); long lens (how will I view this in six months?); wide lens (regardless of outcome, how can I grow from this?). Reframing the story changes the emotional charge.
- **Empathy is not free.** Zaki (HBR 2025): distinguish *emotional empathy* (taking on others' feelings; depletes you) from *empathic concern* (wanting to help; energises). Tune deliberately.
- **What you don't do matters as much as what you do.** Energy management is a subtraction problem at least as much as an addition problem (cf. Sutton & Rao's friction work in HBR 2025).

## Practical Behaviors

A leader managing energy well:

- **Has a non-negotiable bedtime and wake time** (within a band). Sleep is the single largest energy variable.
- **Eats five smaller meals or three plus snacks** instead of two large ones. Pal's-Sudden-Service style steady blood sugar.
- **Schedules a "morning priority hour"** in the calendar before any email or Slack. The hardest thing first, while focus is highest.
- **Takes a 5-15 minute true break every 90-120 minutes.** Stands up. Leaves the desk. Walks if possible. Doesn't check phone.
- **Batches email** at fixed times (e.g., 10:15 a.m. and 2:30 p.m.) rather than reactively.
- **Has a defusing ritual** for fight-or-flight moments — deep breathing, a walk, a journal entry — used before responding to triggering emails.
- **Has a "transition ritual"** between work and home — a specific point on the commute where the phone goes away, an evening walk, changing clothes.
- **Builds an appreciation practice.** Schwartz: a written note, a one-on-one mentoring lunch, a specific compliment — improves the giver's energy at least as much as the receiver's.
- **Says no to work that doesn't fit strengths.** Drucker's strength orientation: it takes more energy to bring incompetence to mediocrity than mediocrity to excellence.
- **Schedules "alignment time" — at least one hour per week** on what matters most outside work (family, faith, craft, community). Not catch-up time — primary time.
- **Audits empathic load.** After hard conversations, takes stock: am I drained? Did I shift toward emotional empathy when concern would have been better?

## Common Failure Modes

- **The martyr mentality.** "If I have any little piece of energy left at the end of the day, then I didn't do all I could." Zaki: when you let yourself burn out, "you deny everyone else the best version of yourself."
- **Mistaking caffeine and sugar for energy.** Both produce short-term arousal followed by deeper troughs.
- **Confusing busy with productive.** A day of email-clearing feels productive but moves nothing.
- **Reactive task-switching all day.** Each switch costs ~25% of the time it would otherwise take to finish the primary task.
- **Suppressing emotion instead of defusing it.** A held-down fight-or-flight response doesn't go away — it leaks into the next interaction.
- **Skipping breaks because you're "too busy"** — the moments when breaks matter most are exactly when you skip them.
- **Performance theatre — "I worked 80 hours this week"** — research (Reid, BU) suggests this is often a lie, but the social reward for the claim reinforces the broken default.
- **Treating recovery as something you'll do later** — on weekends, on vacations. Recovery has to be daily and weekly, not delayed.

## Diagnostic Questions

- How many hours did I sleep last night? Last week? What was the quality?
- When was the last time I took a break that was not in front of a screen?
- What time did I check email this morning, and did I do anything important before that?
- In the last hard conversation, was I bringing empathic concern or absorbing emotional distress?
- Which energy bucket — physical, emotional, mental, spiritual — am I most depleted in right now?
- If I scored myself today on the 16-question energy audit (HBR Managing Yourself), how many would I check as "true for me"?
- Am I managing time or managing energy? What would change if I switched?

## Useful Scripts

- **To self, mid-day slump:** "This is the ultradian trough. Stand up, walk for ten minutes, come back. Do not power through with caffeine."
- **To self, before a hard conversation:** "Two minutes of breathing. Set the empathy mode to 'concern,' not 'absorb.' Pre-regulate."
- **To self, after a hard conversation:** "Take stock. Did that drain me? If yes, take fifteen minutes to recover before the next thing — not the next meeting."
- **To a report whose energy is visibly low:** "I want to flag something — you've looked drained the last two weeks. What's the load like right now? What can we take off?"
- **To a peer pushing a "let's do it tonight" ask:** "I've already used my evening for [family / sleep / recovery]. I can do it first thing tomorrow. Will that work?"
- **To self, when reaching for the phone after a hard meeting:** "I'm reaching for distraction, not recovery. Two minutes of breathing first."

## Open Questions

- The Wachovia and Sony results depend on senior-leadership buy-in. What happens when an individual manager tries to run energy-management rituals in an organisation that culturally rewards overwork?
- The 90-120 minute ultradian cycle is well-documented for individuals. How does it map to a hybrid or distributed team's calendar?
- The "transition ritual" between work and home assumed a physical commute. In remote work, the same need exists but the natural cue is gone. What replaces it?
- Is "empathic concern" trainable independently of personality? The Stanford research suggests yes; lived experience suggests it is harder than the article implies.
