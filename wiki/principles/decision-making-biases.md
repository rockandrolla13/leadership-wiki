# Decision-Making and Cognitive Biases

## Purpose

This framework gives leaders a working toolkit for spotting and counter-acting cognitive biases — narrow thinking about the future, about objectives, and about options — that systematically degrade individual and team decisions. It pairs Soll/Milkman/Payne's "Outsmart Your Own Biases" debiasing toolkit with Flyvbjerg et al.'s "Uniqueness Trap" outside-view techniques and Chevallier et al.'s question-domain audit.

## Core Idea

The default human mode of judgment is fast, intuitive, and biased. Under stress, time pressure, or strong emotional investment, the bias gets worse. Better decisions require broadening on three fronts — the future you imagine, the objectives you set, and the options you consider — and using specific tools (three estimates, premortems, outside view, joint evaluation, vanishing-options test, trip wires) to defeat the predictable failures of each.

## Steps

### 1. Broaden the future

1. **Make three estimates, not a range.** When forecasting, work out a low estimate ("there's a 10% chance we'll do worse than this"), a medium, and a high. The discipline of separating low from high produces wider, more accurate ranges than asking for a single range.
2. **Think twice.** Make a forecast, take a break (sleep if you can), come back without referring to the first one, and make a second forecast. Average the two. The "wisdom of the inner crowd" — your second guess uses different mental models from your first.
3. **Run a premortem.** "It's three years from now. This decision failed. Explain why." Prospective hindsight surfaces risks that ordinary foresight misses.
4. **Take an outside view.** Lovallo & Kahneman: instead of forecasting your specific project ("inside view"), ask what happens to projects of this type ("outside view"). If only 30% of new products in this industry turn a profit within five years, your forecast should reflect that base rate.

### 2. Broaden the objectives

5. **Generate many objectives before sorting.** Keeney's Seagate work: each executive named only ~1/3 of the company's eventual objectives. A complete list emerges from synthesis.
6. **Seek advice on objectives, not just on options.** Show your draft list to peers and ask "what's missing?" Don't anchor them with your conclusions.
7. **Cycle through objectives one at a time.** Trying to satisfy all objectives simultaneously paralyses. Tackle them sequentially, then look for combinations.

### 3. Broaden the options

8. **At least 3-5 strong contenders.** A "yes or no" framing on a single option is almost always a false choice. The decision can be no better than the best option considered.
9. **Use joint evaluation.** Side-by-side comparison reveals trade-offs invisible in isolation. The famous study: candidate with 70 programs and a 3.0 GPA vs candidate with 10 programs and a 4.9 GPA — in joint mode, raters favor the prolific one; in separate mode, they favor the higher-GPA one. Joint evaluation is closer to truth.
10. **Run the "vanishing options" test.** Heath & Heath: "Assume I can't choose any of the options I'm weighing. What else could I do?" This forces alternatives to surface.

### 4. Fight motivated bias

11. **Use trip wires.** A preset rule that forces a re-decision when triggered. Example: Everest guides announce a turnaround time before the expedition; if the group isn't at the summit by then, they descend, no debate. Same logic for business: "If we've spent more than $X without milestone Y, we re-evaluate from scratch."
12. **Use decision points** (softer than trip wires). A trigger that forces a *re-think*, not a *re-decision*. Useful in negotiations where preferences may legitimately change with new information.
13. **Blind where possible.** Orchestras hiring behind screens. Anonymous paper grading. Algorithm-assisted hiring assessments. These remove biases that the conscious mind can't suppress.
14. **Use checklists.** VCs vetting pitches. Hiring managers using structured interviews. Surgical safety checklists. Reduce errors from forgetfulness and ad-hoc weighting.

### 5. The Uniqueness Trap (Flyvbjerg et al.)

15. **Distrust the "this is unique" framing.** Flyvbjerg's 219-project study: a one-point increase on a 10-point uniqueness scale was associated with a 5-percentage-point increase in cost overruns. 37% of projects rated 10/10 unique had >75% cost overruns.
16. **Assume someone has done this before.** Even genuinely-novel projects share components with prior projects. Break the project into modules and find analogues at the module level.
17. **Apply reference-class forecasting.** Find the class of comparable projects and use their performance distribution as your prior.
18. **Run noise audits.** Present multiple decision-makers with similar hypothetical situations and ask them to predict outcomes. High variance signals that the decision is driven more by irrelevant factors ("noise") than by genuine analysis.

## When To Use

- For any decision that is substantially irreversible or expensive: hiring at senior levels; major capital allocation; market entry; corporate restructuring; product launches; M&A.
- For projects you suspect are biased toward "this time it's different" reasoning.
- When you notice strong emotional investment in a particular conclusion — the bias-fighting tools work best when motivation is high.
- In a leadership team where consensus is forming too fast (the petting-zoo dysfunction, HBR 2026).
- For personal decisions: choosing a job, deciding to leave a job, life-strategy choices (Strack et al.'s seven steps applied with debiasing).

## When Not To Use

- For genuinely small, reversible decisions. The cost of the debiasing process exceeds the expected value of better decisions. Default to fast judgment.
- In a crisis where decision speed matters more than decision accuracy (emergency response, safety incidents). Use prebuilt playbooks.
- When the data is so thin that no method will produce a confident answer. Acknowledge uncertainty and design for optionality instead of optimal point estimates.

## Example Application

A SaaS VP is evaluating whether to enter the European market.

- **Naive approach:** "Yes / no, based on our gut." Decision driven by inside view, optimism about market size, and a strong narrative.
- **Debiased approach:**
  - *Three estimates of revenue at 18 months:* low 1M, medium 3M, high 8M.
  - *Outside view:* of comparable US-SaaS companies entering Europe in our segment, what fraction hit 3M ARR by month 18? Probably <30%.
  - *Premortem:* "It's two years from now, we're pulling out of Europe with $0.5M in losses. Why?" (Likely answers: GDPR complexity, sales-cycle differences, hiring locally took longer than planned.)
  - *Vanishing options:* "What if we couldn't do Europe? What else could we do with that capital?" (Likely answers: deeper investment in current markets; product investment; targeted enterprise upmarket.)
  - *Joint evaluation:* compare Europe option side-by-side with two strongest alternatives, not in isolation.
  - *Trip wire:* "If after 9 months our Europe ARR is below 0.6M, we re-evaluate the entire bet."

The output is a decision that survives external scrutiny and has a built-in exit ramp.

## Failure Modes

- **Pseudo-rigor.** Going through the motions of premortems and three-estimates without actually changing the conclusion. The bias survives the form.
- **Outside-view absolutism.** Reference-class data is informative but not deterministic. If your case has genuinely different fundamentals, the base rate is misleading.
- **Decision-process paralysis.** Some teams use the debiasing toolkit as an excuse to delay. The tools should serve the decision, not replace it.
- **Trip wire avoidance.** Setting a trip wire and then ignoring it when triggered. The whole point is to commit *before* you are emotionally invested.
- **Misweighting (Soll et al.):** giving the data you have too much weight; not noticing the data you don't have. Use checklists and blinding to counter.

## Adaptation For This User

- **For founders / startup operators:** the outside view is sometimes misleading because the base rate is mostly failures. Use it to calibrate confidence, not to refuse to start. Pair with trip wires that force a re-evaluation if the bet isn't working.
- **For engineering managers:** the uniqueness trap is endemic ("our codebase is special"). Reference-class forecasting on technical projects (migrations, rewrites) is rare but high-value.
- **For senior executives:** decision speed has a real cost. Reserve the full debiasing toolkit for the largest decisions. Build a faster heuristic version (three-estimate quick check + outside view + one stress-test question) for medium-sized ones.
- **For coaching conversations:** when a coachee is stuck on a binary decision, the vanishing-options test ("What else could you do?") is the highest-leverage single intervention.
