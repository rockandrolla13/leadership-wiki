# A Spy's Guide to Strategy

## Metadata

- **Author:** John Braddock (former CIA case officer)
- **Date:** 2017 (self-published)
- **Type:** book (short — ~75 pages)
- **Path:** raw/books/markdown/A Spy's Guide to Strategy (John Braddock) (z-library.sk, 1lib.sk, z-lib.sk).md
- **Ingested:** 2026-05-17

## Core Argument

Strategy, stripped down, is a discipline of *Endgames* and *Games* — what you want at the end, what other parties want, and what game is actually being played between you. Braddock draws on CIA case-officer experience (running sources, reading adversaries) and historical examples (WWI, the Arab Spring, Bin Laden's strategy) to argue that most strategy fails because the practitioner misreads either their own Endgame or the other side's. The book introduces a compact vocabulary — Endgame, Zero-Sum Game, Positive-Sum Game, hierarchies, the "game inside the game" — that travels across statecraft, business, and personal life.

## Key Leadership Insights

- **Strategy starts with the Endgame.** Not the next move. What does the world look like when you've won? What are the people, places, and things in that picture? Most strategies fail because the Endgame was never made explicit.
- **Then identify the *other side's* Endgame.** Sun Tzu: *"If you know yourself but not the other side, for every victory gained you will also suffer a defeat."* The most common strategic error is assuming the other side wants what you want (or its opposite).
- **Three kinds of Zero-Sum outcomes.** When one side wins and the other loses, three futures are possible: (1) **No future games** — loser is destroyed/exiled (winner-takes-all). (2) **Future Zero-Sum games** — forever-conflict (interminable rivalry). (3) **Future Positive-Sum games** — loser and winner shift to allies (the post-game cooperation). Misreading which type of Zero-Sum your counterpart wants is the strategic-mistake-most-common-in-business.
- **The Arab Spring case: most protesters wanted the third type.** From outside, the Arab Spring looked like winner-take-all. From inside, most protesters wanted a *better boss within the existing system* — a hierarchy reshuffle inside a continued Positive-Sum Game (Egypt staying Egypt). Leaders who misread this as type-1 (destroy the regime) escalated unnecessarily.
- **Hierarchies exist inside larger Positive-Sum Games.** Almost all functioning organizations and societies are hierarchies — fewer at the top, many below — *voluntary* because the larger game is positive-sum and everyone benefits. Dictatorships hold hierarchies in place via threats; healthy organizations hold them in place via mutual benefit. The implication for leaders: when your hierarchy needs threats to stay in place, the larger positive-sum game has broken down.
- **The game inside the game.** Often the apparent game (what's being negotiated) is not the real game (what the parties actually care about). Braddock's lying-source case: the source's *apparent* game was helping the CIA fight terrorism; his *real* game was social status among his friends (telling 23 people he was working with the CIA). The leader who can read the inner game has the strategic advantage.
- **Intent is the unmarked dimension.** Most analysis focuses on capability (what could the other side do?). Strategic insight comes from reading *intent* (what do they want?). Capability without intent doesn't predict behavior; intent with imperfect capability often does.
- **Test hypotheses about the other side.** Braddock surveilled his source even though he didn't expect surveillance — *"that was just a hypothesis. To be tested."* In statecraft and business alike: don't assume; verify. The cost of verification is almost always lower than the cost of being wrong.
- **Positive-Sum Games require trust; Zero-Sum Games require deception.** A leader who runs every interaction as Zero-Sum is teaching counterparts to run every interaction the same way. The deeper strategic move is to recognize which game you are *actually* in and align your behavior accordingly — including changing the game when possible.

## Practical Applications

- **Write your Endgame down.** One paragraph. The picture of the world when you've won. The people, places, and things in it. If you can't write it, you don't have a strategy.
- **Write the *other side's* Endgame down.** Their picture. Don't assume it's the inverse of yours. Often it's orthogonal.
- **For every adversarial situation, ask: which of the three Zero-Sum types does the other side want?** Type 1 (destroy you), Type 2 (forever-rivalry), Type 3 (post-conflict alliance). The answer changes your tactics.
- **Identify the game inside the game.** What does the other party care about that they're not naming? (Status. Face. Legacy. Optionality. Identity.) When you find the inner game, you find the leverage.
- **Treat your model of the other side as a hypothesis, not a fact.** Build in tests. Cheap, frequent.
- **Diagnose whether your hierarchy is held in place by mutual benefit or by threat.** If threat, the positive-sum game has broken — fix the larger game before tightening the threats.

## Relevant Quotes

> "If you know yourself but not the other side, for every victory gained you will also suffer a defeat." — Sun Tzu (Braddock's epigraph)

> "After a Zero-Sum Game, there are three things that can happen between the winner and loser: (1) the loser is killed, vanquished or runs away (No Future Games); (2) the loser and winner engage in another Zero-Sum Game (Future Zero-Sum Games); (3) the loser and winner's next interaction is a Positive-Sum Game. They shift from enemies to allies."

> "He hadn't been followed, he said. He was certain, he said. Which he shouldn't have been. It was just a hypothesis."

> "From the outside, the Arab Spring looked like a simple Zero-Sum Game. … On the inside, you saw people hold back."

> "Inside every Positive-Sum Game, some people have more power than others. Some people make more important decisions than others. … Usually, hierarchies exist inside a Positive-Sum Game. Where underlings voluntarily follow the decisions of people at the top. Because everyone benefits."

## Frameworks Extracted

- **Endgame Identification:** what does your won-state look like? Who, where, what's in it?
- **The Other Side's Endgame:** same question, applied to every counterparty. Sun Tzu's mandate.
- **Three Zero-Sum Outcomes:** No Future Games / Future Zero-Sum Games / Future Positive-Sum Games. Determines tactical posture toward losers.
- **Game-Inside-the-Game:** the unstated objective (status, face, identity) that often drives behavior more than the stated one.
- **Hierarchy-Inside-Positive-Sum:** healthy organizations and societies are hierarchies *embedded* in larger mutual-benefit games. Diagnose the larger game first.
- **Intent vs. Capability:** prioritize reading intent; capability without intent is misleading.
- **Hypothesis-Testing Discipline:** treat models of others as hypotheses; verify cheaply.

## Contradictions / Tensions With Existing Wiki

- **Adds a foundational strategy frame the wiki lacks.** The wiki has [[communication/strategy-communication]] (how to *communicate* strategy) but minimal material on *what strategy is.* Braddock's Endgame discipline is a useful primitive — easy to remember, easy to apply, hard to fake.
- **Reinforces [[organization/organizational-politics]].** The "game-inside-the-game" frame is what makes organizational politics legible. Most political moves make sense once you identify the inner game (status, face, identity, resource control).
- **Extends [[principles/influence]] and [[communication/persuasion]].** Braddock's "intent over capability" reading is what makes persuasion-up (Grant's *if…then* map) work: you read what the counterpart actually wants, then offer it.
- **Tensions with the wiki's [[principles/authentic-leadership]] / [[principles/trust]] emphasis.** Braddock's spy-craft frame treats deception and hypothesis-testing as routine. In a leadership context, deception is corrosive. The synthesis: the *analytic discipline* (read intent, identify Endgame, test hypotheses) is fully transferable; the *deceptive tactics* (running sources, manipulating belief) are not. The leader uses the discipline to *understand* counterparts, not to *manipulate* them.

## Questions This Source Raises

- The Endgame discipline assumes a stable world in which "winning" is definable. In complex/adaptive contexts (Heifetz; see [[sources/heifetz-adaptive-leadership-2009]]), the Endgame *emerges* through the work. How should the discipline be modified for adaptive leadership? Provisional answer: write *direction* and *constraints* rather than fixed end-state.
- Braddock's CIA frame treats counterparts as fundamentally adversarial. In knowledge-economy organizations, most counterparts are colleagues with overlapping interests. Does the framework still hold, or do its core moves invert?
- The "three Zero-Sum outcomes" frame implies the loser's future behavior is endogenous to *how* they lose. In business, this is the difference between firing someone with dignity vs. without — which often determines whether they become a future ally, neutral, or enemy. The wiki could deepen [[management/underperformance]] and [[management/performance-management]] with this lens.
