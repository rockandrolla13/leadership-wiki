# Extreme Teaming: Lessons in Complex, Cross-Sector Leadership

## Core Argument

Many of today's most important problems — high-tech infrastructure, healthcare innovation, climate transition, disaster response — cannot be solved within a single discipline or organization. They require **extreme teaming:** newly assembled, temporary groups of strangers from different occupations, organizations, and industries collaborating on novel, high-stakes problems. Traditional team theory (stable membership, clear boundaries, repeatable processes) does not apply. What separates successful extreme teams from failed ones is a specific repertoire of **four leadership functions** — Build an Engaging Vision, Cultivate Psychological Safety, Develop Shared Mental Models, and Empower Agile Execution — arranged in a 2×2 matrix of motivate/facilitate × interpersonal/technical.

## Key Leadership Insights

- **Teams ≠ teaming.** A team is a stable entity; teaming is a *process* — the dynamic coordination of people in shifting configurations. Increasingly, work happens in teaming arrangements that do not look like Hackman's classical teams (clear boundary, stable membership, repeated history).
- **Diverse expertise is the source of innovation *and* the source of failure.** Cross-boundary teams have more raw material to combine, but they also face "common knowledge effect" (groups discuss what everyone already knows), language barriers, and divergent interests. Diversity is conditional, not automatic, value.
- **The 2010 Chilean mine rescue is the canonical case.** 33 miners trapped 700 meters underground, initial survival odds <1%. Hundreds of experts across organizations, sectors, nations. Senior leadership (Minister Golborne, President Piñera) reached out — "we were humble enough to ask for help" — and on-site leader Andre Sougarret formed three parallel subteams, ran short-cycle drilling experiments, and celebrated *information from failures* as progress. All 33 alive in 70 days.
- **Four leadership functions form a 2×2:**
  - **Build an Engaging Vision** (Motivate / Interpersonal) — make values explicit; articulate a challenging target.
  - **Cultivate Psychological Safety** (Facilitate / Interpersonal) — display authentic caring; frame cross-boundary work as a resource.
  - **Develop Shared Mental Models** (Facilitate / Technical) — diagnose interfaces for knowledge-sharing; leverage boundary objects.
  - **Empower Agile Execution** (Motivate / Technical) — enable expert decision clusters; provide room to maneuver.
- **Psychological safety is the operating system of teaming.** Without it, knowledge diversity is wasted — people stay in their lane and don't speak across boundaries. Edmondson's earlier work on psych safety (1999) is the technical backbone; Extreme Teaming applies it to the cross-boundary case.
- **"Authentic caring" is a leader behavior, not a feeling.** It is *visible* presence ("you need to be visible. People need to know where to reach you, that you will pick up if they call"), *non-checking* engagement ("interaction perceived to be cooperative; the project leaders' initiation of contact being, not for the purpose of 'checking on things,' but rather to offer useful information or guidance"), and *acting on what you hear* ("[Director] would not only listen to our viewpoints, but would also come back to us during the following week to update us on what he had found out in relation to what we had said").
- **Boundary objects move knowledge across disciplines.** Drawings, models, prototypes, PowerPoint decks, even physical tools — these are "objects that help them communicate with one another." Two designers and one engineer drawing the same sketch live, simultaneously, transfers more knowledge than any verbal explanation.
- **"I intend to" applies at the project level.** Marquet's submarine principle scales: empower expert decision clusters with the autonomy to make local calls and the discipline to coordinate with neighbors. Top-down control fails in novel territory because the people with the authority don't have the information.
- **Failures should be celebrated as information.** The Chilean drillers learned to mark each failed drill hole as a data point, not a defeat. "Project leaders ensured that discarded options were not judged as 'failures' but rather as valued lessons that informed next steps."
- **Vision is built, not pronounced.** In stable teams, vision can be set in advance. In extreme teaming, vision must "develop as more is learned, and it must be open to being partially reshaped by input from diverse camps. Vision, for extreme teaming, is thus both leader led and collectively built."

## Practical Applications

- **At project kickoff, articulate values *and* a challenging target.** Don't just state the goal; articulate the ideals (sustainability, patient impact, technical excellence) that justify the effort. Use very specific numbers: "$10 million budget, 60,000 square feet, delivery on [date], LEED Platinum."
- **Schedule "get acquainted" sessions before the work starts.** Marine and Mathieu (Project Fiona) invited everyone to two outside events to swap stories and crack jokes before any technical work began. This isn't social fluff; it is the construction of the trust that allows technical conflict later.
- **Diagnose your interfaces.** Where do hand-offs happen between teams or disciplines? Mark them on a chart. Schedule co-location, face-to-face working sessions, and shared whiteboarding at those interfaces. Don't try to manage them via email.
- **Use boundary objects deliberately.** When two disciplines have to coordinate, put a physical or visual artifact between them — a prototype, a wall-sized chart, a live-shared sketch. Verbal coordination across knowledge boundaries fails predictably.
- **Run a clear authority structure even if you don't draw an org chart.** Project participants must know who decides what. "Decision-making layers were nonetheless clear … project participants knew whom to contact in the event of a problem or conflict and who had authority over, and exercised decision rights in, specific domains."
- **Frame disagreement as "potential for discovery."** When two perspectives clash in a meeting, the leader's job is to name it as the team's raw material, not as conflict to be suppressed. "We were invited to challenge it if we thought it needed to be challenged or to support it if we felt like supporting it. We knew others could see things differently, and that this was what gave us the potential to innovate."

## Relevant Quotes

> "Extreme teaming refers to cross-sector collaboration. … By assembling groups of people with various backgrounds, extreme teaming sets the stage for complex problem solving and innovation that affect more than one organization." — Edmondson & Harvey, Introduction

> "When you lead such a project, you need to be visible. People need to know where to reach you, that you will pick up if they call and be attentive to what they have to say." — Director, Project Sofia, quoted by Edmondson & Harvey

> "Reflecting on the situation, one readily comprehends that a top-down, command-and-control approach would have failed to achieve this stunning outcome [the Chilean mine rescue]. No one person, or even one leadership team, or one organization or agency, could have successfully innovated to solve this problem." — Edmondson & Harvey, ch. 1

> "Project leaders ensured that discarded options were not judged as 'failures' but rather as valued lessons that informed next steps." — Edmondson & Harvey, ch. 7

> "In organizations in which there is no safety provided, people are more likely to hide mistakes or problems out of self-preservation. The issue is, those mistakes and problems, if not addressed, often add up and appear later when they become too big to contain." — Edmondson & Harvey, paraphrasing Marquet

> "Newly formed, temporary teams that assemble members from various backgrounds often set the stage for knowledge recombination and innovation. … When people have worked together many times previously, their projects exhibit less innovation than those with unfamiliar team members." — Edmondson & Harvey, ch. 1 (citing Skilton & Dooley, 2010)

## Frameworks Extracted

- **The Four-Function 2×2 Matrix:**

  |              | INTERPERSONAL | TECHNICAL |
  |--------------|---------------|-----------|
  | **MOTIVATE** | Build an Engaging Vision | Empower Agile Execution |
  | **FACILITATE** | Cultivate Psychological Safety | Develop Shared Mental Models |

- **Build an Engaging Vision:** (a) Make values explicit; (b) Articulate a challenging target.
- **Cultivate Psychological Safety:** (a) Display authentic caring; (b) Frame cross-boundary work as a resource.
- **Develop Shared Mental Models:** (a) Diagnose interfaces for knowledge-sharing; (b) Leverage boundary objects.
- **Empower Agile Execution:** (a) Enable expert decision clusters; (b) Provide room to maneuver.
- **Execution-as-learning:** Building learning *into* action (vs. separating planning from execution). Used by Edmondson elsewhere; central to extreme teaming.
- **Common Knowledge Effect (Stasser):** Teams with diverse information tend to discuss what is *common* across members instead of *unique* to each. The default of group discussion destroys diversity advantage unless actively countered.
- **Boundary Objects (Star & Griesemer):** Diagrams, prototypes, models that are stable enough to circulate across boundaries but flexible enough to adapt to local interpretation. The physical/visual currency of knowledge transfer.
- **Interface Management:** Treating boundaries between sub-teams as managed objects (with explicit registers, coordination meetings, named owners), not as gaps that "should work themselves out."

## Contradictions / Tensions With Existing Wiki

- **Reinforces and extends [[principles/psychological-safety]]:** Edmondson is the originator of the term in management literature. Extreme Teaming applies it to short-lived, cross-organizational teams — exactly the gap the wiki had open. The "authentic caring" practice gives a behavioral specification of what produces psych safety in this context.
- **Reinforces [[principles/purpose-and-why]] (Sinek) and [[principles/authentic-leadership]] (George):** "Make values explicit" is the same move Sinek and George prescribe, applied to project leadership.
- **Tensions with stable-team management theory in the wiki.** Pages on [[organization/team-design]] and [[management/hiring]] assume stable composition over time. Extreme teaming is the opposite — short-lived, novel-composition. The wiki needs both models.
- **Tensions with Lopp's small-team focus.** Lopp's playbooks assume manager-and-direct-reports. Edmondson's frame is *project leadership* without formal authority over the participants' careers. The mechanisms differ — Edmondson's authority is moral and process-based, not performance-review-based.
- **Reinforces Sinek's Circle of Safety (leaders-eat-last):** Different vocabulary, same mechanism — authentic caring + framing differences as resource = Circle of Safety at project scale.

## Questions This Source Raises

- The five focal cases are large, well-resourced projects (>$10M budgets, named industry partners). How much of the leadership function model transfers to small cross-functional teams inside a single mid-sized company?
- Edmondson and Harvey explicitly chose successful projects. The 2×2 is induced from successes — what does failure look like along each axis? Which of the four functions is the most diagnostic when extreme teaming goes wrong?
- The Chilean rescue had clear, time-bounded urgency. How do these leadership functions adapt to slow-burn extreme teaming (climate, public health) where no single failure produces feedback?
- The "interface management" practice presumes the leader can see the interfaces in advance. In emergent novel work, interfaces are discovered after the fact. How does a leader run interface management on interfaces that don't yet exist?
