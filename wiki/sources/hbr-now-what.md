## Core Argument

The issue's most leadership-relevant pieces converge on a single thesis: *the formal acts of leadership — naming a successor, signing a contract, structuring a supply chain, hiring a coach, judging a candidate's "presence" — are only the visible tip of the work. The real work is in the relationships, incentive structures, and execution disciplines that surround them.* Mintzberg argues against the cult of the heroic isolated leader and for "just enough leadership" diffused through the organization. Freeman argues that succession is the CEO's job, not the board's, and must begin years before transition. Ertel argues that signing a deal is the *beginning* of value creation, not the end. Narayanan & Raman argue that supply chains fail not from poor engineering but from misaligned incentives. Sherman & Freas argue that executive coaching only works as a triangular relationship with explicit contracting. Useem warns against using surface "proxies" (poise, charisma, board composition) as substitutes for judging substance. Threaded throughout: the trustworthy leader is the one who designs systems that *do not depend on personal heroics*.

## Key Leadership Insights

- **The cult of the lone leader is a category error (Mintzberg).** Lou Gerstner did not personally rescue IBM; he provided "just enough leadership" — he supported direction-setting by others rather than imposing his own. The remedy: diffuse leadership through the organization (anyone with an idea and initiative can lead), involve followers in selecting leaders (McKinsey's elected managing director), and reward engagement over heroics.
- **Beware proxies (Useem).** "Charisma," "presence," "outside chair," "board independence" — these are surrogates for leadership quality, not the thing itself. McClellan was beloved and indecisive; Lee was reserved and decisive. Enron's board was structurally model — and rubber-stamped fraud in 60-minute teleconferences. The discipline: judge the *content* of decisions, not the *context*.
- **Succession is a CEO job, not a board job (Freeman).** Five principles:
  1. *Force the board to pay attention* — they will avoid it in good times.
  2. *Look for differences* — pick the successor whose strengths match where the *company* is going, not where it has been.
  3. *Make your successor's success your own* — invest in coaching them well in advance (Freeman: weekly Sunday-afternoon calls for five years with Surya Mohapatra).
  4. *Don't drag your feet* — leave at the top of your game.
  5. *Avoid the return appearance* — coming back means both you and your successor failed at succession.
- **Negotiating is not deal-making (Ertel).** Deal-minded negotiators optimize for signature; implementation-minded negotiators optimize for *value created after signature*. The Concert/AT&T-BT joint venture is the case in point. Five behavioral shifts:
  1. Start with the end in mind — run a 12-months-from-now pre-mortem before signing.
  2. Help the other side prepare too — surprise is not your friend if you have to work with them afterward.
  3. Treat alignment as a shared responsibility — their unaligned stakeholder is your problem too.
  4. Send one message — joint hand-off meetings between negotiators and implementation teams on both sides.
  5. Manage negotiation as a business process — preparation discipline, postnegotiation reviews, controls.
- **Executive coaching is a triangular contract (Sherman & Freas).** Three parties (client, coachee, coach), not two. Explicit contracting on goals, roles, accountability, no-fault escape clauses, and measurement is what makes coaching work. Failure modes: ad-hoc coaching with no organizational frame; coachees who are not motivated; coaches selected on credentials rather than chemistry; programs that bypass the CEO/sponsor and never gain traction.
- **Supply chains fail from misaligned incentives, not from bad engineering (Narayanan & Raman).** Three root causes:
  1. **Hidden actions** — you cannot observe whether your partner is doing their best for you.
  2. **Hidden information** — your partner has private knowledge that they cannot or will not share.
  3. **Badly designed incentives** — partners are rewarded for behaviors that do not produce supply-chain optimum.
  Six remedies in sequence: identify symptoms, diagnose incentive misalignment, rewrite contracts, reveal hidden information, develop trust, monitor periodically.
- **Marketing strategy belongs in the boardroom (McGovern et al.).** When marketing decisions are decentralized to a CMO without P&L accountability, the board loses the line of sight to brand health, top-line growth, and competitive positioning. The remedy: a marketing dashboard with three to four business drivers, a pipeline of growth ideas, and a marketing-talent-pool view.
- **VC trust is the substrate of the relationship (Mullins case, four expert commentaries).** When the VC team changes the contract on the way to signature — even on a "minor" clause — the right response is suspicion, not relief. The signing is the *beginning* of a multi-year working relationship; bad-faith behavior pre-signing is the strongest available signal of post-signing behavior. The deeper lesson: psychological commitment (sunk-cost in a process) clouds judgment about whether to proceed. The Petrolink team had committed to leaving Beckman; that commitment made them vulnerable to a worse deal.
- **Prudent paranoia is sometimes correct (Kramer).** "Prudently paranoid people monitor their colleagues' every move, scrutinizing and analyzing each action in minute detail" — useful in environments where bad faith is real, corrosive in environments where it is not. The skill is calibration.
- **Engagement matters more than seat (Mintzberg).** True leaders commit to *their* industry, *their* company, *their* job — seriously, quietly. The career-hopper who sprints between titles never has to live the consequences of their own decisions. *"Unhappy the land that needs heroes."*

## Practical Applications

- **Run a 12-months-from-now pre-mortem before any major deal or alliance.** "It is one year after signing. The deal is failing. Why?" Use the answers to redesign terms, governance, and implementation hand-off — not after, but before.
- **Build a joint hand-off meeting** between the negotiation team and the implementation team. Make the negotiators present *why* the trade-offs were made the way they were, not just *what* the document says.
- **For any executive coaching engagement, write an explicit triangular contract.** Goals (three, measurable, behavioral), confidentiality boundaries, accountability for each party, no-fault escape, and a follow-up 360. Do not start the work without it.
- **Diagnose your supply chain (or any external partnership) by Narayanan & Raman's three questions.** Hidden actions you cannot observe? Hidden information your partner has but you do not? Incentives that pay partners to act against your interest? If any answer is yes, redesign the contract before adding more monitoring.
- **As CEO/leader, start succession planning in year one, not year ten.** Use the Freeman model: weekly recurring time with the candidate, mixing strategic coaching with frank performance feedback. Look for *differences*, not similarities.
- **In any decision meeting, separate substance from proxy.** Useem's discipline: judge the content of the recommendation, not how confident the presenter sounded.
- **Whenever a contract is changed late in the process, take it as a signal, not an accident.** The Petrolink case generalizes: pre-signing behavior predicts post-signing behavior.
- **For each external study or internal datum cited in a decision, ask the Luca-style questions** before accepting it as evidence (this connects to [[sources/hbr-decision-making]]).
- **Notice when you have become psychologically committed to a course of action.** Especially when costs have been sunk (Beckman notice given; team built; capital spent), pause and ask whether the commitment is making you accept a worse deal than you would have walked away from cold.

## Frameworks Extracted

- **Just-Enough Leadership (Mintzberg).** Diffuse leadership through the organization; involve followers in selecting leaders; reward engagement and commitment over heroics; care more than you cure, connect more than you control, demonstrate more than you decide.
- **CEO-Led Succession (Freeman, five principles).** Force board attention; look for differences; make successor's success your own (weekly recurring coaching time over years); don't drag your feet; avoid the return appearance.
- **Implementation-Minded Negotiating (Ertel).** Five shifts from deal-mindset to implementation-mindset: end in mind (pre-mortem), help them prepare, treat alignment as shared, send one message (joint hand-off), manage negotiation as a process.
- **Deal-Minded vs. Implementation-Minded Behavior Table (Ertel).** Behaviors and underlying assumptions on surprise, information sharing, closing techniques, realistic commitments, decision-making, stakeholders — explicit contrast.
- **Triangular Coaching Contract (Sherman & Freas).** Client / coachee / coach. Qualify all three. Explicit goals (three, behavioral, measurable). No-fault escape. Linked to organizational strategy. CEO sponsorship.
- **Supply-Chain Incentive Diagnosis (Narayanan & Raman).** Three root causes (hidden actions, hidden information, badly designed incentives). Six-step remedy (symptoms → diagnose → contracts → reveal information → trust → monitor).
- **Marketing Dashboard for Boards (McGovern et al.).** Three sections: business drivers, pipeline of growth ideas, marketing talent pool. Designed to make marketing's link to strategy visible at board level.
- **The Anti-Proxy Discipline (Useem).** Judge decisions by content, not by surface signs of leadership. Treat charisma, confidence, polished presence, even structural governance metrics, as orthogonal to (and often misleading about) decision quality.
- **Prudent Paranoia Calibration (Kramer).** Trust is not unconditional; in some environments, vigilance is necessary. The skill is matching the level of vigilance to the actual risk of bad faith.

## Contradictions / Tensions With Existing Wiki

- **Mintzberg's "diffused leadership" tensions with the wiki's [[principles/executive-presence]] page** which emphasizes the leader-at-scale. Reconciliation: presence at the top is necessary but not sufficient; without diffused leadership beneath, presence is theater.
- **Freeman's "the board won't drive succession" frame is dated.** In 2026, governance pressure has shifted boards toward more active succession planning. Still useful as the *starting* posture for the CEO who finds the board passive.
- **The Mullins case's lesson — "if they cheat in negotiation they'll cheat afterwards" — tensions with the wiki's general anti-paranoia posture.** Kramer's framing helps: this is *prudent* paranoia, calibrated to the actual environment, not a default stance.
- **Ertel's "joint hand-off meeting" prescription is in tension with some confidentiality norms** (e.g., M&A pre-close information sharing). The principle stands; the implementation must accommodate legal constraints.
- **The Sherman & Freas claim that "no one knows what makes a good coach" tensions with the executive-coaching field's claims of professionalization.** It is still true in 2026 in important ways; treat coach selection as chemistry-and-track-record more than credential-and-method.
- **The Narayanan & Raman framework treats supply-chain partners as economic actors.** In knowledge-work contexts (consultants, agencies, contractors), the analog is similar but the language of "incentives" must be supplemented by [[principles/trust]] and reputation.

## Questions This Source Raises

- Mintzberg's "elected managing director" model at McKinsey is rare. Under what conditions does it travel — and where does it fail (founder-led firms? regulated industries? non-partnership structures)?
- Freeman's five-year coaching of a successor is expensive. What is the minimum viable version for smaller organizations or shorter CEO tenures?
- Ertel's "joint hand-off meeting" is hard to execute when one party is hostile or has departed. What is the fallback when the implementation-minded approach fails because the counterpart was deal-minded throughout?
- Narayanan & Raman's incentive-redesign requires both parties to be willing to renegotiate. In long-tail supplier relationships, this is often infeasible. What is the remedy when only one party can change behavior?
- Sherman & Freas's claim that the field lacks rigorous credentialing remains substantially true in 2026. Should organizations standardize internally rather than waiting for an external standard?
- The Mullins case implies a binary choice (BRX vs. LDP). In practice, the harder lesson is *how to negotiate a third option into being* when the alternatives presented are deficient. What is the playbook?
- Kramer's "prudent paranoia" is a useful frame but operationally underspecified. What are the signals that justify increasing vigilance, and what are the markers that distinguish prudent from corrosive paranoia?
