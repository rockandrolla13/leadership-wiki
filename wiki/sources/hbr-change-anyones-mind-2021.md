# HBR Mar-Apr 2021 — How to Change Anyone's Mind

## Core Argument

You can change the mind of even an overconfident, stubborn, narcissistic, or disagreeable counterpart — but not by arguing harder. Adam Grant's central claim is that personality is not consistent across situations: every difficult person has an **if…then profile** that reveals the conditions under which they become persuadable. The leader's job is not to overpower, but to engineer the situation so that the listener arrives at the new view themselves. The issue extends this thesis: senior leaders also need to *be persuadable*, which requires deliberate listening systems (Bryant & Sharer) and the humility to plan their own succession well in advance (Haythornthwaite & Banga).

## Key Leadership Insights

- **Personality is situational, not fixed.** Even the most rigid person has if…then moments where they flex. Identifying the conditional that opens them is the persuasion move, not pushing harder on the conditional that closes them.
- **Four counter-strategies, one per profile (Grant):**
  - For a **know-it-all**: ask them to *explain how things work*. Trying to articulate a complex causal chain exposes the gaps in their own understanding more reliably than any external argument. The Yale "how does a toilet flush?" experiment generalizes.
  - For a **stubborn** person: hand them the chisel. Don't pitch fully-formed conclusions; ask "what if?" and "could we?" and let them seize the reins. The successful Hollywood screenwriter and Apple's Mike Bell both succeeded by planting the seed and letting Jobs claim it.
  - For a **narcissist**: praise a strength *unrelated* to the area where you want them to change. Reaffirming one identity makes another flaw safer to acknowledge. The feedback sandwich does not work — narcissists ignore the middle.
  - For a **disagreeable** person: stand up to them rather than back down. They are energized by conflict; capitulating signals you are not worth engaging. The Mac team's annual "award for challenging Steve Jobs" turned this dynamic into a feature.
- **Persuasion is a multi-week endeavor, not a single meeting.** Fadell on the iPhone: "had to work as a group, not simply in one meeting but possibly over weeks, to get him to change his mind." Build prototypes, refine, repeat.
- **Senior leaders are systematically under-listening.** Bryant & Sharer: CEOs sit at the top of every information channel, but the information that reaches them is the most compromised. Counterintuitively, the more senior you are, the more *system design* you need around listening. WAIT — "Why Am I Talking?" — is the daily diagnostic.
- **A listening ecosystem is built, not stumbled into.** Sharer's Amgen practice: quarterly competitor-news reports; structured FDA-relations questions; annual all-employee survey including "what do you think of the job that Kevin is doing?"; HR-mediated leadership feedback that the board sees before the CEO does.
- **The CEO's real legacy is their successor.** Freeman's argument is that even in good times, succession planning is the CEO's job, not the board's. Picking a successor whose strengths *differ* from your own is the point; the company's needs will have changed by the time they take over.
- **Offboarding is recruitment-in-reverse.** Departing employees become customers, suppliers, boomerang hires, mentors, brand ambassadors. McKinsey enrolls people in the alumni network on day one of employment, not day one of departure.

## Practical Applications

- Before walking into a contentious conversation with a senior counterpart, **diagnose their type** (know-it-all / stubborn / narcissist / disagreeable) and select the matching opening move. Do *not* default to "make the rational case more clearly" — that is the failure mode for all four types.
- For a know-it-all on your team: in the next meeting, ask them to explain *how* their preferred solution will actually work, step by step, in front of others. The articulation pressure usually does the work.
- For a stubborn leader: stop pitching finished proposals. Re-frame as questions. "What if we tried…?" "Could we explore…?" Plant; let them grow.
- For a narcissistic boss: catalog the strengths they are confident in. When you need to push back on a decision, lead by reaffirming the *unrelated* strength. Then deliver the disagreement.
- For a disagreeable peer or boss: rehearse the conversation where you stand your ground. Backing down accelerates the loss of respect.
- Build a personal **listening ecosystem**: identify the 6–10 signals you should be hearing weekly/quarterly/annually, and design specific mechanisms (rides with sales reps, regulator updates, anonymous employee feedback) to surface them. Then close the loop by writing back what you heard.
- Start succession planning **early in your tenure**, not late. Use the Sunday-afternoon mentoring model (Freeman & Mohapatra: weekly hour-long calls for five years).
- Build a formal alumni program. Track who leaves, what they go on to do, and keep them connected. Treat the offboarding moment with the same care as onboarding.

## Relevant Quotes

> "Plenty of leaders are so sure of themselves that they reject worthy opinions and ideas from others and refuse to abandon their own bad ones. The good news is that it is possible to get even the most overconfident, stubborn, narcissistic, and disagreeable people to open their minds." — Adam Grant, *Persuading the Unpersuadable*

> "Every leader has an *if…then* profile: a pattern of responding to particular scenarios in certain ways. If the dominant manager is interacting with a superior…then she becomes submissive. If the competitive colleague is dealing with an important client…then he shifts into cooperative mode." — Grant

> "A second obstacle to changing people's opinions is stubbornness. Intractable people see consistency and certainty as virtues. Once made up, their minds seem to be set in stone. But their views become more pliable if you hand them a chisel." — Grant

> "Because disagreeable people are energized by conflict, they don't always want you to bend to their will right away; they're eager to duke it out." — Grant

> "The key is to praise people in an area different from the one in which you hope to change their minds…We all have multiple identities, and when we feel secure about one of our strengths, we become more open to accepting our shortcomings elsewhere." — Grant on praising narcissists

> "Asking questions instead of giving answers can overcome people's defensiveness. You're not telling your boss what to think or do; you're giving her some control over the conversation and inviting her to share her thoughts." — Grant

> "When leaders lack the wisdom to question their convictions, followers need the courage to persuade them to change their minds." — Grant, closing sentence

> "Creating listening systems is not just about passively accepting what comes your way. You have to create structures so that people know you want to hear what they have to say." — Kevin Sharer, *Are You Really Listening?*

> "If you just walk around and see a bunch of smiling faces and say, 'Gee, everybody looks happy to me,' you're not listening." — Sharer

> "Your true legacy as a CEO is what happens to the company *after* you leave the corner office." — Kenneth W. Freeman

> "In the hiring process, we all tend to choose the people who most resemble us. But instead of looking in the mirror for the essential characteristics of a successor, we should look out the window toward the real future of the company and let that guide our choice." — Freeman, on choosing a different-shaped successor

## Frameworks Extracted

- **If…Then Persuasion Map (Grant).** Diagnose the difficult counterpart along four axes (overconfidence / stubbornness / narcissism / disagreeableness) and select the matching opening move: explain-how-it-works / hand-them-the-chisel / praise-an-unrelated-strength / stand-your-ground.
- **Listening Ecosystem (Sharer).** A leader's deliberate set of channels through which honest signal reaches them: structured external relationships (regulators, customers, suppliers), structured internal channels (rides with frontline staff, regular skip-levels, FDA/compliance liaison), structured upward feedback (anonymous employee surveys, HR-mediated leadership reviews), and a public **closure loop** (writing back what you heard, with a plan).
- **WAIT diagnostic.** Joel Peterson's one-syllable reminder: "Why Am I Talking?" Use it whenever the urge to speak arrives in a meeting where someone else is supposed to be the source of information.
- **CEO-led succession (Freeman).** Five principles:
  1. *Force the board to pay attention* — succession is the CEO's job, even when directors don't want to discuss it.
  2. *Look for differences* — the right successor is the one whose strengths match where the *company* is going, not where it has been.
  3. *Make your successor's success your own* — the Sunday-afternoon model, sustained for years before transition.
  4. *Don't drag your feet* — leave at the top of your game.
  5. *Avoid the return appearance* — coming back as CEO means both you and your successor failed.
- **Loyal-alumni offboarding (Dachner & Makarius).** Five steps: prepare for exits well in advance; recognize people's contributions when they leave; conduct thoughtful exit interviews; provide transition support tailored to individual needs; create formal alumni programs that keep people connected.

## Contradictions / Tensions With Existing Wiki

- **Grant's "if…then" view of personality tensions with [[principles/authentic-leadership]] / Sinek's WHY-as-stable-core.** Both can be true (purpose stable; behavior situational), but the wiki should acknowledge the distinction.
- **The "praise a narcissist" move has a manipulation flavor.** It is technically true and operationally useful, but it sits uneasily next to [[principles/trust]] and the wiki's general posture that leadership manipulation is corrosive. Note: Grant frames the move as *reducing defensiveness*, not creating false belief — the strength being praised must be real.
- **The feedback-sandwich critique conflicts with some popular HR guidance.** Grant: "the feedback sandwich doesn't taste as good as it looks." This aligns with [[communication/feedback-scripts]] and SBI, but should be made explicit.
- **The "stand up to a disagreeable boss" prescription tensions with cultural-context warnings.** [[communication/cross-cultural-negotiation]] (Meyer's five rules) flags that disagreement-norms vary widely. Grant's prescription is American/Anglo by default; in some cultures, public pushback is permanently damaging.
- **Freeman's "the board won't drive succession" claim tensions with modern governance literature** and the [[organization/c-suite-as-team]] page. In 2026 boards are more involved in succession; Freeman's frame is closer to the 2004 reality. Still useful as the *starting* posture for the CEO.

## Questions This Source Raises

- How do you diagnose a counterpart's profile without working with them long enough to observe it? Are there fast tells?
- Grant treats the four traits as discrete; in practice they co-occur. What is the right move when a counterpart is *both* narcissistic and disagreeable (e.g., the canonical "difficult founder" archetype)?
- The "stand up to a disagreeable boss" prescription requires that the boss have *some* respect for the speaker. What is the move when that respect is absent and the boss is purely punitive?
- Sharer's listening ecosystem requires meaningful organizational scale. What does the analog look like for a leader of a team of 15?
- Freeman's argument that the CEO should leave at the top requires that there is somewhere to go. In a typical career, what is the analog for a director or VP — when should they step out of a role they are succeeding in?
- The if…then map is descriptive. Is there a developmental version — can a leader train themselves *out* of their own brittle if…then patterns?
