# 7 Lessons for Leading in Crisis

## Metadata

- **Author:** Bill George (Harvard Business School; former CEO Medtronic 1991–2001)
- **Date:** 2009 (Jossey-Bass, Warren Bennis Signature Series)
- **Type:** book
- **Path:** raw/books/markdown/7 Lessons for Leading in Crisis (Bill George) (z-library.sk, 1lib.sk, z-lib.sk).md
- **Ingested:** 2026-05-17

## Core Argument

Written at the depth of the 2008–2009 financial meltdown, the book argues that crisis is the *defining moment* of a leader's career. Crises do not reveal a leader's quality; they *forge* it. Drawing on cases from Medtronic, Goldman Sachs, J&J, Xerox, Target, JetBlue, Mattel, and the 2008 collapse cohort (Lehman, AIG, Bear Stearns, Citigroup), George distills seven sequential lessons: face reality (starting with yourself), don't be Atlas, dig deep for the root cause, get ready for the long haul, never waste a good crisis, follow True North under the spotlight, and go on offense focused on winning. The unifying thread: leaders aligned with their inner compass (True North) survive crises by accepting personal responsibility, refusing to declare premature victory, and using the disruption to transform the enterprise.

## Key Leadership Insights

- **Failed leadership, not failed regulation, caused the meltdown.** "The root cause of the problem was failed leadership from leaders who didn't follow their True North." Subprime loans, CDS, and excessive leverage were symptoms — the real failure was leaders unwilling to face reality and accept responsibility.
- **Crisis tests True North, it does not provide one.** Staying on course is easy when business is growing; the test comes when destruction looms. Crisis is the *crucible*, not the *curriculum.*
- **Defining reality is the leader's first job (citing Max DePree).** "Before you can lead your organization through a crisis, you have to acknowledge that you are indeed in one. Next, you have to get everyone else to acknowledge it as well."
- **Denial destroys more careers than incompetence.** Schwartz at Bear Stearns and Fuld at Lehman both said, in retrospect, that they could not have done anything differently. George: "Which of us who has been responsible for less significant failures couldn't think of many things we would have done differently?"
- **The leader's role in the crisis precedes the team's.** Kevin Sharer at Amgen could not get his executives to act on Aranesp safety until he publicly acknowledged his own role in the crisis: "For a deeply reflective hour, I asked myself what I owned of this problem. I came up with a long list."
- **Don't shoot the messenger, or no message will arrive.** Jamie Dimon: "If you have ten people around you and only one of them is telling you the truth, you have a real problem, because everyone has to do it."
- **You cannot get through a crisis alone.** Leaders who turn inward (Purcell at Morgan Stanley, Fiorina at HP) lose the firm. Leaders who reach out to their team (Steinhafel at Target, Mulcahy at Xerox) survive.
- **Vulnerability is power.** Tad Piper at Piper Jaffray shared his chemical-dependency story with his branch leaders during a near-bankruptcy crisis: "I decided to be completely honest and totally vulnerable… All of a sudden everybody was on our team, even the skeptics." See [[principles/trust]].
- **Things will get worse before they get better.** Goldman Sachs marked subprime to market daily and got out of the business in early 2007; Lehman, AIG, Countrywide kept dancing. Survivability is a function of the willingness to confront the iceberg below the tip.
- **Crises have long roots.** Anne Mulcahy: "A lot of crises seem to happen overnight, but they have really long roots, like ten to fifteen years in terms of the source of the real problems." Citigroup's 2008 collapse began in Sandy Weill's 1990s strategy. The leader at the moment of crisis often inherited it.
- **In a crisis, cash is king.** Not earnings per share, not revenue growth, not ROE — survivability. Goldman built liquidity to $100B before September 2008; Lehman minimized cash reserves.
- **Never waste a good crisis (Machiavelli, via Wendy Kopp).** Crisis lowers the resistance to change that exists in good times. Use it to shed weaknesses, restructure, reset expectations.
  - Teach For America used its 1995 near-collapse to refocus on its core mission, cut payroll 40%, and emerge as a national education-reform engine.
  - GE's Welch used the early-1980s globalization threat to *create* a crisis ("be #1 or #2 or be divested") and dismantle GE's bureaucracy.
  - Medtronic used the 1998 growth slowdown to acquire seven companies in high-growth segments, transforming from a pacemaker-defibrillator company into the world's leading medical technology company.
- **In a crisis, you are in the spotlight whether you want to be or not.** Giuliani's visible presence in NYC after 9/11 contrasted with national leaders who retreated to bunkers. The leader's *physical visibility and emotional access* during crisis is the message.
- **Be transparent.** Internal and external communications have merged. "Whatever is said inside the company is quickly transmitted to outsiders, and whatever is written or said outside is also read or heard inside." Hunkering down with vague statements creates a vacuum that rumor and adversaries will fill.
- **Acknowledge what you don't know.** "Acknowledging that you don't know is far healthier than pretending you are certain." Bennis: a leader's willingness to publicly say "I don't know" is a sign of strength, not weakness.
- **Accept responsibility, do not shift blame.** Mattel's Robert Eckert initially blamed Chinese contract manufacturers for lead-paint toy recalls; this triggered xenophobic backlash and damaged Mattel's brand more than direct acceptance would have. JetBlue's David Neeleman accepted responsibility for the Valentine's Day 2007 stranding, posted apology videos on YouTube, and announced a customer Bill of Rights — but his board still removed him.
- **Survivability is half the battle; reshaping the market is the other half.** Coming out of a crisis, markets never look the same as they did going in. Use the crisis to reshape the industry to play to your strengths and expose competitors' weaknesses.

## Practical Applications

- **Build your support team *before* the crisis hits.** "By the time you are facing a crisis, it is too late to form your support team. The time to do so is when things are going smoothly." George maintains: a primary intimate partner; a weekly men's group (28+ years); a monthly couples' group of eight (since 1983); a small set of mentors. Form these in calm times.
- **Build resilience as a daily practice, not an emergency response.** George's three pillars: keep your body in shape (regular workouts 3–4×/week); keep your mind sharp and spirits high (twice-daily meditation, prayer, reflection); don't take yourself too seriously (laughter, friends, dancing, deep conversations).
- **Use the "trust but verify" discipline.** Terry Lundgren at Macy's: "I just pop into stores, call store managers on their cell phones, and tell them to come down. Then we walk through the floor. They have had no time to prepare the store. I learn as much as anything I do." George logged ~1000 implant procedures during 12 years at Medtronic.
- **Build a culture of candor before you need it.** George at Medtronic: "You'll never get fired for having a problem, but you will get fired for covering one up. Integrity is not the absence of lying. Rather, it is telling the whole truth, so that we can gather together the best people in the company to solve the problem."
- **Create internal whistle-blower channels.** Medtronic's anonymous hotline surfaced fraud on the FDA by a newly acquired subsidiary; Medtronic self-reported and pulled the products. Internal channels make external whistle-blowing unnecessary.
- **Run the "if we got kicked out and the board brought in a new CEO" thought experiment.** Andy Grove asked Gordon Moore this question to break Intel out of memory chips in 1984; Andrea Jung at Avon did the same in 2005. The exercise strips the emotional encumbrance from the decision.
- **Execute Lesson 7's 7 Steps for offense:** (1) rethink your industry strategy; (2) shed weaknesses; (3) reshape the industry to play to your strengths; (4) invest during the downturn; (5) keep key people focused on winning (carve out a small future-strategy team while the rest fights the fire); (6) create your company's image as the industry leader; (7) develop rigorous execution plans.
- **Hold the line on cash and on R&D simultaneously.** Medtronic in 1993 cut overhead, perks, and the executive dining room while *increasing* R&D from 9% to 11% of sales and adding hundreds of physician-support specialists. The cuts were about reinforcing egalitarian culture; the investments were about market position.

## Frameworks Extracted

- **The 7 Lessons:** (1) Face reality, starting with yourself; (2) Don't be Atlas — get the world off your shoulders; (3) Dig deep for the root cause; (4) Get ready for the long haul; (5) Never waste a good crisis; (6) You're in the spotlight: follow True North; (7) Go on offense, focus on winning now.
- **The Dueling Narratives (with Nitin Nohria):** every leader carries two internal narratives — positive (capable, doing worthwhile work) and negative (acknowledging shortcomings will destroy self-esteem). Crisis amplifies the negative narrative and drives leaders inward. The remedy is naming the narratives and reaching out.
- **The Three Resilience Pillars:** body in shape, mind sharp and spirits high, don't take yourself too seriously. Practiced daily, not in emergency.
- **Concentric Support Team Model:** Layer 1 — one intimate partner (spouse, best friend, mentor) with whom you are completely open. Layer 2 — a small standing peer group (men's group, couples' group). Layer 3 — mentors and advisors. Form before crisis.
- **7 Steps to Focus on Winning (Lesson 7):** rethink industry strategy → shed weaknesses → reshape industry → invest during downturn → keep key people on the future → create industry-leader image → develop rigorous execution plans.
- **Trust-but-Verify Discipline:** combine reports up the hierarchy with direct, unannounced contact with the front line (Macy's store-pop-in, Medtronic operating-room visits).
- **The Iceberg Disclosure Discipline (Lesson 3):** when you disclose a problem, frame it as "tip of the iceberg, investigating further" rather than as the total problem. George's $25M Honeywell disclosure turned out to be the first chip of a $450M problem; framing it as the full problem then destroyed credibility when the rest emerged.
- **Vulnerability-as-Connection (Tad Piper / John Hope Bryant):** strategic disclosure of one's own struggle, used sparingly and in service of inviting commitment, builds support more powerfully than projecting invulnerability.

## Contradictions / Tensions With Existing Wiki

- **Reinforces and operationalizes [[principles/authentic-leadership]]:** the 2003 book defined True North; the 2009 book applies it under fire. Same author, same framework, with crisis case material.
- **Sharpens [[principles/courage]]:** the wiki's "competent courage" page describes courage as right action despite risk. George adds the *structural* substrate: courage requires the support team and resilience practices built in calm times. Courage is not just disposition; it is preparation.
- **Tensions with the wiki's [[principles/decision-making]] emphasis on RAPID/RACI and structured decision rights.** In Lesson 3, George *abandoned consensus* in Medtronic's 1998 growth crisis: "Uncharacteristically, I decided to abandon achieving a consensus. Instead I took responsibility to pursue the acquisition route." Structured decision rights presume sufficient time; crisis often does not afford it. The synthesis is in [[organization/decision-rights]]: closure discipline (Charan) means the CEO can and should override consensus when the cost of delay exceeds the cost of imperfect alignment.
- **Reinforces [[communication/stakeholder-updates]]:** the merge of internal and external communication, and the cost of "hunker-down" communication strategies.
- **Tensions with the Welch/GE "be #1 or #2" stance for current AI-era organizations.** Welch's market-position framing assumes stable industries; the [[organization/adaptive-organization]] page's hypothesize–experiment–reflect–spread loop is closer to the operating model for 2026. George's principle — *use crisis to transform* — is still valid; the *target* of transformation is now adaptive capacity, not just cost position.
- **Extends [[coaching/resilience]]:** adds the three-pillar daily-practice model (body, mind, lightness) and the pre-crisis support-team formation discipline. The microstress page identified the load; this source provides the recovery architecture.

## Questions This Source Raises

- George wrote in 2009 from the position of a successful retired CEO who navigated several internal crises. The 2008 financial crisis is treated as confirming his thesis. How does the framework hold up against crises George did not face directly — e.g., a pandemic-induced crisis (COVID), a cyber-incident crisis, a regulatory-led crisis, or a crisis that begins as a reputational scandal rather than an operational one?
- The "never waste a good crisis" maxim assumes leadership will hold through the crisis. Many of George's positive cases (Mulcahy, Welch, Gerstner) were *successor* CEOs who inherited the problem. How does the playbook differ for the predecessor CEO who must dismantle their own work to enable their successor's transformation? (Suggests link to [[management/managing-senior-people]] succession material.)
- The book treats vulnerability (Tad Piper) and decisiveness without consensus (George 1998) as both authentic. What is the principle distinguishing when each is appropriate? Provisional answer: vulnerability builds the *coalition*; decisive action without consensus moves the *strategy* once the coalition exists. But this needs sharper operationalization.
- The trust-but-verify discipline scales linearly with the leader's calendar. In an AI-era organization (see [[management/leading-technical-teams]]), how is this discipline executed when the data flow is mediated by dashboards, agents, and AI summaries? When the leader walks the factory floor, what is the equivalent of the operating room in an AI-augmented enterprise?
