# The Art of Leadership: Small Things, Done Well

## Core Argument

Leadership cannot be hacked — it must be built through repeatable practices, done consistently over time, until the judgment to deploy them situationally becomes instinct. The book presents thirty "small things" organized across three leadership levels (manager, director, executive) drawn from Lopp's career at Netscape, Apple, and Slack. The central thesis: leadership is a set of habits whose value is realized through compounding repetition, not through understanding them intellectually.

## Key Leadership Insights

- **1:1s are the highest-signal meeting of the week.** Not status updates — real conversations that build trust and surface what doesn't get reported anywhere else. Schedule them first; protect them hardest.
- **The New Manager Death Spiral** is the result of the IC instinct applied to management: doing the work yourself, half-delegating, withholding context, losing trust, and blaming the team. The antidote is trust through listening, delegation, and diversity.
- **Delegation is the most important leadership merit badge.** Entrust work, not just tasks. A B on a delegated project is better leadership than an A solo because it builds the team's capacity and trust.
- **Performance management** should be rare. The default is coaching through 1:1s over months. The key question: have you had multiple face-to-face conversations, clearly named the gap, and agreed on specific actions? If not, you are not yet in performance management — you are in the early stages of coaching.
- **Hiring deserves 50% of a manager's time** when reqs are open. The Must List (every person you'd want to work with again) is a living document for proactive sourcing. The three pipeline stages — Discover, Understand, Delight — center the candidate's evolving mindset, not just the funnel metrics.
- **Read the room before acting.** Act last in meetings — let others speak first. More information produces better decisions. The leader's opinion disproportionately closes conversation; delay it.
- **Spidey-sense is real.** Pattern recognition from experience produces legitimate intuitions. When something feels off, track it. The Spidey-sense spreadsheet (listing team members who seem at risk, even without clear explanation) surfaces early warning signals that prevent surprise attrition.
- **The Blue Tape List.** In any new context, everything broken is visible. List it. Wait a month. Much will resolve; the rest demands address.
- **Culture Creek:** culture flows downstream. Stories within an organization reveal what is actually valued. If you want to know the culture, listen to the stories people tell.
- **Protect unstructured time.** Anti-flow time — disconnected time that is not structured — is where the most important thinking happens for executives. It cannot be found; it must be protected.

## Practical Applications

- Start every new role by scheduling 1:1s with all direct reports. Never cancel them.
- Keep a Must List of every former colleague you'd want to work with again. Review it monthly. Contact anyone at 90-day intervals.
- When a team member's behavior seems off, add them to a Spidey-sense tracker. Develop a shared language for early warning signals.
- Before entering formal performance management, ask: "Have I had multiple face-to-face conversations over multiple months, clearly named the gap, and agreed on specific measurable actions?" If no, keep coaching.
- When delegating, provide the goal, what winning looks like, resources, timeline, and explicit openness to questions. Then get out of the way.
- Create a Blue Tape List in any new role. Review it at 30 days. Address every item — fix it, explain why you won't, or explain when you'll revisit.

## Frameworks Extracted

- **The New Manager Death Spiral:** A cascading failure sequence driven by the IC instinct applied to management. Antidotes: listen, diversify, delegate.
- **Discover / Understand / Delight:** Three phases of the engineering recruiting pipeline, centered on the candidate's evolving mindset.
- **The Must List:** A maintained spreadsheet of every former colleague you'd want to work with again. Used for proactive sourcing and network maintenance.
- **The Blue Tape List:** A log of everything that seems broken in a new context. Used to defer action until context is sufficient and then address every item.
- **Spidey-Sense Spreadsheet:** A team-maintained tracker of individuals who seem at risk (burnout, boredom, misalignment), even without clear explanation. Used to surface early attrition warning signals.
- **The Checklist Sentence (Performance Management):** Before entering formal performance management, confirm: multiple face-to-face conversations, over multiple months, where gap was clearly explained and agreed, and specific measurable actions were agreed upon.

## Questions This Source Raises

- At what point does coaching genuinely become performance management? Lopp draws the line clearly, but in practice the transition is rarely clean.
- How does the Must List translate to non-engineering contexts? Does the same proactive sourcing model apply in sales, design, or operations?
- Lopp argues most performance situations are coachable. What percentage of situations where leaders invoke formal PIP would have resolved through better earlier coaching?
